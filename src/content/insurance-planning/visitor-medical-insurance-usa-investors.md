International investors sometimes need to physically travel to the US - opening a brokerage account in person at some institutions, meeting a financial advisor or estate attorney face to face, or handling a property or business interest directly. That trip raises a genuinely separate question from anything else on this site: what happens if something medical goes wrong while you're there, and what does that have to do with the tax residency questions this site covers elsewhere.

---

## It's Not Legally Required - That's Also the Risk

**Most visitors entering the US on a B1/B2 tourist or business visa, or under the Visa Waiver Program, face no legal requirement to carry travel medical insurance.** Unlike some countries that mandate proof of coverage for entry, the US doesn't require it for these visitor categories. That's exactly why it's worth buying anyway rather than treating the absence of a legal mandate as a signal it's unnecessary: a single emergency room visit in the US can run into the thousands of dollars, and a hospital stay can reach six figures, with no reciprocal healthcare agreement most foreign visitors can rely on to offset it.

---

## What It Actually Costs

**Basic coverage runs well under $1 per day for younger travelers**, with plans offering coverage limits up to $8 million available at that price point. More concretely: a traveler in their thirties visiting from Europe might pay roughly $40 a month for a basic fixed-benefit plan, or around $60 a month for more comprehensive coverage. Cost rises meaningfully with age - a 60-year-old traveler might pay somewhere between $122 and $1,050 for six months of coverage, depending on the specific plan, coverage limits, and how pre-existing conditions are handled. **Pre-existing condition coverage is the detail most worth reading carefully before buying** - many visitor plans exclude or sharply limit coverage for conditions the traveler already had before the trip, which matters more for an older investor with an established health history than for a younger one.

**Commonly available providers in this specific market** - travel medical insurers focused specifically on visitors to the US rather than general international travel insurance - include names like Atlas America, Patriot America Plus, Safe Travels USA, and IMG's visitor-specific plans, generally offering a range of coverage tiers and durations rather than a single standardized product. Comparing specific plan terms directly (coverage limits, deductibles, pre-existing condition handling, and whether the plan is "fixed benefit" versus "comprehensive") matters more than picking a name recognized from general searches.

## The Connection to Your Actual Tax Situation

**Here's the detail specific to this site's readers that a generic travel-insurance guide won't mention: time spent in the US, for any purpose including a financial or investment-related trip, counts toward the substantial presence test that determines US tax residency.** The 183-day threshold covered in our [options trading tax treatment guide](/taxes/options-trading-tax-treatment-non-us-residents/) and elsewhere on this site isn't specific to any one activity - a trip to open a brokerage account, meet an advisor, or handle a property matter adds to the same day-count as any other visit, using a formula that weights the current year's days fully and prior years' days at a fraction.

**The actual formula is worth knowing precisely, not just as a vague "183 days" rule.** The substantial presence test adds: every day of US presence in the current calendar year, counted in full, plus one-third of the days present in the immediately preceding year, plus one-sixth of the days present in the year before that - and you're also required to have been present at least 31 days in the current year for the test to apply at all. A worked example makes the weighting concrete: 84 days this year, 168 days last year, and 261 days the year before works out to 84 (full) plus 56 (one-third of 168) plus 43.5 (one-sixth of 261), totaling 183.5 - enough to cross the threshold even though no single year's count looks alarming on its own.

**For most investors making an occasional, short trip for a specific financial purpose, this isn't a practical concern** - a handful of days across a year sits nowhere near the substantial presence threshold. It becomes relevant specifically for an investor making frequent or extended US visits for business, investment management, or property oversight reasons, where the cumulative day count across a rolling period could plausibly approach the threshold - a scenario worth tracking deliberately rather than discovering after the fact that enough trips accumulated to change your US tax residency status.

## What Visitor Insurance Doesn't Do

**Visitor medical insurance is not a substitute for the broader life, disability, or long-term coverage questions covered elsewhere in this category** - it's short-term, trip-specific coverage for an acute medical event during a visit, not a tool for estate planning, income replacement, or any of the longer-horizon insurance needs covered in our guides on [life insurance for cross-border investors](/insurance-planning/how-much-life-insurance-do-you-need-cross-border-investors/) or [disability coverage](/insurance-planning/disability-insurance-considerations-international-investors/). Someone planning a longer US stay - beyond a short trip - should also look at the more comprehensive [expat health insurance](/insurance-planning/) options covering extended or ongoing time in the country, a genuinely different product from short-term visitor coverage.

---

## Bottom Line

Visitor medical insurance for a US trip isn't legally required for most tourist and business visitors, which is precisely why it's easy to skip and precisely why skipping it is a real financial risk given how expensive US emergency care can be without coverage. For an international investor whose US trips are specifically tied to account-opening, advisor meetings, or managing US-based assets, the added wrinkle worth tracking is that those same travel days count toward the substantial presence test - a separate, tax-residency question from the insurance decision itself, but one worth having on the radar for anyone whose US visits start adding up.
