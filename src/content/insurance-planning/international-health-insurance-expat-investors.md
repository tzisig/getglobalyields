International health insurance - ongoing coverage for someone actually living outside their home country, not the short-term trip coverage covered in our [visitor insurance guide](/insurance-planning/visitor-medical-insurance-usa-investors/) - is a genuinely different product built around a different problem: staying insured across multiple countries over years, not a single trip. For an investor holding real US assets or ties, one specific detail in how these plans are structured deserves more attention than it usually gets.

---

## "Worldwide" Often Has an Asterisk

**A large share of international health insurance plans marketed as worldwide coverage actually offer a choice: worldwide including the US, or worldwide excluding it** - and excluding the US, along with a short list of other expensive-to-treat-in countries like Canada, Japan, and Singapore, meaningfully lowers the premium. This isn't a hidden trick so much as a standard, disclosed plan option most insurers offer deliberately, since US healthcare costs are high enough to move the price of an entire global plan.

**The cost gap is real and substantial.** Plans that include US treatment can run $28,000 or more per year, against overall international plan pricing that averages around $6,900 annually and can start under $500 for limited coverage. An investor comparing plans purely on the advertised premium, without checking whether "worldwide" specifically includes or excludes the US, can end up with coverage that looks comprehensive right up until the moment they actually need care during a US trip.

---

## Why This Matters Specifically for This Site's Readers

**An investor with meaningful US brokerage assets, US real estate, or a business with American ties has a genuinely elevated reason to travel to the US relative to someone with no such connections** - covered throughout this site's other content on US market access, brokers, and cross-border planning. If that travel ever coincides with a medical emergency, a "worldwide excluding US" plan provides zero coverage for exactly the trip most likely to actually happen, defeating the purpose of carrying international coverage in the first place. Confirming this specific detail before buying - not assuming "worldwide" means everywhere - is a small check against a real, specific gap that intersects directly with why this site's readers travel to the US at all.

---

## What Real Plans Actually Cost

**Cigna Global**, one of the larger providers in this space, prices plans from around $150 a month for basic coverage up to an average closer to $460 a month (roughly $5,520 a year) for the coverage level most clients actually select, with annual benefit limits ranging from $1 million on its Silver tier up to unlimited coverage on its top Platinum tier. The company's network spans roughly 2.4 million providers across more than 200 countries. **Allianz Care**, a Munich-headquartered competitor, typically runs $350 to $600 a month for a 40-year-old, with benefit limits reaching beyond €2 million depending on the specific policy - both real providers, among several serious options in a market with meaningful price variation by age, coverage level, and specifically whether US coverage is included.

---

## What You Actually Get Beyond Just "Coverage"

**International health insurance plans typically bundle features a domestic-only plan doesn't offer by default**: emergency medical evacuation and repatriation (getting you to appropriate care, or home, from wherever you happen to be when something happens), access to English-speaking providers regardless of country, and the ability to choose your own doctor or hospital rather than working within a single country's provider network. For an investor genuinely living a multi-country life - splitting time across jurisdictions, managing assets and travel across borders - these features address a real gap that a standard domestic health plan, built around one country's system, structurally can't cover.

---

## Where This Coverage Becomes Mandatory, Not Optional

**Unlike the US visitor insurance covered separately on this site - not legally required for most tourist visits - a growing number of countries make international health insurance a hard visa requirement, particularly for digital nomad and remote-work visa programs.** Spain requires a policy matching the coverage standards of its own public health system, with no copayments or deductibles, running the full duration of the visa. Portugal requires a minimum of €30,000 in medical coverage, explicitly accepting international providers like Cigna as long as the coverage threshold is met. Indonesia's B211A visa requires a minimum of $25,000 in coverage that specifically covers Indonesia and matches the visa dates exactly, while the UAE requires UAE-valid coverage plus a separate medical fitness test. For an internationally mobile investor considering any residency-by-remote-work visa route as part of a broader cross-border life, checking that specific country's insurance minimums before applying isn't optional paperwork - it's frequently a hard gate on the visa itself, not a recommendation.

---

## How This Differs From Visitor Insurance

**The distinction from short-term visitor coverage is worth being explicit about, since the two products solve different problems and sometimes get conflated.** Visitor insurance, covered separately on this site, is built for a defined trip of days or weeks, priced and structured for that scope. International health insurance is annual, ongoing coverage for someone whose life genuinely spans multiple countries - a different pricing model, different underwriting, and generally a more expensive, more comprehensive product because it's covering an entire year of unpredictable healthcare needs across potentially several jurisdictions, not a single trip's worth of risk.

---

## International Health Insurance for Expats Explained, in Short

International health insurance solves a real problem for anyone genuinely living across multiple countries, but the "worldwide" label on a plan is not a guarantee the US is actually included - confirm that specific detail before assuming coverage extends to exactly the country where a meaningful share of this site's readers hold real financial ties. The cost gap between "worldwide" and "worldwide including US" is large enough, and the coincidence of "has real US assets" with "might need to travel to the US" common enough among this site's audience, that it's worth checking directly rather than assuming.
