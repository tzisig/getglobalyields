The life insurance coverage discussed elsewhere in this category - sized specifically to offset a projected US estate tax liability - solves a narrow, specific problem. Most investors with dependents also have a broader, more foundational question the estate-tax use case doesn't address at all: if I died tomorrow, how much would my family actually need to maintain their life without my income? Cross-border finances - income earned in one currency, assets held in another country, dependents potentially living in a third - add real complexity to a needs calculation that's already not straightforward even in a single-country situation.

---

## The Basic Needs-Analysis Framework

**A standard life insurance needs analysis works backward from what dependents would actually need, then subtracts what's already available, to arrive at the coverage gap a policy should fill.** The core components: **income replacement** (how many years of the insured's income would need replacing, and at what amount), **debt payoff** (mortgage balance, other significant debts that shouldn't fall to surviving dependents), **future major expenses** (education costs for children, for instance), **minus existing resources** (current savings, existing investment portfolio value, any existing coverage already in place).

**This is a subtraction exercise, not just an addition exercise** - a common mistake is calculating total future need without properly crediting existing assets against it, resulting in an inflated coverage recommendation that duplicates protection the investor's own portfolio already provides. An investor who has already built substantial brokerage holdings, exactly the kind of investing covered throughout this site, needs meaningfully less additional life insurance coverage than someone starting from zero net worth, because the existing portfolio is already doing part of the job additional insurance coverage would otherwise need to do.

---

## A Worked Example, Adjusted for Cross-Border Factors

**Consider an investor earning the equivalent of $70,000 annually, supporting a spouse and two young children, with a $180,000 brokerage portfolio already built and a $150,000 mortgage remaining on a home in the investor's home country.** A standard income-replacement approach might target replacing 10 years of income for the surviving spouse ($700,000), add the mortgage payoff ($150,000) and an estimate for the children's future education costs ($100,000 combined), for a gross need of $950,000 - then subtract the existing $180,000 portfolio, arriving at a net coverage gap of roughly $770,000.

**The cross-border wrinkles that adjust this standard calculation:** if the investor's income is earned in a currency different from where the family would continue living, the income-replacement figure should be calculated in the currency the family actually spends in, not the currency the investor earns in - a distinction covered in more depth in our [currency risk vs market risk guide](/currency-banking/currency-risk-vs-market-risk-international-investors/), applied here to a insurance-sizing decision rather than a portfolio-performance one. If the $180,000 brokerage portfolio is itself subject to potential US estate tax exposure above the $60,000 non-resident alien exemption, covered in our [$60,000 exemption guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/), the amount actually available to the family net of any estate tax liability is somewhat lower than the portfolio's full gross value - a nuance a purely domestic needs analysis wouldn't need to account for.

## Why Existing Portfolio Value Should Reduce, Not Ignore, the Coverage Target

**An investor actively building wealth through the kind of disciplined, long-term investing covered throughout this site is, in a real sense, self-insuring over time** - each year of continued contributions and market growth reduces the theoretical life insurance gap that would otherwise need filling by a policy. **This argues for either declining coverage over time (a decreasing term structure, where the death benefit steps down on a schedule matched to expected portfolio growth) or periodically revisiting and reducing coverage amounts as the portfolio grows**, rather than locking in a large, fixed coverage amount calculated once early on and never revisited as circumstances change.

**This is also why the estate-tax-liquidity policy covered in our [life insurance vs brokerage assets guide](/estate-planning/life-insurance-vs-brokerage-assets-estate-planning/) and a broader income-replacement policy discussed here are genuinely separate calculations, sized for different purposes** - an investor might reasonably hold both, sized independently rather than treating one policy as covering both goals by default.

---

## Accounting for Dependents' Actual Future Location

**Where surviving dependents would actually continue living affects the needs calculation in a way that's easy to overlook** - cost of living, currency, local social benefits (some countries provide meaningfully more robust survivor benefits or social insurance than others), and local tax treatment of insurance payouts and inherited assets all vary by country. **An investor whose family might relocate after their death - back to a home country, for instance, if currently living abroad for the investor's career - should base the needs analysis on the cost of living and financial environment the family would actually face, not the investor's current location if that location wouldn't persist.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should the estate-tax-liquidity policy and the income-replacement policy be combined into one larger policy, or kept separate?**
They can be structured either way, but keeping them conceptually separate in the sizing calculation - even if ultimately consolidated into a single policy - helps ensure neither need is under- or over-funded relative to its own actual requirement, rather than a single number that vaguely tries to cover both.

**How often should a life insurance needs calculation be revisited?**
Revisiting every few years, or after any major life event (a child's birth, a significant change in income, a major change in portfolio value, or relocating to a different country), keeps the coverage amount aligned with actual current circumstances rather than a calculation done once early on and left unexamined as the underlying numbers change.

**Does a growing brokerage portfolio ever eliminate the need for life insurance entirely?**
In principle, yes - if the portfolio eventually grows large enough to fully cover the family's projected needs on its own, additional insurance coverage for income-replacement purposes becomes progressively less necessary, though the separate estate-tax-liquidity use case can persist independently as long as US-situs exposure above the exemption exists.

**Is it worth using an online life insurance calculator, or should this be done with a professional?**
An online calculator can provide a reasonable starting estimate for the basic framework, but the cross-border adjustments discussed in this guide - currency mismatch, potential estate tax exposure reducing net portfolio value, dependents' actual future location - generally aren't accounted for by generic calculators built for a single-country audience, making a professional review worthwhile once the rough numbers suggest coverage is actually needed.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Calculate a gross coverage need using income replacement, debt payoff, and future major expenses
- [ ] Subtract existing resources, including your current brokerage portfolio value, to reach a net coverage gap
- [ ] Convert the calculation into the currency your dependents would actually be spending in, not necessarily the currency you earn in
- [ ] Account for any reduction in net available portfolio value from potential US estate tax exposure
- [ ] Consider a decreasing coverage structure or periodic downward revision as your portfolio grows over time
- [ ] Base the calculation on where dependents would actually continue living, including relevant local cost of living and benefits
- [ ] Revisit the calculation after major life events or every few years rather than treating an early estimate as permanent

---

## Bottom Line

Sizing life insurance coverage for income replacement is a subtraction exercise - future need minus existing resources - and cross-border finances add real wrinkles: currency mismatch between income and where dependents would spend, potential US estate tax exposure reducing net portfolio value, and uncertainty about where dependents would actually continue living. Working through these factors deliberately, and revisiting the calculation periodically as circumstances change, produces a far more accurate coverage target than a generic, single-country calculator or a round number chosen without reasoning through the specifics.
