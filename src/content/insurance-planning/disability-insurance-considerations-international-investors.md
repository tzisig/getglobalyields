Most of this category's coverage so far addresses what happens to assets and beneficiaries after an investor's death. Disability insurance addresses a different, statistically more likely scenario: what happens to the income funding the entire investment plan if the investor becomes unable to work, well before retirement. It's a coverage gap that gets far less attention than life insurance in most people's financial planning, and the cross-border version of the problem is harder to solve than the domestic one.

---

## Why Disability Is a Genuinely Underrated Risk

**Over a full working career, the odds of experiencing a period of disability lasting a year or more are meaningfully higher than the odds of dying during the same working years** - a statistical reality that runs counter to most people's intuitive risk assessment, which tends to weight death far more heavily than long-term disability in everyday planning conversations. **The financial consequence of a long-term disability can be more severe than death in one specific sense: a disabled individual still needs ongoing income to live on, while life insurance is specifically designed to replace income after death - there's no equivalent "coverage after disability" built into most people's default financial planning the way life insurance is.**

**For an investor whose entire long-term plan depends on continuing to earn and invest** - contributing to a brokerage account, building toward the kind of long-term compounding covered throughout this site's [strategies](/strategies/) content - a disability that interrupts income for years, or permanently, doesn't just stop new contributions; it can force drawing down the very portfolio the investor spent years building, at exactly the wrong time.

---

## Why Cross-Border Access Is Harder Than for Life Insurance

**Disability insurance underwriting depends even more heavily than life insurance on verifiable, ongoing income documentation** - insurers need to confirm not just that an applicant is currently earning a specific income, but that the income is stable and verifiable enough to base a benefit amount on, since disability payouts are typically structured as a percentage of pre-disability income rather than a fixed death benefit amount. **This makes cross-border disability insurance underwriting genuinely more complex than the foreign national life insurance market covered in our [life insurance access guide](/insurance-planning/can-non-us-residents-buy-us-life-insurance/)** - an insurer needs ongoing proof of the exact thing (income) that disability insurance protects against losing, verified through a process built primarily around domestic income documentation systems.

**An investor whose income comes from employment in one country, while holding investments in a US brokerage account, generally needs to look for disability coverage in their country of actual employment and residency**, rather than expecting to access US-based disability insurance products the way they can access a US brokerage account - the insurance product itself is tied to where the insured actually works and earns, not to where their investment assets happen to be held.

---

## Employer-Provided Coverage: Often the More Practical Starting Point

**Many employers, particularly larger ones, provide group short-term and long-term disability coverage as part of a standard benefits package** - and for an investor working a conventional employment arrangement, this employer-provided coverage is frequently the most practically accessible starting point, since it doesn't require the individual underwriting process a private policy does. **The coverage amount and terms of employer-provided disability insurance are often more limited than what a private policy could provide** - commonly capped at a percentage of salary with a maximum dollar limit, and frequently ending if employment itself ends - which is worth understanding as a real limitation rather than assuming employer coverage fully addresses the risk on its own.

**For a self-employed investor, a freelancer, or someone running their own business** - a meaningfully more exposed position, since there's no employer-provided coverage to fall back on at all - private disability insurance, sourced through whatever channels are available in the country of actual residence and work, becomes a more directly necessary consideration rather than a supplemental one.

---

## What to Actually Check, Practically

**Confirm what your current employer-provided coverage actually includes** - the specific benefit percentage, the maximum monthly or annual benefit amount, the definition of disability used (some policies pay out only for total inability to work in any occupation, a stricter standard than inability to work in your own specific occupation), and what happens to the coverage if you leave the employer or relocate to a different country.

**If self-employed or otherwise without employer coverage, research private disability insurance options specifically available in your country of residence** - this is a domestic insurance market question in your home country, not a cross-border US-access question the way the life insurance guides in this category are, since disability coverage is fundamentally tied to your actual working situation and location.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does US Social Security disability insurance apply to a non-US resident who has never worked in the US?**
Generally no - US Social Security disability benefits are tied to a work history covered by the US Social Security system, which a non-US resident who has never worked in the US (and only holds US investments) generally hasn't accumulated, meaning this is not a source of coverage relevant to most readers of this site.

**Is disability insurance more or less important than life insurance for someone without dependents?**
Life insurance's primary purpose is replacing income for dependents after death - less relevant for someone without dependents relying on their income. Disability insurance protects the individual's own ability to fund their own life and continued investing regardless of dependents, making it arguably more universally relevant than life insurance for a single investor with no one else depending on their income.

**Can disability insurance be purchased to specifically protect the ability to keep contributing to a US brokerage investment plan?**
Not as a specifically labeled product - disability insurance generally replaces a percentage of your overall income rather than being earmarked for any specific use, including investment contributions, but replacing income broadly does indirectly protect your ability to continue funding an investment plan alongside all your other expenses.

**How does disability insurance interact with the life insurance and estate planning techniques covered elsewhere in this category?**
These address different, complementary risks - life insurance and estate planning address what happens to assets and dependents after death; disability insurance addresses maintaining income and the ability to keep investing while alive but unable to work. A complete risk picture generally considers both rather than treating either as a substitute for the other.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Recognize that long-term disability is a statistically underweighted risk relative to death in most people's default financial planning
- [ ] Review your current employer-provided disability coverage's actual benefit percentage, cap, and definition of disability, rather than assuming it's comprehensive
- [ ] Understand that cross-border disability insurance access is generally tied to your country of actual residence and employment, not to where your investment assets are held
- [ ] If self-employed or without employer coverage, treat researching private disability insurance in your home country as a genuine priority, not an afterthought
- [ ] Confirm what happens to any employer-provided coverage if you change employers or relocate internationally

---

## Bottom Line

Disability insurance protects against a genuinely underrated risk - losing the ability to earn and continue investing, well before death - and is generally harder to access across borders than life insurance, since it's tied to your actual country of employment and residency rather than to where your investment assets sit. Reviewing what coverage you actually have, whether through an employer or privately, is worth doing as its own step, separate from the estate-planning-focused life insurance considerations covered elsewhere in this category.
