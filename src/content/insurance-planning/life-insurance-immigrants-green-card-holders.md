Our [guide to whether non-US residents can buy US life insurance](/insurance-planning/can-non-us-residents-buy-us-life-insurance/) covers a genuine non-resident alien - someone with no US residency at all, applying from abroad. This article covers a different, larger group of readers entirely: immigrants who actually live in the US, whether as green card holders or on a work/student visa. Legally and financially, this is a fundamentally different starting point - a green card holder is a US tax resident, taxed on worldwide income exactly like a citizen - and insurers largely treat them that way too. A visa holder is a genuinely more complicated case, and conflating the two is where a lot of general "life insurance for immigrants" content goes wrong.

---

## Green Card Holders: Closer to "Same as a Citizen" Than Most Assume

**Once a green card holder has established roughly one to two years of continuous US residency, insurers largely underwrite them the same way they underwrite a citizen** - the same rate classes, the same product menu (term, whole, universal, indexed universal life), and the same medical underwriting process, with immigration status itself not functioning as a separate rating factor. Age, health, and lifestyle drive the price, not the green card.

**Before that residency threshold is met, some carriers still apply visa-holder-style scrutiny even to a green card holder** - worth knowing if you're newly arrived rather than assuming full citizen-equivalent treatment applies from day one. Multiple industry sources report no meaningful cost difference between a green card holder and a citizen for comparable coverage once past that early window, which is the single most useful thing to know if you're a permanent resident wondering whether your status will cost you anything on price - generally, it won't.

---

## Visa Holders: A Genuinely More Complicated Case

**This is where the real distinctions live.** Insurers commonly accept applications from specific non-immigrant visa categories - E, H-1B, K, L, O, and TN/TD are named across multiple carriers' own guidance - while F-1 student visa holders are, industry-wide, described as facing real difficulty getting coverage at all. Waiting periods before eligibility vary meaningfully by carrier: some require just one year of continuous US residency, others up to five, with three years of continuous presence commonly cited as the practical benchmark for accessing the *best* available rate class rather than just baseline eligibility. Processing for a visa holder's application typically takes six to eight weeks - noticeably slower than a citizen's, reflecting the added verification steps insurers layer on.

**Pricing for visa holders is driven less by the visa category itself than by a country-of-origin risk classification insurers apply** - applicants from what carriers categorize as lower-risk countries can access citizen-equivalent best rates, while applicants from higher-risk-classified countries face higher premiums or a narrower set of available rate classes, independent of which specific visa type they hold. No source in this research produced a standardized public percentage for exactly how much more a visa holder pays versus a citizen - that figure genuinely isn't published as a single industry-wide number, and any specific percentage you encounter elsewhere is worth treating skeptically rather than as a reliable benchmark.

---

## What Documentation to Expect Beyond the Standard Application

**Beyond what a citizen provides, expect to submit:** your passport along with visa type, expiration date, and country of issuance (or your green card number if applicable), a Social Security Number or - if you're not yet SSN-eligible - an ITIN (accepted by several major carriers including Prudential, Mutual of Omaha, and Transamerica), proof of US residential address, three to six months of US bank statements, proof of income or assets, and a W-8 or W-9 tax form as applicable to your status. Disclosure of your international travel plans for the next two years is also commonly requested. Coverage amounts above roughly $5 million typically trigger additional third-party financial verification, regardless of immigration status.

---

## The Question Most Guides Skip: What Happens If You Leave the US Later

**This is genuinely thinly documented, and worth flagging honestly rather than pretending there's a clean answer.** Some policies are only guaranteed valid as long as you remain a US resident - language that shows up in carrier materials without always being spelled out in plain terms at the point of sale. More concretely: **a green card holder who spends more than roughly twelve weeks a year outside the US can, with some carriers, be reclassified as a foreign national**, which can change the terms of an existing or future policy. Death benefits are generally described as payable worldwide regardless of where the insured is living when they die - but for a permanent (not term) policy, ongoing premium payment logistics and cash-value access can become genuinely complicated after a permanent relocation, in ways that aren't uniformly handled across carriers.

**The practical takeaway if relocation abroad is a realistic possibility for you**: contact your specific carrier directly about their policy on continued coverage, premium payment, and reclassification risk before you assume your existing US policy will simply continue working the same way once you're living elsewhere - this is one of the few points in this research where "it depends entirely on your specific insurer" is the honest answer, not a hedge.

## Which Carriers Actually Work With Immigrants

**Named companies that accept applications from immigrants and visa holders, per their own published guidance, include New York Life, Prudential, AIG, Mutual of Omaha, Transamerica, Lincoln Financial, Northwestern Mutual, Nationwide, Penn Mutual, MassMutual, John Hancock, Pacific Life, and Guardian.** Worth noting: Guardian's "Global Citizen Program" specifically targets high-net-worth non-resident foreign nationals abroad - a different product and audience than the resident-visa-holder coverage described throughout this article, so don't assume it's the right starting point if you're applying as a resident immigrant rather than from overseas.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does having a green card actually cost me anything on price compared to a citizen applying for the same coverage?**
Generally no, once you've established roughly one to two years of continuous US residency - multiple sources report no meaningful cost difference at that point. Age, health, and lifestyle drive your rate from there, not your immigration status.

**I'm on an F-1 student visa - can I get life insurance at all?**
This is the one visa category where sources consistently describe real difficulty getting any coverage, industry-wide - worth confirming directly with several carriers rather than assuming any single one will work, since F-1 holders face meaningfully narrower options than the named visa categories (E, H-1B, K, L, O, TN/TD) that insurers more routinely accept.

**Can I use an ITIN instead of a Social Security Number to apply?**
Yes, with several major carriers - Prudential, Mutual of Omaha, and Transamerica are specifically confirmed to accept an ITIN in place of an SSN, though not every carrier does, so confirm this directly with whichever insurer you're applying to.

**If my country of origin affects my rate, is there anything I can do about it?**
Not directly - the country-of-origin risk classification is set by the carrier's own underwriting guidelines, not something an individual applicant can negotiate. Shopping across multiple carriers is the practical lever, since classifications and the resulting rate impact aren't identical insurer to insurer.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] If you're a green card holder with over a year of US residency, expect citizen-equivalent underwriting and pricing - don't assume a status-based surcharge exists
- [ ] If you're on a visa, confirm your specific visa type is on the carrier's accepted list before applying, since F-1 and some other categories face real restrictions
- [ ] Gather visa/green card documentation, an SSN or ITIN, proof of US address, and recent bank statements before starting the application to avoid delays
- [ ] Ask your specific carrier directly about their policy on coverage continuation and reclassification risk if you might relocate abroad later
- [ ] Shop across multiple carriers if your country of origin might affect your rate class, since classifications vary by insurer

---

## Bottom Line

A green card holder is underwritten for US life insurance almost exactly like a citizen once past an initial residency window, with immigration status itself not functioning as a separate cost factor - the common assumption that being an immigrant automatically means higher premiums generally doesn't hold for lawful permanent residents. A visa holder's situation is genuinely more complex: real restrictions exist by visa category, pricing is shaped more by country-of-origin classification than the visa itself, and what happens to coverage after a later departure from the US is thinly documented enough that it's worth a direct conversation with your specific carrier rather than an assumption either way.
