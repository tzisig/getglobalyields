Key person insurance (still commonly called "key man insurance") is a policy a business buys on the life of someone whose death would genuinely disrupt it financially - a founder, a top revenue-generator, a specialist whose knowledge isn't easily replaced. The business pays the premiums, the business is the beneficiary, and the payout is meant to cover what actually happens next: lost revenue, the cost and time of finding a replacement, jittery lenders or investors. For a business with real US operations but an owner or key figure who isn't a US resident, the mechanics carry a few extra layers worth understanding before assuming this works the same way it does for a purely domestic company.

---

## The Deduction You Won't Get, and Why

**Key person insurance premiums are not tax-deductible, under a specific, named provision: IRC Section 264(a)(1).** The rule blocks a deduction for premiums on any life insurance policy covering an officer, employee, or anyone with a financial interest in the business, whenever the business itself is a direct or indirect beneficiary of the policy. The premiums get treated as a capital expense rather than an ordinary deductible business cost.

**The logic behind the rule is symmetry, not punishment.** The death benefit the business eventually receives is generally tax-free income. Letting a business deduct the premiums on top of receiving a tax-free payout would mean getting a tax advantage on both ends of the same transaction - the IRS closes that off by denying the deduction going in, in exchange for a tax-free benefit coming out. There's a narrow exception worth knowing about: if premiums are structured as part of the insured person's actual compensation - tied to salary continuation or a broader employee welfare arrangement rather than pure business protection - a portion may become deductible as compensation expense instead, a structuring question worth raising with a tax professional rather than assuming it applies by default.

---

## Getting Coverage When the Key Person Isn't a US Resident

**This is where key person insurance gets genuinely harder for an internationally-connected business, and it's the detail most general explainers of the product skip entirely.** Most US life insurers simply won't underwrite a policy on a non-resident alien at all. The insurers that will generally require a real, demonstrable financial connection to the US - business ownership, US real estate, US equity holdings, or other tangible ties - not just the fact that the business itself operates domestically.

**The underwriting process itself has to happen in the US.** Solicitation, the application, the medical exam, and policy delivery are all generally required to take place on US soil, not remotely from the insured person's home country - a real logistical constraint for an owner who splits time internationally or rarely visits the US directly. This is a narrower, more specific problem than the general question of whether a non-US resident can buy US life insurance at all, covered in more depth in our [access and alternatives guide](/insurance-planning/can-non-us-residents-buy-us-life-insurance/) - here the complication is compounded by the business context, since it isn't just the insured person's own eligibility at stake, but the business's ability to actually protect itself against the disruption their absence would cause.

## The Estate Tax Angle This Product Quietly Touches

**A detail worth connecting explicitly, since it rarely gets mentioned in general key-person-insurance coverage:** if the key person is themselves a non-resident alien with a personal ownership stake in the business, their own estate planning runs into the dramatic gap between US-resident and non-resident estate tax exemptions. As of 2026, a US resident's federal estate tax exemption sits at $15 million per person - made permanent, with no scheduled reduction, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed in mid-2025. A non-resident alien's exemption on US-situs assets, by contrast, is just $60,000, a gap covered in detail throughout this site's [estate planning category](/estate-planning/). Life insurance death benefits are generally treated as exempt from US estate tax regardless of the beneficiary's residency status - which means a personally-owned policy, separate from the business's own key-person coverage, can be a genuinely useful tool for an NRA business owner managing that specific $60,000-exemption exposure on their other US-situs holdings, a use case covered in more depth in our [life insurance vs brokerage assets guide](/estate-planning/life-insurance-vs-brokerage-assets-estate-planning/).

## What Actually Drives the Cost

Key person insurance pricing follows the same core underwriting factors as any life insurance policy - the insured person's age, health, and the coverage amount requested - layered with business-specific considerations: the policy typically needs to reflect a defensible estimate of the actual financial impact the person's loss would cause, which insurers may want justified with real figures (revenue attributable to the person, replacement and recruiting costs, outstanding loan guarantees tied to their involvement) rather than an arbitrary round number. For an internationally-connected business, the added underwriting complexity of confirming US ties and completing the process domestically can also translate into a longer, more document-intensive approval process than a purely domestic key-person policy would require, worth budgeting time for rather than assuming a fast turnaround.

---

## Sizing the Coverage: Three Methods, Not One Formula

There's no single official formula for how much key person coverage a business should carry, but three approaches show up consistently and are worth running together rather than picking just one. The **salary multiple method** takes the key person's annual compensation and multiplies it by five to ten - an employee earning $200,000 a year suggesting coverage somewhere between $1 million and $2 million. The **revenue multiple method** instead estimates how much revenue the person directly generates or influences and multiplies that by three to five years - a top revenue-driver bringing in $1 million annually implying $3-5 million in coverage. The **replacement cost method** builds up from the actual expected cost of the disruption itself: executive search and recruiting fees, onboarding and training costs, and the productivity gap during a realistic 6-to-12-month ramp-up period for a successor, sometimes expressed as (salary plus benefits, multiplied by expected replacement time) plus (lost monthly revenue, multiplied by months to recover).

**Running more than one of these methods and comparing the results** gives a sturdier answer than trusting any single formula in isolation - a business whose salary-multiple and revenue-multiple estimates land far apart from each other has likely mis-specified something (an inflated sense of one person's revenue contribution, or a compensation figure that doesn't reflect their real economic value), and that gap is worth investigating before settling on a coverage amount rather than defaulting to whichever number happens to be largest.

---

## When It Actually Makes Sense

**A business with genuine, concentrated dependence on one person** - a founder whose relationships or expertise the business can't easily replace, a technical specialist central to the product, a rainmaker who personally drives a large share of revenue - has a real case for key person coverage, separate from any broader estate or succession planning the individual might also need personally. **A business where the "key person" label is more aspirational than accurate** - spreading the same coverage logic across several interchangeable senior staff, none of whom is individually irreplaceable - is a weaker case, since the underlying premise (this specific person's absence would materially damage the business) is what justifies the cost and underwriting complexity in the first place.

---

## Bottom Line

Key person insurance trades a denied deduction for a tax-free payout, a fair exchange under IRC 264(a)(1) rather than a punitive one - but for a business owner or key employee who isn't a US resident, actually getting the coverage is the harder problem: most insurers won't underwrite non-resident aliens at all, and the ones that will require real US financial ties and an underwriting process conducted domestically. Layer in the $60,000-versus-$15-million estate tax exemption gap for any non-resident alien with a personal ownership stake, and key person insurance for an internationally-connected business touches more of this site's cross-border planning content than the product's simple premise suggests.
