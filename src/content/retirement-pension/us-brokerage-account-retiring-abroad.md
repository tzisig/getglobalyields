Relocating for retirement - a genuinely common pattern among the international investors this site serves - raises a practical question that's easy to overlook amid the larger logistics of a move: what actually happens to your existing US brokerage account when your country of tax residence changes.

---

## The Account Itself Generally Doesn't Need to Close

**Moving countries does not automatically require closing or transferring your brokerage account.** A US-facing broker like Interactive Brokers, built specifically to serve clients across many countries, is generally able to accommodate a residency change through an update to your account profile rather than requiring you to open an entirely new account. This is meaningfully simpler than, for example, many domestic banking relationships, which are often tied more rigidly to a specific country.

**What genuinely needs updating:**

- **Your address and tax residency on file with the broker** - this needs to reflect your actual new country of residence, not remain on the old country by default.
- **Your W-8BEN treaty claim** - the treaty rate you're entitled to on US dividend withholding is determined by your country of tax residence, covered throughout this site's [country-specific tax guides](/taxes/). Moving from a 15%-treaty country to a no-treaty country (or the reverse) means your actual withholding rate changes, and this needs to be reflected correctly going forward.

---

## Confirm Your Broker Actually Serves Your New Country

**This is the step most likely to cause a genuine problem if skipped.** Not every broker accepts residents of every country, and a broker that served you well in your prior country of residence may have more limited - or no - service for your new one. Before finalizing a retirement relocation, confirm directly with your broker whether accounts are still supported for residents of your destination country, rather than assuming continuity.

**If your broker does not serve your new country of residence**, you may need to transfer your holdings to a broker that does, ideally via an in-kind transfer (moving the actual securities rather than liquidating and repurchasing) to avoid triggering an unnecessary taxable event purely because of the relocation. Confirm both brokers support in-kind transfers before initiating this process.

---

## The Tax Picture Changes With Your Residency, Not Your Broker

Moving to a new country of tax residence means your ongoing investment income is now subject to that new country's domestic tax rules and its own specific treaty with the US - a genuinely different picture from wherever you were before, covered on a country-by-country basis throughout this site's [us-investing](/us-investing/) and [taxes](/taxes/) content. This is worth researching for your specific destination country before the move, not after, since it affects real planning decisions (timing of any planned sales, whether to realize gains before or after the move, and similar).

**A genuinely important edge case:** some countries impose an exit tax or deemed-disposition rule on departure, treating unrealized gains as if they were sold for tax purposes at the point you leave - independent of anything happening on the US brokerage side. This is set entirely by your departing country's rules, not by the US or your broker, and is worth confirming specifically with a tax advisor in your current country before an international retirement move, since it can create a real tax liability timed to the move itself rather than to any actual sale.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Confirm with your broker, before finalizing your move, that accounts are supported for residents of your destination country
- [ ] Update your address and tax residency information with your broker promptly after establishing residency in the new country
- [ ] Submit a new W-8BEN reflecting your new country of tax residence, since your treaty rate may change
- [ ] Research your destination country's tax treatment of US investment income and any relevant treaty, before the move rather than after
- [ ] Confirm with a tax advisor in your departing country whether an exit tax or deemed-disposition rule applies to your situation

---

## Bottom Line

A US brokerage account generally survives an international retirement relocation intact, but three things need active attention: confirming your broker still serves your new country, updating your tax documentation to reflect the new residency (and the different treaty rate that may come with it), and researching your destination country's tax rules before the move - along with checking whether your departing country imposes any exit tax tied to the relocation itself.
