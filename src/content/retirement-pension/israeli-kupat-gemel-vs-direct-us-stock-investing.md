Israeli investors researching US stock exposure often run into Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah - a flexible Israeli investment savings product offering a genuine capital gains tax exemption most direct brokerage accounts can't match. Whether it's a better vehicle for US stock exposure than a direct international brokerage account depends on your goals, your fund selection within the product, and critically, whether you are also a US citizen - a single fact that can flip the entire comparison.

---

## What Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah Actually Offers

Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah is a flexible investment savings plan available to Israeli residents, with an annual deposit limit (roughly NIS 80,000, adjusted periodically) and typical management fees in the 0.6%-0.9% range. **The genuine advantage:** funds held inside this structure are exempt from Israeli capital gains tax - a real, meaningful benefit compared to a standard taxable brokerage account, where capital gains are taxed under Israel's ordinary rules. Funds are also liquid and can generally be withdrawn without penalty (unlike a pure pension-locked account structure), giving the product genuine flexibility alongside its tax advantage.

**Fee structure worth comparing directly:** the 0.6%-0.9% management fee range compounds over time the same way any ongoing fee does, covered generally in our discussion of expense ratios throughout this site's [index-funds category](/index-funds/) - a meaningfully higher ongoing cost than the expense ratios on broad US index funds like VOO or VTI held directly, which is a real cost to weigh against the Israeli capital gains exemption, not simply assume is automatically outweighed by the tax benefit.

---

## The US Exposure Question

This is a fund-based product, not a direct brokerage account - you select from a menu of funds the Kupat Gemel provider offers, rather than picking individual US stocks or specific ETFs like TQQQ directly. Some Kupat Gemel funds do include meaningful S&P 500 exposure as part of their broader allocation, but this is indirect, blended exposure within a managed fund - not the direct, specific control over individual holdings that a standalone brokerage account provides.

**What this means practically:** if your goal is broad, diversified equity exposure with a real tax advantage and you're comfortable with a fund manager's specific allocation choices, Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah can serve that goal well. If your goal is holding specific US securities - TQQQ, individual stocks, or a specific ETF selection - a direct brokerage account is the only way to get that level of control, and Kupat Gemel is not a substitute for it, regardless of how favorable its Israeli tax treatment is.

## The Real Trap: PFIC Risk for US Citizens

**This is the single most important point in this comparison for a specific subset of readers.** If you are a US citizen or green card holder living in Israel - a genuinely common situation given the size of the American-Israeli community - Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah's underlying funds are very likely classified as Passive Foreign Investment Companies (PFICs) under US tax law, since they are foreign investment vehicles holding passive assets. As covered in detail in our [PFIC rules guide](/taxes/pfic-rules-international-investors/), PFIC classification triggers some of the harshest tax treatment in the US tax code.

**The mechanics of why PFIC treatment is so punitive, worked through:** absent a valid mark-to-market or qualified electing fund election (often unavailable in practice for foreign pension-style products like Kupat Gemel, since the fund typically doesn't provide the specific annual information a QEF election requires), gains and certain distributions from a PFIC are taxed under the "excess distribution" regime - a mechanic that allocates the gain ratably across your entire holding period, taxes the portion allocated to prior years at the highest marginal US tax rate applicable in each of those years (regardless of your actual tax bracket in those years), and adds an interest charge on top, as if you had owed and underpaid tax in each of those earlier years. This is meaningfully more punitive than ordinary capital gains treatment, which taxes the gain once, at your actual applicable rate, in the year of sale.

**Form 8621 filing burden:** a US person holding a PFIC is generally required to file Form 8621 annually for each PFIC holding - a complex form requiring detailed calculation under the excess distribution rules (or the mark-to-market/QEF rules, if elected and available), adding a genuine, recurring compliance burden and often meaningful tax-preparation cost on top of the substantive tax exposure itself.

**This is not a minor footnote for a US citizen in Israel.** The general capital gains tax exemption that makes Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah attractive for a non-US-connected Israeli resident can be substantially offset - or worse, turned into a net negative - by PFIC exposure on the US side for someone who is also a US person. This is exactly the kind of situation where the "best domestic product" and the "best product accounting for your full tax picture" diverge sharply, and where following only the Israeli-side advice without independently confirming the US-side consequence can be a costly mistake.

## The Comparison, Summarized

**For an Israeli resident with no US citizenship or US tax obligations:** Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah's capital gains exemption is a genuine, real advantage worth using, within the limits of the fund menu's actual US exposure and weighed against its higher management fee relative to a direct low-cost index fund.

**For a US citizen or green card holder resident in Israel:** the PFIC exposure this product likely creates needs to be weighed seriously against its Israeli-side tax benefit - for many US persons in this situation, a direct US brokerage account holding US-domiciled ETFs (which are not PFICs, since PFIC status applies to foreign, not US, investment vehicles) avoids the PFIC problem entirely, even though it forgoes the Israeli capital gains exemption and instead faces the standard US withholding framework under the [US-Israel tax treaty](/taxes/us-israel-tax-treaty-explained-capital-gains-dividends-2026/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there any way for a US person to use Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah without PFIC consequences?**
A mark-to-market or QEF election can, in principle, reduce the punitive impact of PFIC treatment, but both require specific conditions - mark-to-market requires the fund to be "marketable" under IRS rules, and QEF requires the fund to provide specific annual shareholder information most Israeli Kupot Gemel providers don't furnish. In practice, both elections are often unavailable, which is why this guide treats the excess distribution regime as the realistic default outcome rather than a worst-case scenario.

**Does this PFIC issue apply to other Israeli investment products beyond Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah specifically?**
Yes, in principle - most Israeli mutual funds and similar pooled investment vehicles held by a US person would face the same PFIC analysis, since the classification depends on the fund's structure and income type, not the specific product name. This guide focuses on Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah specifically because of how commonly it comes up for this site's Israeli readership, not because it's uniquely affected.

**If I'm not currently a US person but might become one (e.g., through marriage or a future green card), should that change my Kupat Gemel decision now?**
This is worth discussing with a cross-border tax advisor proactively if a future US tax connection is a realistic possibility, since PFIC exposure attaches based on your status during the years you hold the position - an investor who becomes a US person while already holding a Kupat Gemel position may face PFIC consequences on the full historical holding period, not just the period after their status changed.

**Does holding Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah create any FBAR or FATCA reporting obligation for a US person, separate from the PFIC issue?**
Very likely yes - a US person's foreign financial accounts, including an Israeli Kupat Gemel account, generally fall within FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and potentially FATCA (Form 8938) reporting requirements, entirely separate from and in addition to the PFIC-specific Form 8621 obligation discussed above. This is a genuinely layered compliance picture worth having a cross-border tax advisor map out completely rather than addressing one piece at a time.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Confirm whether you are a US citizen, green card holder, or otherwise a US person for tax purposes - this single fact determines whether the rest of this comparison tilts toward Kupat Gemel or a direct brokerage account
- [ ] If not a US person, evaluate Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah's specific fund menu for how much genuine US equity exposure it actually provides, and compare its management fee against direct low-cost index fund alternatives
- [ ] If a US person, get dedicated cross-border tax advice on your specific PFIC exposure before contributing meaningfully to this or any similar Israeli fund product
- [ ] If a US person, ask specifically whether a mark-to-market or QEF election is realistically available for the specific fund, rather than assuming the excess distribution regime is your only option
- [ ] If PFIC exposure is a concern, compare the real after-tax outcome of a direct US brokerage account (no PFIC issue, full US withholding per the [US-Israel tax treaty](/taxes/us-israel-tax-treaty-explained-capital-gains-dividends-2026/)) against Kupat Gemel's Israeli-side benefit weighed against its US-side cost
- [ ] If a US person, confirm your FBAR and FATCA reporting obligations for any Kupat Gemel account, separate from the PFIC-specific Form 8621 requirement

## Bottom Line

Kupat Gemel Lehashkaah offers a real, meaningful Israeli tax advantage and reasonable US equity exposure through its fund menu - a genuinely good option for an Israeli resident with no US tax connection. For a US citizen or green card holder in Israel, the same product likely creates real, mechanically punitive PFIC exposure - taxed under the excess distribution regime with an added interest charge, plus an annual Form 8621 filing burden - that can outweigh its domestic tax benefit, making a direct US brokerage account the more carefully-considered choice despite forgoing the Israeli capital gains exemption.
