If you worked in the US at some point, accumulated a Traditional IRA or 401(k) balance, and have since moved abroad and become a non-resident alien, one question eventually becomes unavoidable: does the RMD requirement still apply to you, even from outside the US and even without US citizenship. It does - RMDs follow the account type, not your citizenship or current residency, a fact that catches a genuine number of former US workers off guard years after they've relocated and stopped thinking of themselves as connected to the US tax system at all.

---

## What RMDs Are and Which Accounts They Apply To

Required Minimum Distributions are mandatory annual withdrawals the IRS requires from certain tax-advantaged retirement accounts once the account holder reaches a specific age - currently 73, rising to 75 for those born in 1960 or later, under current law. RMD rules apply to Traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, and similar defined-contribution plans. **Roth IRAs are the notable exception** - the original account owner does not face lifetime RMDs on a Roth IRA, though this treatment differs for account beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA.

**The amount owed each year** is calculated as the account's prior-year-end balance divided by an IRS life-expectancy factor specific to the account holder's age - the calculation itself does not vary based on citizenship or residency status, using the same standardized IRS life-expectancy tables regardless of where the account holder lives.

**A worked illustration:** an account holder turning 75 with a Traditional IRA balance of $200,000 as of the prior year-end would divide that balance by the applicable IRS life-expectancy factor for their age (a specific number published in IRS tables, generally in a range that produces a distribution somewhere around 4-5% of the balance at that age) to arrive at their required distribution amount for the year - a figure that must be withdrawn regardless of whether the account holder needs or wants the cash, or faces the penalty described below.

---

## Why This Applies Regardless of Where You Live

RMD rules are a feature of the account type itself, established under US tax law when the account was created and funded - they do not change based on where the account holder currently resides or what citizenship they hold. A non-resident alien who accumulated a Traditional IRA or 401(k) balance while working in the US, and later relocated abroad permanently, is still subject to the same RMD schedule as if they had remained a US resident, with the same age thresholds and the same calculation methodology.

**This connects directly to a point covered in our [401(k) and IRA access guide](/retirement-pension/401k-ira-access-non-us-citizens/):** while a nonresident alien generally cannot make *new* contributions to these accounts without qualifying US-source earned income, an *existing* balance accumulated during a period of US work remains fully subject to ongoing RMD rules once the applicable age is reached, regardless of current residency or citizenship status. The eligibility-to-contribute question and the obligation-to-distribute question are governed by entirely separate rules, and it's a mistake to assume the same "must have US earned income" logic that governs contributions also governs the RMD obligation - it doesn't.

## Withholding on RMD Distributions

RMD withdrawals taken by a non-resident alien are generally subject to US withholding - the default rate is 30%, though this can be reduced if a specific provision in your country's tax treaty with the US addresses pension or retirement account distributions specifically. **This is worth checking specifically, rather than assuming it matches the standard dividend withholding rate covered throughout this site's [country-specific tax guides](/taxes/)** - treaty treatment of pension and retirement distributions is a distinct provision from the dividend article in the same treaty, appearing in a different section of the treaty text, and the rates do not always match. Some treaties provide meaningfully more favorable treatment for pension-type distributions than for ordinary dividend income; others don't address the distinction at all, defaulting to the standard rate.

**A practical step worth taking well before reaching RMD age:** contact your account custodian and confirm both (a) whether they will process a treaty-reduced withholding rate on your behalf, which requires the appropriate documentation on file, and (b) whether they can process distributions to a foreign bank account or require a US-based account - a genuinely practical logistics question separate from the tax-rate question, covered in more detail from the broader brokerage-account perspective in our [retiring abroad guide](/retirement-pension/us-brokerage-account-retiring-abroad/).

## The Penalty for Missing an RMD

Failing to take a required distribution on time results in a real penalty - historically up to 25% of the amount that should have been withdrawn, though this can potentially be reduced to 10% if corrected within a specified window after the missed deadline. This penalty structure applies regardless of citizenship or residency, making it genuinely important for a former US worker living abroad to actually track their RMD obligation rather than assuming distance from the US, or years of not thinking about the account, somehow exempts them from a deadline the IRS still expects to be met.

**Why this specifically catches internationally mobile former US workers:** an investor who worked in the US decades earlier, accumulated a modest 401(k) or IRA balance, and then built the rest of their financial life entirely outside the US system may genuinely forget the account exists, or assume that having left the US and stopped filing US returns for years somehow ended the obligation. It doesn't - the account remains subject to US tax rules for as long as it exists, independent of the account holder's broader tax filing history or current connection to the US.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I avoid RMDs entirely by converting my Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA before reaching the RMD age?**
A Roth conversion is a standard option available to eliminate future RMD obligations on the converted amount, since Roth IRAs aren't subject to owner-lifetime RMDs - but the conversion itself is generally a taxable event, and for a nonresident alien, the specific mechanics and tax consequences of executing a Roth conversion from abroad should be confirmed with a cross-border tax advisor before assuming it's a straightforward or automatically beneficial move.

**What happens to RMD obligations if I inherit a Traditional IRA or 401(k) from a US relative while living abroad?**
Inherited retirement accounts follow their own, separate set of distribution rules (often requiring full distribution within a set number of years for many types of beneficiaries under current law), distinct from the original-owner RMD rules covered in this guide - a genuinely different set of rules worth researching specifically if this situation applies to you, rather than assuming the same age-based RMD framework applies.

**Does my home country also tax RMD distributions, in addition to US withholding?**
This depends entirely on your home country's own tax rules regarding foreign retirement account distributions, a separate question from the US withholding covered in this guide - some countries provide relief for double taxation on this type of income under their treaty with the US, others tax it under their own domestic rules regardless of US withholding already applied. Confirm this separately with a tax advisor in your country of residence.

**Is there a minimum account balance below which RMDs don't apply?**
No - the RMD requirement applies based on account type and age, not account balance; even a modest legacy balance from a brief period of US employment remains subject to the same RMD rules as a much larger account, once the applicable age threshold is reached.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] If you have a legacy Traditional IRA or 401(k) balance from working in the US and are now living abroad, confirm your current age against the applicable RMD start age (73 or 75, depending on birth year)
- [ ] Set up a reliable system to track and take RMDs annually, since the penalty for missing one is real and applies regardless of your current country of residence
- [ ] Confirm the specific withholding rate that applies to RMD distributions under your country's treaty with the US, since it may differ from the standard dividend withholding rate
- [ ] Confirm with your account custodian whether they can still service the account and process distributions for a non-resident alien - some providers restrict services for account holders outside the US
- [ ] Consider whether a Roth conversion before reaching RMD age makes sense for your situation, with cross-border tax advice specific to executing it from abroad
- [ ] Remember Roth IRAs are not subject to the same lifetime RMD requirement, if you're deciding how to structure any rollover from an existing Traditional account
- [ ] Separately confirm your home country's tax treatment of RMD income, independent of US withholding

---

## Bottom Line

RMDs are tied to the account, not to where you live or what passport you hold - a non-resident alien with a legacy Traditional IRA or 401(k) from a period of US employment remains subject to the same distribution schedule and the same real, meaningful penalties for missing it as a US resident would be. If this describes your situation, confirm your custodian can still service the account from abroad, get current guidance on your specific treaty's withholding treatment for retirement distributions, and set up a reliable tracking system well before reaching the applicable age rather than discovering the obligation after already missing a deadline.
