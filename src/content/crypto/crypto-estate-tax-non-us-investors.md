This site's [estate planning category](/estate-planning/) covers, in detail, how US-situs assets - stock in a US corporation, most notably - expose a non-resident alien's estate to US tax above a $60,000 exemption, covered specifically in our [$60,000 exemption guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/). That entire framework depends on a settled, well-understood concept: situs, the legal determination of where an asset is considered "located" for estate tax purposes. Cryptocurrency doesn't have an equally settled situs rule, and that gap is worth understanding honestly rather than assuming an answer that doesn't yet clearly exist.

---

## Why Situs Matters So Much for This Question

**US estate tax for a non-resident alien only reaches US-situs assets** - the entire structure of the $60,000ex emption and the graduated rate schedule above it, covered in our base estate planning guide, applies specifically to property classified as US-situs. Stock in a US corporation has a clear, long-settled situs rule (US-situs, regardless of where the physical stock certificate or brokerage account is located, or where the investor lives). **Cryptocurrency has no equivalent long-settled rule specifically addressing its situs for US estate tax purposes.**

**This isn't a minor technical gap - it's the single most consequential open question determining whether a non-resident alien's direct cryptocurrency holdings are exposed to US estate tax at all.** Traditional situs rules were developed around concepts like where a company is incorporated, where physical property is located, or where a debt obligation is payable - none of which map cleanly onto a decentralized digital asset with no incorporation, no physical location, and no single issuing entity in the way a stock has an issuing corporation.

---

## What Is and Isn't Settled

**What's genuinely unclear: whether directly held cryptocurrency (in a self-custodied wallet or on an exchange) held by a non-resident alien would be treated as US-situs property for US estate tax purposes**, and if so, based on what specific factor - the investor's own domicile, the location of the exchange or custodian holding the assets, the location of the private keys, or some other yet-to-be-established criterion. **No definitive, comprehensive IRS guidance directly addressing this specific question currently exists in the way clear guidance exists for stock, real property, or the life insurance situs rule covered in our [life insurance vs brokerage assets guide](/estate-planning/life-insurance-vs-brokerage-assets-estate-planning/).**

**What's more settled: a US-listed spot Bitcoin ETF share is a security issued by a US-domiciled fund entity, following the same clear US-situs treatment that applies to any other US stock or ETF share**, covered generally in our [$60,000 exemption guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/). This is the same clear-versus-unclear distinction that shows up elsewhere in this site's content - direct real property ownership triggering clear FIRPTA rules versus the publicly traded stock exception, or direct crypto's situs ambiguity versus a crypto ETF's clear equity-security treatment.

---

## Why This Makes a Crypto ETF the More Predictable Estate Planning Position

**An investor specifically concerned about US estate tax exposure, who wants a predictable, well-understood tax position rather than an open legal question hanging over their estate, has a real reason to prefer a Bitcoin ETF over direct crypto ownership beyond the custody and capital-gains considerations covered in our [Bitcoin ETF vs direct ownership guide](/crypto/bitcoin-etf-vs-direct-crypto-ownership/).** With the ETF, the estate tax analysis is exactly the same as it would be for holding any other US-listed stock or ETF - subject to the $60,000 exemption framework, with a known, calculable exposure. With direct crypto ownership, heirs and estate administrators would be navigating a genuinely unresolved area of tax law at exactly the worst possible time to be doing so - during an actual estate settlement, under time pressure, with real money at stake.

**This uncertainty cuts both ways and shouldn't be read as "direct crypto ownership is definitely worse for estate tax purposes"** - it's entirely possible a future definitive rule (through IRS guidance, regulation, or case law) determines that direct crypto held by a non-resident alien is not US-situs at all, which would be a more favorable outcome than the ETF's clear US-situs treatment. The point isn't that direct ownership is definitely worse - it's that the outcome is currently unknown, while the ETF's outcome is currently known and predictable.

## What This Means Practically for Now

**An investor with meaningful direct cryptocurrency holdings and genuine US estate tax planning concerns should treat this as a specific, standalone question to raise directly with a cross-border estate planning professional who follows current crypto tax guidance developments**, rather than assuming either a favorable or unfavorable outcome based on general reasoning from how other asset types are treated. **This is an area where "the guidance may have changed since this was written" is a particularly live concern** - crypto tax and estate law guidance has evolved meaningfully over a relatively short period, and staying current on this specific question matters more here than for the more settled areas covered elsewhere on this site.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does holding crypto through a foreign (non-US) exchange change the situs analysis?**
This is itself an open question within the broader open question - even the specific factors that might eventually determine crypto's situs (exchange location, custodian location, investor domicile) remain unsettled, so a foreign exchange doesn't provide a clear, confirmed answer any more than a US-based exchange does.

**Is there any current guidance at all on this topic, or is it a complete blank?**
Tax professionals and commentators have written analysis and proposed frameworks for how crypto situs might eventually be resolved, drawing analogies to existing situs rules for other intangible property - but analysis and commentary aren't the same as definitive IRS guidance or settled case law, and treating informed speculation as a settled answer would be a mistake.

**Does this uncertainty apply to stablecoins the same way it applies to Bitcoin?**
The underlying situs ambiguity is a function of cryptocurrency's decentralized, no-single-issuer structure generally, which applies to stablecoins in the same basic sense - though a stablecoin's centralized issuer (unlike Bitcoin's lack of one) could theoretically become a relevant factor in a future situs analysis in a way that doesn't apply the same way to Bitcoin; this itself remains unresolved.

**Should I avoid direct crypto ownership entirely because of this estate tax uncertainty?**
Not necessarily - this is one factor among several (alongside custody, tax-on-gains treatment, and access considerations covered elsewhere in this category) to weigh in the direct-versus-ETF decision, not automatically a decisive reason on its own, particularly for an investor with modest holdings where the estate tax stakes are correspondingly smaller.
