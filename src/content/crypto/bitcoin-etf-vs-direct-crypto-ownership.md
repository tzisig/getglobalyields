Since the SEC approved the first spot Bitcoin ETFs for US listing in January 2024, an investor wanting price exposure to Bitcoin has had a genuinely different path available than setting up a crypto exchange account and a wallet: simply buying a fund like IBIT or FBTC through the same brokerage account already used for QQQ or TQQQ. The two paths end up tracking roughly the same underlying asset price, but the mechanics underneath - custody, tax treatment, and what you actually own - are meaningfully different.

---

## What a Spot Bitcoin ETF Actually Holds

**A spot Bitcoin ETF holds actual Bitcoin directly (through a designated custodian), with shares of the fund designed to track the underlying Bitcoin price** - a structurally different approach from a Bitcoin futures ETF, which holds futures contracts rather than the underlying asset itself and can diverge from spot price movement over time due to futures-market mechanics. **Since the January 2024 approvals, several large asset managers - including BlackRock (IBIT), Fidelity (FBTC), and others - have launched competing spot Bitcoin ETFs**, all pursuing the same basic structure with differences mainly in fee levels and the specific custodian arrangements each uses.

---

## Custody: Who Actually Holds the Keys

**Direct cryptocurrency ownership means the investor (or an exchange holding assets on the investor's behalf) is responsible for the underlying custody of the asset** - either through self-custody (controlling the private keys directly, with the security responsibility and technical complexity that involves) or through an exchange's custodial services (trusting the exchange's own security practices and solvency). **A Bitcoin ETF shifts custody entirely to the fund's professional custodian**, removing the individual investor from any direct custody responsibility - no private keys to manage, no wallet security decisions, no direct exposure to an individual exchange's own security or solvency risk in the way a crypto exchange account holder has.

**This custody shift is a genuine trade-off, not a strict improvement**: self-custody gives an investor direct, unmediated ownership of the underlying asset with no intermediary counterparty risk at all, while an ETF introduces the fund structure itself - and its custodian - as an intermediary layer, along with the fund's own management fee, that direct ownership doesn't carry. An investor with strong views on the philosophical or practical value of direct, self-custodied ownership may specifically prefer that path despite the added complexity; an investor primarily interested in price exposure without the custody responsibility generally finds the ETF wrapper more practical.

---

## Access: A Brokerage Account vs a Crypto Exchange Account

**A Bitcoin ETF requires nothing beyond the same brokerage account access already used for any other US-listed stock or ETF** - the same account that holds VOO, QQQ, or TQQQ can hold IBIT, without any separate account, separate KYC process, or separate platform. **Direct crypto ownership requires a separate relationship with a crypto exchange**, each with its own account opening process, and - covered in more depth in our [non-US resident crypto exchange access guide](/crypto/can-non-us-residents-buy-crypto-etfs/) - varying levels of accessibility and restriction depending on the specific exchange and the investor's country of residence.

**For an international investor who already has a US brokerage account for stock investing**, the ETF path adds essentially zero incremental account-access complexity, while direct ownership adds an entirely separate platform relationship, KYC process, and set of country-specific access considerations on top of what stock investing already requires.

---

## Tax Treatment: A Meaningfully Simpler Picture

**As covered in our [crypto tax treatment guide](/crypto/crypto-tax-treatment-international-investors/), the US tax sourcing analysis for a non-resident alien's direct cryptocurrency capital gains is genuinely less settled than the framework governing US-listed securities.** A Bitcoin ETF share, by contrast, is a standard US-listed security, following the same well-established capital gains framework that applies to any other US stock or ETF - generally no US tax on capital gains for a non-resident alien, the same treatment covered throughout this site's other equity content, without the sourcing ambiguity that comes with holding the underlying cryptocurrency directly.

**This tax-treatment simplification is one of the more practically significant reasons an international investor might specifically prefer the ETF path over direct ownership** - not because the underlying price exposure is different, but because the tax analysis governing that exposure is meaningfully more settled and predictable.

---

## What Doesn't Change Between the Two Paths

**Price volatility is identical in principle** - both paths track the same underlying Bitcoin price movement, so an investor isn't reducing Bitcoin's inherent volatility by choosing the ETF wrapper over direct ownership, or increasing it by choosing direct ownership over the ETF. **Neither path changes the fundamental investment thesis or risk of holding Bitcoin exposure** - the ETF wrapper changes custody, access, and tax mechanics, not the underlying asset's price behavior itself.

**Fund expense ratios are a real, ongoing cost unique to the ETF path** that direct ownership doesn't carry in the same form (though direct ownership carries its own costs - exchange trading fees, potential wallet or custody costs) - worth comparing specifically for the funds under consideration rather than assuming the ETF wrapper is cost-free relative to direct ownership.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a Bitcoin ETF be converted into actual Bitcoin, or redeemed for the underlying asset?**
For most retail investors, no - spot Bitcoin ETF shares are generally bought and sold on the exchange like any other ETF, without a practical mechanism for an individual retail shareholder to redeem shares for the underlying Bitcoin directly; that kind of in-kind redemption is typically limited to large institutional "authorized participants" involved in the fund's creation and redemption process.

**Do Bitcoin ETFs pay any distribution similar to a dividend?**
No - since Bitcoin itself generates no income or yield, a spot Bitcoin ETF has no equivalent income to distribute, meaning there's no dividend-style payment or withholding question to consider, unlike the REIT dividends covered in our [real estate category](/real-estate/) or standard equity dividends.

**Is there a spot Ethereum ETF, or is this limited to Bitcoin?**
Spot Ethereum ETFs have also launched on US exchanges following a similar regulatory path as Bitcoin ETFs, though the specific fund lineup and details continue to develop - confirm current fund availability and structure directly rather than assuming the Bitcoin ETF landscape fully describes the Ethereum ETF landscape.

**Does holding a Bitcoin ETF avoid the record-keeping burden described for direct crypto trading?**
Yes, substantially - since an ETF share is a single security position, buying and selling it generates the same kind of straightforward capital gains record-keeping as any other stock or ETF, rather than the potentially large number of separate taxable events that active crypto-to-crypto trading can generate under direct ownership.

## Bottom Line

A spot Bitcoin ETF and direct cryptocurrency ownership provide the same underlying price exposure through genuinely different mechanics - custody shifts from the individual to a professional custodian, access simplifies to the same brokerage account already used for stocks, and the tax analysis becomes meaningfully more settled and predictable. For an international investor whose goal is price exposure without the custody responsibility and tax-sourcing ambiguity of direct ownership, the ETF path is generally the more practical default - direct ownership earns its added complexity mainly for an investor with a specific reason to want unmediated, self-custodied ownership of the underlying asset itself.
