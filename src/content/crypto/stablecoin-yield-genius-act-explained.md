Stablecoins like USDC and USDT are pegged to the dollar, and for years the pitch for holding one instead of cash was simple: park it somewhere paying real yield, with none of bitcoin's volatility. That pitch got genuinely more complicated in 2025 and 2026, not because yield disappeared, but because federal law now draws a real, specific line between the kind of yield that's legal and the kind that isn't - and where you get it from matters more than it used to.

---

## What the GENIUS Act Actually Prohibits

**The GENIUS Act, signed into law July 18, 2025, is the first comprehensive federal framework for who can issue a dollar-pegged stablecoin and how they have to operate.** One of its core provisions: stablecoin issuers are prohibited from paying interest or yield directly to holders - no cash payments, no token rewards, no similar benefit passed through from the issuer itself. Companies like Circle (issuer of USDC) and Paxos Trust don't pay you anything for simply holding their stablecoin anymore, by law.

**The ban specifically targets issuers, and that distinction is doing real work.** In the immediate aftermath, affiliated platforms and intermediaries kept offering yield on the same stablecoins anyway - a genuine gap between what the issuer itself can do and what a separate platform built around that stablecoin can do. Regulators noticed: the OCC has proposed expanding the prohibition to cover affiliates and third parties too, not just the issuer directly, though that expansion remains a proposal rather than settled law as of this writing. A separate law, the CLARITY Act, reached a compromise in May 2026 that preserves reward programs specifically while banning passive, bank-style interest - a real, if legally intricate, distinction between "rewards" and "interest" that determines what's actually still permitted.

---

## Real Yield Still Exists - Just Not From the Issuer

**This is the part a simple "GENIUS Act banned stablecoin yield" headline misses: yield on USDC and USDT is genuinely still available in 2026, just structurally routed through lending platforms rather than paid by the issuer itself.** DeFi lending protocols - Aave, Compound, Morpho, Spark, and Sky among them - currently offer somewhere in the 3.5% to 9% APY range on USDC and USDT deposits, with rates moving based on real-time borrowing demand and protocol utilization rather than a fixed, issuer-set number. Centralized platforms offer a similar range through different mechanisms: Bybit's flexible USDT and USDC products run in the high-3% range, while platforms like Ledn (6.5-8.5% APY) and others price higher, generally reflecting a different risk profile rather than free extra return.

---

## Why the Yield Isn't Free - The Real Risk Split

**DeFi and centralized ("CeFi") platforms deliver similar-sounding yields through genuinely different risk mechanisms, and knowing which one you're taking matters more than the headline rate.** A DeFi lending protocol like Aave generates yield from actual borrowers paying interest to borrow against collateral, executed through smart contracts - meaning the real risk is smart contract failure or exploit, not a company's solvency. A centralized platform offering stablecoin yield is generally taking counterparty risk on your behalf and passing a share back to you - meaning the real risk is that specific company's own solvency and custody practices, the same category of risk that caused several high-profile crypto lending platform collapses in prior years. A materially higher advertised rate from one platform over another isn't found money; it's compensation for one of these two risk categories being higher, or a specific platform running thinner risk management than a more conservative peer offering a lower headline number.

---

## What This Means Practically

**For an investor who held stablecoins specifically for the old issuer-paid interest model**, that specific product no longer exists in its previous form - the GENIUS Act genuinely closed that door. **For an investor willing to accept either smart-contract risk (DeFi lending) or platform counterparty risk (CeFi lending) in exchange for yield**, real yield in the 3-10% range remains available on the same stablecoins, just sourced through a different mechanism than before. The practical decision isn't "is stablecoin yield dead" - it demonstrably isn't - but which specific risk category you're comfortable taking on to earn it, since the regulatory change shifted where the yield comes from without eliminating the underlying lending-and-borrowing activity that generates it.

## Tax Treatment for International Investors

**Interest or yield earned on stablecoins is generally treated as ordinary income, not capital gains, for US tax purposes** - a meaningfully different character than the capital gains treatment covered for direct coin appreciation in our [crypto tax guide](/taxes/crypto-etf-tax-treatment-international-investors/). Whether a specific yield payment counts as US-source income for a non-resident alien depends on where the paying platform is organized and how the specific product is structured - a genuinely more fact-specific question than the general "NRAs don't owe US capital gains tax" framework covered elsewhere on this site for direct crypto price appreciation, and worth confirming with a tax professional given how recently this entire regulatory landscape has shifted.

---

## Bottom Line

The GENIUS Act didn't kill stablecoin yield - it banned one specific channel (direct issuer payments) while leaving lending-platform-generated yield, through both DeFi protocols and centralized platforms, genuinely intact and currently paying real rates in the 3-10% range. The regulatory story is more precise than "yield is banned": it's "issuer-paid interest is banned, lending-based yield through a different structure is not," and understanding which category any specific product you're considering actually falls into is the real due diligence question in 2026, not whether stablecoin yield exists at all.
