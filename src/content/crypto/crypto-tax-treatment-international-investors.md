An investor who's built a comfortable understanding of how US stock dividends and capital gains work for a non-resident alien - the 30% (or treaty-reduced) withholding on dividends, generally no US tax on capital gains, covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - runs into a genuinely different framework when adding cryptocurrency to the picture. The IRS doesn't treat crypto as a currency or as a security in the same regulatory sense as a stock. It treats it as property, and that single classification decision ripples through everything else about how it's taxed.

---

## Why "Property" Instead of "Currency" or "Security" Matters

**The IRS formally classified cryptocurrency as property for US federal tax purposes, not as currency**, meaning transactions involving crypto are analyzed the same way transactions involving any other property (like a stock, a piece of art, or real estate) are analyzed - not the way foreign currency exchange gains and losses are handled, and not identically to how a security like a stock or ETF share is handled either, even though the practical result for many investors ends up similar.

**The practical consequence of "property" treatment: disposing of cryptocurrency - selling it, trading it for another cryptocurrency, or using it to purchase goods or services - is generally a taxable event generating a capital gain or loss**, calculated as the difference between the crypto's value at disposal and its cost basis (what was originally paid for it). This is a meaningfully broader trigger for taxable events than stock investing typically involves: trading one cryptocurrency for another is a disposal event under property treatment, in a way that, say, converting one currency to another to buy a stock generally isn't treated as a taxable event in itself.

---

## How This Differs From the Dividend-Withholding Framework

**Most of this site's tax content addresses dividend withholding - the mechanism that applies to income a US company pays out to shareholders**, covered in depth in our [qualified vs non-qualified dividends guide](/taxes/qualified-vs-non-qualified-dividends-foreign-investors/). **Cryptocurrency itself generates no equivalent income distribution** - Bitcoin, Ethereum, and most major cryptocurrencies don't pay anything resembling a dividend, since there's no underlying company distributing profits. This means the entire dividend-withholding framework that dominates this site's other tax content simply doesn't apply to direct cryptocurrency holdings - the tax question for crypto is almost entirely about capital gains treatment on disposal, not about withholding on ongoing distributions.

---

## Capital Gains Sourcing for Non-US Investors: A Genuinely Less Settled Area

**For US-listed stocks and ETFs, capital gains realized by a non-resident alien are generally not subject to US tax at all** (outside specific exceptions like the 183-day physical presence rule for individuals, and FIRPTA for certain real-estate-linked gains, covered in our [FIRPTA guide](/real-estate/firpta-explained-foreign-investors/)) - a well-established, settled area of US international tax law. **Applying the same logic to direct cryptocurrency holdings is less clearly settled**, because the sourcing rules that determine whether a gain is even "US-source" in the first place were developed for traditional property and securities, and cryptocurrency's borderless, exchange-agnostic nature doesn't map cleanly onto those existing sourcing frameworks the way a US-listed stock (with an obvious US connection) does.

**This is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over: the specific US tax treatment of a non-US investor's direct cryptocurrency capital gains is a genuinely less mature area of tax guidance than the dividend and capital-gains framework covered elsewhere on this site.** Where a stock's US-source status is unambiguous, a cryptocurrency held in a wallet with no inherent geographic location raises sourcing questions that don't have the same decades of settled case law and IRS guidance behind them. This is exactly the kind of area where relying on a general educational overview like this one, instead of current, specific professional tax advice, is a mistake.

## Where a Bitcoin ETF Changes the Analysis

**Holding crypto exposure through a US-listed spot Bitcoin ETF, rather than owning the underlying cryptocurrency directly, meaningfully changes the tax analysis** - covered in more depth in our [Bitcoin ETF vs direct ownership guide](/crypto/bitcoin-etf-vs-direct-crypto-ownership/) - because an ETF share is a security with the same well-established US-source characteristics as any other US-listed stock or ETF, sidestepping the direct-crypto sourcing ambiguity described above. **This is a genuinely useful simplification for an investor primarily interested in price exposure to cryptocurrency, without wanting to navigate the less settled tax questions that come with direct ownership.**

---

## Record-Keeping: A Real, Practical Burden

**Because property treatment makes trading one cryptocurrency for another a taxable disposal event, an investor actively trading between different cryptocurrencies can generate a large number of separate taxable transactions to track**, each requiring its own cost basis and disposal value calculation - a meaningfully heavier record-keeping burden than holding a single stock position and only realizing a gain or loss on an occasional sale. Specialized crypto tax tracking software has developed specifically to address this record-keeping burden, reflecting how genuinely impractical manual tracking becomes for an investor trading across multiple cryptocurrencies and exchanges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does my home country's tax treatment of crypto follow the same "property" classification as the US?**
Not necessarily - crypto tax classification varies significantly by country, with some jurisdictions treating it similarly to the US property approach, others as a distinct asset category with its own rules, and some with less developed guidance altogether. Your home country's specific treatment needs to be confirmed independently rather than assumed to mirror the US approach.

**Do crypto exchanges provide the same kind of tax reporting document (like a 1042-S) that US brokers provide for stock dividends?**
This varies significantly by exchange and has been an evolving area of US tax reporting requirements - some exchanges provide detailed transaction reporting, others provide considerably less, and non-US-based exchanges in particular may provide minimal or no US-style tax documentation at all, making independent record-keeping especially important.

**Is staking or earning yield on crypto holdings taxed the same way as simply holding and later selling?**
No - income earned through staking, lending, or other yield-generating crypto activities is generally treated as its own separate taxable event at the time it's received, distinct from the capital gain or loss calculated when the underlying crypto asset is eventually sold - a more complex picture than simple buy-and-hold crypto ownership.

**Should I assume the "no US tax on capital gains for non-resident aliens" rule that applies to stocks also applies cleanly to my direct crypto holdings?**
Not without professional confirmation - as this guide discusses, the sourcing analysis for direct crypto holdings is genuinely less settled than for US-listed stocks, and assuming the stock rule applies identically to crypto without confirming it specifically is a real risk given how much less mature this specific area of guidance is.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that the IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, not currency, meaning most disposals (including crypto-to-crypto trades) are taxable events
- [ ] Recognize that crypto generates no dividend-like income, so the withholding framework covered elsewhere on this site doesn't apply to direct holdings
- [ ] Treat the US tax sourcing analysis for a non-resident alien's direct crypto capital gains as a genuinely less settled area requiring specific professional advice, not an assumed extension of the stock capital-gains rule
- [ ] Consider a US-listed spot Bitcoin ETF as a way to access crypto price exposure through the same well-established security tax framework that applies to any other US-listed stock or ETF
- [ ] Maintain detailed transaction records given how many separate taxable events active crypto trading can generate
- [ ] Confirm your home country's specific crypto tax classification independently rather than assuming it mirrors the US property approach

## Bottom Line

Cryptocurrency's classification as property rather than currency or a security means it follows a genuinely different tax framework than the dividend-withholding mechanics that dominate most of this site's other tax content - and for a non-resident alien holding crypto directly, the specific US tax sourcing analysis is a meaningfully less settled area than the well-established rules governing US stock capital gains. That uncertainty, more than any specific rate or rule, is the single most important thing to understand before assuming direct crypto ownership works the same way stock ownership does for tax purposes.
