ETHA, BlackRock's iShares Ethereum Trust, is the largest spot Ethereum ETF by assets - roughly $6.1 billion as of early 2026, at a 0.25% expense ratio. What most people defaulting to it as "the Ethereum ETF" don't realize: it doesn't stake its underlying ether, and staking is where a real, meaningful chunk of Ethereum's actual return comes from. A newer generation of competing products now captures that yield, following a specific regulatory shift in March 2026 that most coverage of the original spot ETH ETF launches predates.

---

## What Changed in March 2026

**On March 17, 2026, the SEC and CFTC jointly published an interpretive release classifying 16 named cryptocurrencies, Ethereum included, as digital commodities rather than securities - and explicitly clarified that staking rewards themselves don't implicate securities law.** That single regulatory clarification is what unlocked staking-enabled ETH ETFs as a real, launchable product category. Before it, fund sponsors had genuine legal uncertainty about whether staking their holdings and passing the rewards to shareholders would turn the product into something requiring a different regulatory framework entirely.

**The unlock happened fast once it landed.** Grayscale's staking-enabled Ethereum product became the first US-listed fund to actually distribute staking rewards as cash dividends, paying out $9.4 million to shareholders by early 2026. BlackRock followed with a dedicated product - ETHB, its staked Ethereum Trust, launched March 12, 2026 with $107 million in seed capital, staking 70-95% of its holdings through Coinbase Prime and distributing 82% of the resulting rewards to shareholders monthly.

---

## ETHA Specifically Still Doesn't Stake

**This is the detail worth being precise about: ETHA and ETHB are two different BlackRock products, not one fund that added a feature.** ETHA remains a plain spot-holding trust - it owns ether and tracks its price, full stop, with no staking component. ETHB is the separate, newer vehicle actually built to stake and distribute yield. An investor who bought ETHA at launch and has simply held it since isn't automatically getting staking exposure just because BlackRock now offers a staking product under a different ticker.

**Fidelity's FETH sits in a similar position, one step further back.** As of recent disclosures, Fidelity's fund still doesn't stake any of its ether, despite having filed with Cboe BZX back in March 2025 to add the capability. The pending proposal would let Fidelity stake up to 100% of the trust's ether with no minimum threshold, paying rewards to holders net of a 15% staking fee, on top of the existing 0.25% sponsor fee. Until that filing is actually approved and implemented, FETH holders are in the same position as ETHA holders - full price exposure to ether, zero staking yield.

---

## What the Missed Yield Is Actually Worth

**Ethereum staking yield has generally run in the rough 3.3% to 4.2% APY range** since the March 2026 regulatory clarity settled the legal question. That's not a trivial amount to leave on the table for a long-term holder - it's a real, recurring yield on top of whatever ether's price does, roughly comparable in scale to a solid dividend-stock yield, except most spot ETH ETF holders aren't currently receiving any of it because they're holding a non-staking product by default, often without realizing a staking alternative now exists.

**The trade-off isn't free for the products that do stake, worth naming honestly.** Staking involves lock-up and validator mechanics that add real operational complexity a pure spot-holding trust doesn't have, and the fee structures on staking products (like FETH's proposed 15% cut of staking rewards on top of the base sponsor fee) mean holders don't capture the full headline staking rate - the 82% distribution rate on ETHB's rewards is a concrete example of that gap between gross staking yield and what actually reaches shareholders.

---

## Why ETHB Stakes 70-95%, Not 100%

**The gap between "fully staked" and what ETHB actually does comes down to a real, mechanical liquidity constraint that's worth understanding rather than treating as an arbitrary conservatism.** Unstaking ether isn't instant - validators exit through a dynamic queue that limits how many can leave per epoch, and when many validators try to exit at once during market stress, withdrawal times have stretched to weeks, with over 2.5 million ETH (roughly $12 billion) sitting in exit queues during one such period. An ETF that needs to meet daily investor redemptions can't simply tell a shareholder wanting to sell that their ether is stuck in a multi-week unstaking queue.

**That's the specific reason native-staking ETF issuers keep a meaningful chunk of holdings unstaked and immediately liquid** - industry practice for funds using direct (non-liquid) staking runs as high as a 50-60% idle buffer in some structures, specifically to handle redemptions without running into the withdrawal queue. Staking 70-95% rather than 100%, as ETHB does, is a deliberate trade-off between capturing more yield and maintaining enough instantly-available ether to meet redemption demand without forcing a delay on shareholders trying to exit.

---

## Choosing Between Them

**An investor who wants pure, simple price exposure to ether, with the deepest liquidity and lowest complexity**, has a reasonable case for sticking with ETHA specifically - it remains the largest, most liquid spot ETH product, which matters for anyone trading in and out or running an options strategy against the position. **An investor specifically motivated by capturing Ethereum's real staking yield on top of price exposure** needs to actively choose a staking-enabled product like ETHB rather than assuming any "Ethereum ETF" automatically includes it - the product category has split into two meaningfully different things carrying the same general "spot Ethereum ETF" label.

---

## Access and Tax Treatment for International Investors

**All of these products trade as standard US-listed ETFs**, accessible through any broker offering US market access - but the tax mechanics underneath them are genuinely different from an ordinary equity ETF's, which matters more, not less, once staking distributions enter the picture. ETHA, FETH, and their peers are structured as grantor trusts rather than regulated investment companies, a structural difference covered in full in our [spot crypto ETF tax treatment guide](/taxes/crypto-etf-tax-treatment-international-investors/) - the standard "treaty rate or 30% default" dividend withholding framework that applies to a conventional stock or RIC-structured ETF doesn't map directly onto a grantor trust the same way. A non-US investor considering a staking-enabled product specifically should treat the staking-reward distributions as a new, still-settling wrinkle on top of that existing grantor-trust structure, and confirm the specific reporting and withholding treatment with their broker rather than assuming it mirrors an ordinary dividend.

---

## Bottom Line

ETHA remains the biggest, most liquid spot Ethereum ETF, but "biggest" and "captures ether's full real return" aren't the same thing anymore. Since March 2026's regulatory clarity, a newer generation of staking-enabled products - BlackRock's own ETHB among them - actually distributes the 3-4%-ish annual staking yield that a plain spot-holding trust like ETHA or FETH simply doesn't touch. Anyone defaulting to "the Ethereum ETF" without checking which specific product they're buying is very possibly leaving real, recurring yield on the table without realizing it.
