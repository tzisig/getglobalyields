Every country-specific guide on this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) covers income tax treaties - a network the US maintains with dozens of countries. Estate tax treaties are a genuinely different, much shorter list. If your country of residence has one, it can meaningfully change your estate tax exposure beyond the standard [$60,000 non-resident alien exemption](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/). If it doesn't, the base rules apply without modification - and understanding why this specific list is so much shorter helps explain why you can't simply assume treaty coverage based on your country's income tax treaty status.

---

## The Countries With US Estate Tax Treaties

As of 2026, the US maintains estate tax treaties with a relatively short list of countries, including Australia, Austria, Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, South Africa, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. **This is a meaningfully shorter list than the income tax treaty network** covered throughout this site's country-specific guides - a country having a US income tax treaty does not mean it also has a US estate tax treaty, and the two lists should not be assumed to match.

**Confirm your specific country's status directly** rather than assuming either way based on its income tax treaty status - the estate tax treaty network was negotiated separately and covers a distinctly smaller set of countries.

---

## Why This List Is So Much Shorter Than the Income Tax Treaty Network

**Estate tax treaties are negotiated separately from income tax treaties**, at different times, addressing a fundamentally different type of tax with different administrative and political considerations on both sides. Many of the US's estate tax treaties were negotiated decades ago, during a period when estate and gift tax coordination was a more active area of international tax treaty-making than it has been more recently - meaning the current list reflects a specific historical era of treaty negotiation rather than a continuously updated, actively expanding network the way income tax treaties have been.

**The practical consequence:** many countries with a robust, modern US income tax treaty - covering dividend withholding, tie-breaker residency rules, and other provisions covered throughout this site's [country-specific guides](/taxes/) - simply have no corresponding estate tax treaty at all, having never negotiated one, or having negotiated one that was subsequently allowed to lapse or was never ratified. This isn't an oversight or a gap waiting to be filled; it reflects the genuinely separate, much narrower scope of the estate tax treaty program specifically.

---

## What Estate Tax Treaties Generally Do

Estate tax treaties exist for the same fundamental reason income tax treaties do: preventing the same assets from being taxed in full by two countries at the same time. The specific mechanics vary by treaty, but commonly include:

- **Clarifying domicile** for estate tax purposes, resolving cases where both countries might otherwise claim taxing rights over the same estate
- **In some treaties, allowing a pro-rated exemption** based on the ratio of US-situs assets to the decedent's total worldwide estate - potentially delivering meaningfully more relief than the flat $60,000 non-resident alien exemption, depending on the specific estate's composition
- **Reducing or eliminating double taxation** where both the US and the treaty partner country might otherwise impose estate-type tax on the same assets

**How the pro-rated exemption mechanic works, conceptually:** rather than applying the flat $60,000 figure regardless of estate size, a treaty with this provision effectively extends the same proportional share of the (much larger) US citizen exemption that the decedent's US-situs assets represent relative to their total worldwide estate. For an estate where US-situs assets are a small fraction of a much larger worldwide estate, this pro-rated approach can produce a dramatically higher effective exemption than the flat $60,000 figure - though the precise mechanics, including exactly how "worldwide estate" is defined and valued for this calculation, are treaty-specific and require careful, professional application rather than a rough estimate.

**This guide is not in a position to specify the exact mechanics for each individual treaty** - they genuinely differ from one agreement to the next, and applying the wrong one incorrectly (or assuming a generic treaty benefit that your specific treaty doesn't actually provide) is a real risk. If your country is on the treaty list, this is a case for a cross-border estate planning attorney to confirm the specific provisions that apply to your situation, not a generic summary this article could responsibly provide.

## If Your Country Isn't on the List

**The standard $60,000 exemption and rates up to 40%, covered in our [dedicated guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/), apply without treaty modification.** This describes the majority of countries covered throughout this site's country-specific [us-investing](/us-investing/) and [taxes](/taxes/) guides - most readers of this site are in countries without a US estate tax treaty, making the base exemption and rate structure, along with the mitigation approaches covered in our [avoidance strategies guide](/estate-planning/avoiding-us-estate-tax-brokerage-account/), the relevant planning framework rather than any treaty-based relief.

**This is worth internalizing specifically because of how the income tax treaty comparison can mislead investors** - an investor from a country with a strong, favorable US income tax treaty (a low dividend withholding rate, for instance) might reasonably but incorrectly assume similarly favorable estate tax treatment exists, when in fact no estate tax treaty exists at all for their country. The two questions need to be researched entirely separately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If my country isn't on the treaty list today, could that change in the future?**
In principle, yes - new treaties can be negotiated, though as discussed above, the estate tax treaty program has been considerably less active in recent decades than the income tax treaty network, making new additions to this specific list a less frequent occurrence than periodic updates to income tax treaties.

**Does having a US estate tax treaty automatically mean a better outcome than the standard $60,000 exemption?**
Not automatically in every case - the specific provisions determine the actual outcome for your specific estate composition, and while the pro-rated exemption mechanic described above is often more favorable, the precise result depends on your specific worldwide estate size and composition, which is exactly why professional confirmation matters rather than assuming treaty coverage is uniformly better.

**Can I choose whether to apply treaty benefits, or are they automatic?**
Treaty benefits generally need to be properly claimed and documented as part of the estate tax filing process (Form 706-NA and any required treaty-specific attachments), not automatically applied without action - another reason professional guidance matters for an estate with treaty-country involvement.

**Does the estate tax treaty list include every major European or wealthy country?**
No - notably, several major economies with substantial US income tax treaties and significant international investor populations covered throughout this site are absent from the estate tax treaty list, reinforcing that the two treaty networks should never be assumed to overlap based on general country prominence or income tax treaty strength alone.
