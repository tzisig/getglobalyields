Ancillary probate is a second, local court proceeding required in a US state when someone who died domiciled elsewhere - another state, or another country - owned property there. For a foreign investor holding a US brokerage account, whether that account actually needs one comes down to a legal technicality most families never hear about until they're already stuck in the process: intangible property like a brokerage account is legally deemed to be located wherever its owner was domiciled, not wherever the broker's headquarters sits. In theory, that should mean a foreign national's US brokerage account isn't a US-situs asset for probate purposes at all. In practice, the broker holding the money often doesn't see it that way.

---

## What Ancillary Probate Actually Is, and What Triggers It

**Ancillary probate exists to appoint a local fiduciary with legal authority over in-state assets and debts when the main probate proceeding is happening somewhere else** - a narrower, secondary process layered on top of the decedent's primary ("domiciliary") probate in their actual home jurisdiction. The trigger is a legal concept called situs, and situs rules differ meaningfully by asset type. **Real estate and tangible personal property - a house, a car, art physically sitting in a state - reliably trigger ancillary probate wherever they're physically located.**

**Intangible property is different, and this is the detail that matters most here: a bank or brokerage account is legally deemed to be sited wherever the decedent was domiciled, not wherever the custodian bank or broker is headquartered** - confirmed under New York law specifically, and consistent with the general principle across US jurisdictions. Followed to its logical conclusion, a foreign national's US brokerage account isn't a New York-situs (or any-state-situs) asset at all - it should properly fall under whatever probate or succession process applies in the decedent's actual home country, not a US ancillary proceeding.

**This is a genuinely different situation from a US resident's estate**, even a naturalized former non-resident: a US domiciliary generally needs only one probate proceeding, in their home state. Ancillary probate is specifically a foreign-decedent or out-of-state-decedent problem, and the legal theory around intangible-asset situs is exactly why a family's actual experience with a brokerage account can diverge sharply from what the underlying law technically requires.

---

## The Gap Between the Law and What Actually Happens

**In practice, brokers and banks routinely insist on a court-appointed local fiduciary before releasing funds, regardless of whether the account is technically a state-situs asset under the intangible-property rule above.** A brokerage holding a deceased foreign client's account isn't in the business of independently adjudicating situs law - it wants a legally recognized representative it can safely release funds to, and the path of least resistance for many institutions is requiring exactly the ancillary proceeding that, in strict legal theory, shouldn't have been necessary. **This gap between the law and institutional practice is the single most important thing for a family to understand going in**: knowing the legal theory doesn't automatically mean your broker will act on it without a fight, and being prepared for that reality up front is more useful than being technically correct and still stuck.

**For Interactive Brokers specifically**, the estate process starts with an email to the firm's estate-processing team along with a certified death certificate and required forms. Third-party probate-service guidance (not IBKR's own published documentation, so treat this as a secondary source) describes IBKR as generally following the probate process of the decedent's state of domicile, and states that a non-US-resident's estate valued over $60,000 needs an **IRS Transfer Certificate (Form 5173)** before assets release - a step that can add another six to nine months on top of everything else, with total processing time on an international account cited at three months to over a year depending on complexity. Schwab's specific internal process wasn't independently confirmable in this research, though Schwab's own international estate-planning materials flag that forced heirship rules in a client's home country can override arrangements made under US law - a related complication covered further below.

## What It Actually Costs and How Long It Takes

**General ancillary probate costs commonly run $2,000-$20,000 or more**, driven by attorney fees (either a percentage of asset value, roughly 3-7% in states with statutory fee schedules, or hourly rates of $250-$500), court filing fees ($150-$1,200 depending on the state), and sometimes a fiduciary bond running 1-3% of asset value. **Timelines commonly run six to eighteen months**, with a straightforward case sometimes resolving in four to six months. New York's specifics illustrate the mechanics well: filing fees on a sliding scale from $45 for estates under $10,000 up to $1,250 for estates over $500,000, a mandatory seven-month creditor claim period once Letters (the court's formal authorization) are issued, and nine to eighteen months to full distribution in practice.

**A separate local attorney is typically required, and not just for convenience** - the person appointed as local fiduciary usually has to qualify under that specific state's residency rules. Florida is a clear example: state law requires a personal representative to be either a Florida resident or a close blood relative of the decedent, meaning a foreign national's friend or distant relative, however trusted, generally can't legally serve in that role.

## What Foreign Documentation the US Court Actually Needs

**A US ancillary proceeding built on a foreign decedent's home-country probate or succession documents requires those documents to be authenticated for use in a US court** - either through an "exemplified" or "triple-sealed" certified copy process, or through an apostille (the Hague Convention's standardized authentication certificate) for documents originating in a Hague member country, plus a certified translation with a formal translator's affidavit for any non-English document. Apostille processing commonly takes two to four weeks through normal channels, or as little as one to three days through an expedited paid service - worth knowing if the broader timeline is already under pressure. Once authenticated, the US court issues "ancillary letters" to the local fiduciary based on that foreign record.

## Real Alternatives - and Their Real Limits

**A Transfer on Death (TOD) designation on the brokerage account itself avoids probate in every US state except Texas** - the simplest, lowest-cost fix where it applies, covered in more detail in our [TOD designations guide](/estate-planning/tod-designations-foreign-investors/). For Interactive Brokers specifically, whether a non-US-resident can actually set a TOD designation is genuinely unclear from available sources - some documentation implies TOD registration is a US-resident feature, while other account-inheritance materials describe a process for registering a non-US-resident beneficiary using national ID rather than a US-specific identifier. **This is worth confirming directly with your specific broker rather than assuming either answer applies to your account.**

**A properly funded [revocable living trust](/estate-planning/revocable-living-trust-explained/) reliably avoids probate**, since assets titled in the trust's name bypass the court process entirely regardless of citizenship - but it's worth repeating plainly: **a revocable trust does not avoid US estate tax.** Assets inside it remain part of your taxable estate exactly as they would if held directly, a distinction covered fully in our [$60,000 exemption guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/) and our [LLC vs trust structure guide](/estate-planning/llc-trust-vs-direct-holding-us-stocks/). Probate avoidance and estate-tax reduction are two entirely separate problems, and it's a common, costly mistake to assume solving one automatically solves the other.

## The Complication Civil-Law-Country Investors Should Know About

**If you're domiciled in a country with a forced-heirship legal tradition - common across much of Europe and Latin America - a TOD designation or trust arrangement made under US law can be challenged back home**, even when it was validly executed under US rules. A French appellate court ruling in 2026 (Cour d'appel de Versailles, reported May 2026) allowed forced heirship to reach French-situated assets despite a valid foreign-law election having been made - and forced-heirship mandatory shares in these jurisdictions can run as high as 50-75% of an estate, overriding a decedent's own stated wishes for a meaningful portion of what they leave behind. **If your home country follows forced heirship, a US probate-avoidance strategy that looks airtight under US law may still face a real challenge at home** - worth raising specifically with an estate attorney familiar with your home country's succession rules, not just a US-focused one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If my brokerage account technically isn't a US-situs asset, can I just tell the broker that and skip ancillary probate entirely?**
Not reliably in practice - as described above, many brokers require a court-appointed fiduciary regardless of the underlying situs theory, simply as their own risk-management policy. Knowing the legal argument is useful context, including potentially for an attorney negotiating with the broker, but it isn't a guarantee the broker will release funds without one.

**Does a small brokerage account balance mean I can skip this process entirely?**
Possibly - many states offer a small-estate affidavit process for estates under a specific dollar threshold, avoiding full probate (ancillary or otherwise). Thresholds are state-specific and vary considerably, so check your specific state's figure rather than assuming a blanket exemption applies.

**Who actually pays for ancillary probate - does it come out of the estate, or does my family need to front the cost?**
Attorney fees, court costs, and any bond are generally paid from the estate's own assets once the proceeding is underway, but there can be a real cash-flow gap early on, since the funds needed to pay for the process are often the very assets tied up in it - worth discussing directly with the attorney handling the case about how fees are actually structured and paid.

**Is there anything I can do now, while alive, to make this process faster for my family later?**
Yes - setting up a TOD designation where your broker supports it for a non-US-resident, or properly funding a revocable trust, are both real steps you can take now specifically to avoid the process described in this article, rather than leaving your family to navigate it after the fact.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that your brokerage account's legal situs is your domicile, not the broker's location - but confirm your specific broker's actual practice, since many require a court fiduciary regardless
- [ ] Ask your specific broker directly whether a TOD/beneficiary designation is available to a non-US-resident account holder
- [ ] If pursuing a trust instead, confirm it's actually funded (assets retitled into it), and remember it solves probate, not estate tax
- [ ] If you're from a forced-heirship jurisdiction, consult an attorney familiar with your home country's succession rules in addition to a US-focused one
- [ ] Keep home-country probate or succession documents in a form that can be authenticated (apostille or exemplified copy) and translated quickly if needed
- [ ] Check your state's small-estate affidavit threshold before assuming full ancillary probate is required for a modest account balance

## Bottom Line

A foreign investor's US brokerage account sits in a genuine gap between legal theory and institutional practice: the law generally treats it as located at your home-country domicile, not the broker's location, which in principle should mean US ancillary probate isn't required at all - but brokers routinely demand a court-appointed local fiduciary anyway, and families who don't know this in advance lose real time and money bridging that gap after the fact. A TOD designation where available, or a properly funded revocable trust, are the two real ways to close that gap before it becomes your family's problem - confirmed directly with your specific broker, since availability and practice vary enough that no general article, this one included, can promise a single answer for your account.
