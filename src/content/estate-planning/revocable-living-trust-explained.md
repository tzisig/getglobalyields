A revocable living trust does exactly one thing especially well: it lets assets pass to your beneficiaries without going through probate. It's frequently marketed as doing more than that - reducing estate tax, protecting assets from creditors - and it does neither, a distinction worth getting right before you pay an attorney to set one up. For a non-US investor holding a US brokerage account specifically, there's a second, genuinely more complicated question worth understanding before assuming a trust works exactly the same way it does for a US person.

---

## What a Revocable Living Trust Actually Is

**Three roles exist: the grantor (who creates and funds the trust), the trustee (who manages the assets - often the grantor themselves, while alive and capable), and the beneficiaries (who receive the assets per the trust's terms).** Unlike a will, which only takes legal effect at death, a revocable trust is effective immediately once signed and funded - it operates as a real, functioning legal arrangement during your lifetime, not just a set of instructions that activates later.

**"Funding" the trust means retitling assets into its name** - a brokerage account, for instance, gets re-registered from your own name into something like "Jane Doe, Trustee of the Jane Doe Revocable Living Trust." This step is where most of the real value gets lost when it's skipped: an unfunded trust is a common, costly mistake, because any asset left titled in your own name still goes through probate regardless of the trust document existing at all.

**What it does not do: reduce estate tax or protect assets from creditors.** Because you, as grantor, retain full control and the power to revoke or amend the trust at any time, the IRS treats everything in it as still part of your taxable estate, and creditors can generally still reach it the same as if you held the assets directly. Only an irrevocable trust - where the grantor permanently gives up ownership and control - can meaningfully achieve either of those two goals. This is one of the most common points of confusion in how revocable trusts get marketed, and worth stating plainly before assuming a trust solves a tax or asset-protection problem it isn't built to solve.

## What It Actually Costs

**A DIY or online-service trust typically runs $400-$1,000.** An attorney-drafted trust costs meaningfully more - commonly $1,500-$4,000 nationally for a standard package, exceeding $5,000 for a genuinely complex estate, and running $5,000-$10,000+ specifically in higher-cost states like California. Average estate attorney rates cluster around $370/hour nationally, with high-cost metros (New York, California, Massachusetts) running $400-$800/hour against $150-$400/hour in lower-cost regions. **A full attorney-drafted package typically bundles more than just the trust document itself** - usually a pour-over will (which catches any asset accidentally left outside the trust and directs it in), a financial power of attorney, a healthcare directive, and a HIPAA authorization, all as one coordinated set rather than separate purchases.

---

## Trust vs Will - What Actually Differs

**Probate avoidance is the headline difference.** A properly funded trust bypasses probate entirely for the assets inside it; a will, by contrast, must go through probate before anything distributes to beneficiaries - a court process that commonly takes months and carries its own separate costs. **Privacy is the second real difference**: a probated will becomes public record, meaning your assets, their values, and your beneficiaries are all searchable by anyone who looks; a trust document is never filed with a court and stays private.

**The cost tradeoff runs in opposite directions**: a trust costs more upfront to set up properly, but avoids the delay and expense of probate later. A will is cheaper to draft initially, but shifts real cost and months of delay onto your estate after you're gone. **For smaller estates, neither may be strictly necessary** - many states offer a small-estate affidavit process that skips full probate for estates under a specific dollar threshold. California's threshold, as one concrete example, sits at $239,700 for deaths on or after April 1, 2026 - state-specific and worth checking against your own state's actual figure rather than assuming this number applies universally.

## Can a Non-US Investor Actually Do This?

**Yes, mechanically - a non-resident alien can establish and serve as grantor of a US revocable trust holding US assets.** But real friction exists at several points, and this is where a generic "how trusts work" article stops being sufficient.

**Not every general-practice attorney takes on foreign clients for this**, and several firms explicitly market dedicated "international estate planning" services specifically because working with an NRA's cross-border situation isn't every estate attorney's regular practice - a specialized attorney is the more realistic starting point than your first web search result for "trust attorney near me."

**A revocable trust with a living grantor is a disregarded entity for income tax purposes** - it uses the grantor's own SSN or ITIN, not a separate EIN, meaning a foreign grantor without SSN eligibility needs to apply for an ITIN via Form W-7 as part of the setup process. **Opening a US brokerage account in the trust's name generally requires the same core documentation an NRA needs for a personal account** - a completed [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/), passport, foreign tax ID, and typically that same ITIN - though the specific policies for accepting a trust account from a foreign grantor vary by broker and are worth confirming directly rather than assuming.

**The genuinely technical part: whether the trust is classified as "domestic" or "foreign" for US tax purposes** depends on a two-part test under the tax code - a court test (a US court has primary supervision) and a control test (US persons, often meaning a US-based trustee, control all substantial decisions). Passing both matters, and having a foreign grantor doesn't automatically fail either test on its own. **Separately, whether the trust qualifies for favorable "grantor trust" income-tax treatment - where income is taxed to you directly as an NRA, generally via 30% withholding on US-source dividends and interest - versus being taxed as a more complex non-grantor foreign trust involves rules that sources genuinely don't agree on cleanly for every fact pattern.** This is a real area of technical disagreement, not a settled point this article can flatten into a single confident rule - it's worth having an attorney experienced specifically in cross-border trust structures confirm your exact classification before you rely on any general description of how it should work, including this one.

---

## Does It Still Avoid Probate for a Foreign Grantor?

**Yes - probate avoidance is a function of how an asset is titled, not the owner's citizenship or residency.** A properly funded revocable trust keeps US-situs assets like a brokerage account out of probate for a foreign grantor exactly the same way it does for a US person; state probate courts look at title, not nationality. **This is worth contrasting against the estate-tax exposure question**, which is a genuinely different topic - a revocable trust doesn't reduce your taxable estate regardless of citizenship, and a non-resident alien's US-situs assets remain subject to the much lower $60,000 exemption (versus $15 million for a US person under the current permanent exemption) covered in detail in our [estate tax exemption guide](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/) and our [LLC vs trust structure guide](/estate-planning/llc-trust-vs-direct-holding-us-stocks/) - probate avoidance and estate-tax reduction are two separate problems, and a revocable trust only solves the first one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a revocable living trust the simplest way for a foreign investor to avoid probate on a US brokerage account, or are there lighter-weight alternatives?**
Not necessarily the simplest - a [Transfer on Death (TOD) designation](/estate-planning/tod-designations-foreign-investors/) on the account itself can achieve probate avoidance for that specific asset with far less setup cost and complexity than a full trust, though it doesn't offer the broader estate-planning structure (successor trustee provisions, incapacity planning, consolidating multiple assets) a trust provides. Which is right depends on how much beyond "avoid probate on this one account" you actually need.

**If I set up a trust in my home country, can I just use that instead of a US one?**
Generally no - a foreign trust doesn't automatically hold or manage US-situs assets the way a properly structured US trust does, and using a foreign trust structure introduces its own separate, often more complex, US tax classification questions rather than a simpler path. A US-situs trust, properly structured for your NRA status, is the more standard approach.

**Does my trust need its own separate US bank account?**
Not necessarily for a revocable trust holding brokerage assets specifically - since it's a disregarded entity using your own SSN/ITIN, the practical need for a separate trust bank account depends more on whether you're also using it to hold other cash assets, which is a setup question worth discussing directly with your attorney rather than assuming either way.

**Can I be both the grantor and the trustee of my own revocable trust as a foreign national?**
Yes, this is the standard structure for most revocable trusts regardless of the grantor's citizenship - but remember the trust's overall domestic/foreign classification depends on the court and control tests described above, which can be affected by who serves as trustee, particularly if a US-based co-trustee or successor trustee is part of the structure.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand clearly that a revocable trust avoids probate only - it does not reduce estate tax or protect assets from creditors
- [ ] Budget realistically: $400-$1,000 DIY, or $1,500-$4,000+ attorney-drafted (higher in high-cost states), typically bundled with a pour-over will and power of attorney documents
- [ ] If a non-US grantor, find an attorney with specific cross-border/international estate planning experience, not a generalist
- [ ] Apply for an ITIN if you don't have SSN eligibility, since the trust uses your own tax ID as a disregarded entity
- [ ] Actually fund the trust - retitle every intended asset into its name - since an unfunded trust provides none of its intended benefit
- [ ] Get your trust's domestic-vs-foreign and grantor-trust tax classification confirmed directly by your attorney rather than assuming a single general rule applies to your situation

---

## Bottom Line

A revocable living trust is a genuinely useful, well-understood tool for avoiding probate and keeping your estate's details private - and a non-resident alien can absolutely use one for US-situs assets like a brokerage account, with probate avoidance working exactly the same way it does for a US person. What it isn't is an estate-tax reduction or asset-protection tool, for anyone, regardless of citizenship. And for a foreign grantor specifically, the trust's US tax classification - domestic versus foreign, grantor versus non-grantor - is a real technical question that sources don't answer identically, making a cross-border-specialist attorney worth the cost rather than an optional upgrade.
