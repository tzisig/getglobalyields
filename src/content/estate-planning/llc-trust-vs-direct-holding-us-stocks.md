Once an international investor understands the [$60,000 estate tax exemption](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/), it's natural to ask whether holding US stocks through an entity - an LLC, a trust, a foreign corporation - could sidestep the exposure entirely. Sometimes it genuinely can. It's also one of the areas of cross-border planning most prone to being implemented incorrectly, creating real complexity and cost without actually achieving the intended tax result.

---

## Why This Isn't a Simple Yes-or-No Question

**The core issue: the entity structure itself has to be designed correctly to change the estate tax analysis, and a poorly designed structure can fail to achieve the intended result while still adding real cost and complexity.** This is genuinely different from the [non-US-domiciled fund approach](/estate-planning/avoiding-us-estate-tax-brokerage-account/) covered elsewhere in this category, which works through a relatively straightforward, well-established mechanism (fund domicile determining situs). Entity-based planning is more bespoke, more fact-dependent, and more prone to being implemented in a way that doesn't hold up.

---

## The General Logic Behind Why This Can Work

The reasoning behind entity-based structures typically runs like this: if a non-resident alien owns shares in a properly structured foreign corporation, and that foreign corporation - not the individual - owns the US stocks directly, the individual's estate holds shares in a foreign corporation (generally not a US-situs asset) rather than US stocks directly (which are US-situs). The US estate tax exposure, in principle, shifts from the underlying US stocks to the foreign corporate shares, which sit outside the US estate tax net.

**Why this is genuinely more complicated than it sounds:**

- **The structure must be respected as a genuine, properly operating entity**, not merely a formality layered on top of what is functionally still direct personal ownership - tax authorities scrutinize structures that lack real substance.
- **Ongoing compliance costs are real** - proper corporate formalities, potentially separate accounting and filing obligations, and the entity's own operating costs.
- **Your home country's treatment of the structure matters just as much as the US side.** Some countries have their own anti-avoidance rules for foreign corporate or trust structures, potentially creating home-country tax consequences (such as controlled foreign corporation rules) that offset or exceed the US estate tax benefit.
- **US income tax treatment can change too.** Depending on the structure, holding US stocks through certain entities can affect the dividend withholding treatment and treaty eligibility covered throughout this site's [tax guides](/taxes/) - not always favorably.

---

## Trusts: A Related but Distinct Question

Trust structures for holding US assets raise related but distinct considerations from corporate entities - the trust's classification (as a grantor trust versus a non-grantor trust, for US tax purposes) significantly affects how it's treated, and getting this classification wrong can produce the opposite of the intended result. This is genuinely specialized territory requiring an attorney who works specifically in cross-border trust and estate planning, not general estate planning experience alone.

---

## When This Is Worth Pursuing vs When It Isn't

**Worth seriously considering:** for an investor with substantial US-situs assets - well beyond the $60,000 exemption, where the potential estate tax savings clearly justify the structure's setup and ongoing costs - and who is prepared to maintain genuine operational substance in the structure, not just paperwork.

**Generally not worth pursuing:** for an investor whose US-situs exposure is modest, where the [non-US-domiciled fund approach](/estate-planning/avoiding-us-estate-tax-brokerage-account/) achieves most of the practical benefit with far less complexity and cost, or for an investor unwilling or unable to maintain the structure's ongoing compliance requirements properly.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Do not implement an LLC, trust, or foreign corporate structure for US stock holdings without a cross-border estate planning attorney's involvement - this is not a self-directed project
- [ ] Confirm the structure will be respected as having genuine substance, not merely a formality that a tax authority could disregard
- [ ] Model your home country's treatment of the structure (including any controlled foreign corporation or similar anti-avoidance rules) alongside the US-side estate tax analysis
- [ ] Weigh the structure's real ongoing costs against your actual US-situs exposure - for modest exposure, simpler approaches like non-US-domiciled funds are often more cost-effective
- [ ] Confirm the structure doesn't inadvertently worsen your income tax treatment (dividend withholding, treaty eligibility) while attempting to solve the estate tax question

---

## Bottom Line

Entity structures can genuinely reduce US estate tax exposure when properly designed and maintained, but this is meaningfully more complex, costly, and fact-dependent than the more straightforward non-US-domiciled fund approach covered elsewhere in this category. This is worth pursuing seriously only for investors with substantial US-situs exposure who are prepared to engage proper cross-border legal counsel and maintain the structure's genuine operational substance - not a general recommendation for the typical international investor covered throughout this site.
