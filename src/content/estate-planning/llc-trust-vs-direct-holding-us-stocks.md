Once an international investor understands the [$60,000 estate tax exemption](/estate-planning/us-estate-tax-non-resident-aliens-60000-exemption/), it's natural to ask whether holding US stocks through an entity - an LLC, a trust, a foreign corporation - could sidestep the exposure entirely. Sometimes it genuinely can. It's also one of the areas of cross-border planning most prone to being implemented incorrectly, creating real complexity and cost without actually achieving the intended tax result - and this guide spends more time on what can go wrong than on the theoretical benefit, because the failure modes are where most of the practical risk actually sits.

---

## Why This Isn't a Simple Yes-or-No Question

**The core issue: the entity structure itself has to be designed correctly to change the estate tax analysis, and a poorly designed structure can fail to achieve the intended result while still adding real cost and complexity.** This is genuinely different from the [non-US-domiciled fund approach](/estate-planning/avoiding-us-estate-tax-brokerage-account/) covered elsewhere in this category, which works through a relatively straightforward, well-established mechanism (fund domicile determining situs). Entity-based planning is more bespoke, more fact-dependent, and more prone to being implemented in a way that doesn't hold up under scrutiny.

---

## The General Logic Behind Why This Can Work

The reasoning behind entity-based structures typically runs like this: if a non-resident alien owns shares in a properly structured foreign corporation, and that foreign corporation - not the individual - owns the US stocks directly, the individual's estate holds shares in a foreign corporation (generally not a US-situs asset) rather than US stocks directly (which are US-situs). The US estate tax exposure, in principle, shifts from the underlying US stocks to the foreign corporate shares, which sit outside the US estate tax net.

**Why this is genuinely more complicated than it sounds:**

- **The structure must be respected as a genuine, properly operating entity**, not merely a formality layered on top of what is functionally still direct personal ownership - tax authorities scrutinize structures that lack real substance, and a "substance-over-form" analysis can disregard a structure that exists on paper but functions as if the individual still directly controls and personally benefits from the assets without any genuine separation.
- **Ongoing compliance costs are real** - proper corporate formalities (separate bank accounts, formal decision-making processes, genuine governance), potentially separate accounting and filing obligations in the entity's jurisdiction, and the entity's own operating costs, none of which disappear once the structure is initially set up.
- **Your home country's treatment of the structure matters just as much as the US side.** Some countries have their own anti-avoidance rules for foreign corporate or trust structures - controlled foreign corporation (CFC) rules being the most common example - potentially creating home-country tax consequences that offset or exceed the US estate tax benefit, sometimes by a wide margin.
- **US income tax treatment can change too.** Depending on the structure, holding US stocks through certain entities can affect the dividend withholding treatment and treaty eligibility covered throughout this site's [tax guides](/taxes/) - not always favorably. A structure that successfully avoids US estate tax exposure but simultaneously loses access to a favorable treaty withholding rate on dividends may end up a net loss depending on the specific numbers involved.

---

## A Concrete Illustration of How This Can Go Wrong

**Consider an investor who sets up a foreign holding company specifically to own their US brokerage account, hoping to shift the estate tax situs as described above - but continues to personally direct every trade, use the account's funds for personal expenses without formal loan or dividend documentation, and never holds a formal board meeting or maintains separate corporate records.** In a substance-over-form challenge, this structure is genuinely vulnerable to being disregarded for exactly the tax purpose it was created to serve - the IRS (or a court reviewing the estate's tax position after the fact) can conclude that the corporate form was never respected in practice, and treat the individual as the true owner of the underlying US assets all along, defeating the intended estate tax benefit entirely, while the investor has still paid for the setup and ongoing maintenance of a structure that ultimately provided no protection.

**This is not a hypothetical concern specific to this guide** - it reflects a well-established general principle in tax law that a structure's tax treatment depends on how it actually operates, not merely on the paperwork establishing it, and it's exactly why this guide repeatedly emphasizes genuine operational substance rather than treating entity formation as a one-time paperwork exercise.

---

## Trusts: A Related but Distinct Question

Trust structures for holding US assets raise related but distinct considerations from corporate entities - the trust's classification (as a grantor trust versus a non-grantor trust, for US tax purposes) significantly affects how it's treated, and getting this classification wrong can produce the opposite of the intended result. A grantor trust, for instance, is generally treated as if the grantor still owns the underlying assets directly for many US tax purposes - potentially defeating an estate-tax-motivated structure that assumed the trust itself would be respected as the owner. This is genuinely specialized territory requiring an attorney who works specifically in cross-border trust and estate planning, not general estate planning experience alone, given how easily the wrong trust structure can produce the opposite of the desired outcome.

## When This Is Worth Pursuing vs When It Isn't

**Worth seriously considering:** for an investor with substantial US-situs assets - well beyond the $60,000 exemption, where the potential estate tax savings clearly justify the structure's setup and ongoing costs - and who is prepared to maintain genuine operational substance in the structure, not just paperwork, indefinitely for as long as the structure remains in place.

**Generally not worth pursuing:** for an investor whose US-situs exposure is modest, where the [non-US-domiciled fund approach](/estate-planning/avoiding-us-estate-tax-brokerage-account/) achieves most of the practical benefit with far less complexity and cost, or for an investor unwilling or unable to maintain the structure's ongoing compliance requirements properly - which describes a genuinely large share of this site's readership, for whom the simpler mitigation approaches covered elsewhere in this category are the more appropriate starting point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much US-situs exposure justifies considering an entity structure specifically?**
There's no universal threshold - the right answer depends on the specific structure's setup and ongoing costs (which vary by jurisdiction and complexity) relative to the estate tax exposure being addressed, a cost-benefit calculation that requires input from a cross-border estate planning attorney familiar with current costs in the relevant jurisdictions, not a general rule of thumb.

**Can I set up an entity structure myself using a general online formation service?**
This guide strongly cautions against it - the entire value of an entity structure for this specific purpose depends on correct design and ongoing operational substance, both of which require specialized cross-border tax and legal expertise well beyond what a general-purpose entity formation service provides.

**Does an entity structure eliminate the need to think about US-situs fund domicile choice (covered in the avoidance strategies guide)?**
Not necessarily - the two approaches can be complementary rather than mutually exclusive, depending on your specific situation; an investor might use non-US-domiciled funds for a portion of their portfolio while considering an entity structure only for a specific, larger concentrated position where the entity's cost is more clearly justified.

**If I already have an entity structure set up, how do I know if it's actually working as intended?**
This requires a periodic review by a cross-border estate planning attorney to confirm the structure continues to be operated with genuine substance and that no changes in your home country's or the US's tax rules have undermined its intended effect - not a one-time setup-and-forget arrangement.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Do not implement an LLC, trust, or foreign corporate structure for US stock holdings without a cross-border estate planning attorney's involvement - this is not a self-directed project
- [ ] Confirm the structure will be respected as having genuine substance, not merely a formality that a tax authority could disregard
- [ ] Model your home country's treatment of the structure (including any controlled foreign corporation or similar anti-avoidance rules) alongside the US-side estate tax analysis
- [ ] Weigh the structure's real ongoing costs against your actual US-situs exposure - for modest exposure, simpler approaches like non-US-domiciled funds are often more cost-effective
- [ ] Confirm the structure doesn't inadvertently worsen your income tax treatment (dividend withholding, treaty eligibility) while attempting to solve the estate tax question
- [ ] If already using an entity structure, schedule periodic professional review to confirm it continues to operate with genuine substance

---

## Bottom Line

Entity structures can genuinely reduce US estate tax exposure when properly designed and maintained, but this is meaningfully more complex, costly, and fact-dependent than the more straightforward non-US-domiciled fund approach covered elsewhere in this category - and a structure lacking genuine operational substance can fail entirely while still incurring its full cost. This is worth pursuing seriously only for investors with substantial US-situs exposure who are prepared to engage proper cross-border legal counsel and maintain the structure's genuine operational substance indefinitely - not a general recommendation for the typical international investor covered throughout this site.
