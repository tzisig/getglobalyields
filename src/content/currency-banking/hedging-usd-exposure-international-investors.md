Buying a US-listed stock or ETF with non-USD money creates two separate exposures stacked on top of each other: the return of the underlying investment, and the movement of the dollar against your home currency. Most individual investors only think about the first one. The honest answer on the second is that, for most long-term buy-and-hold investors, actively hedging it usually isn't worth the cost and complexity - but the reasoning matters more than the conclusion.

---

## What "Unhedged" Actually Means for Your Returns

If you buy a US stock that rises 10% in USD terms, but the dollar simultaneously weakens 5% against your home currency, your actual return in home-currency terms is closer to 5%, not 10% - the currency movement partially offset the investment gain. The reverse is also true: a weak year for the stock combined with dollar strength can produce a home-currency return better than the USD-denominated number suggests.

**This isn't a flaw in international investing - it's an unavoidable feature of holding assets denominated in a currency other than your own**, whether or not you think about it explicitly.

---

## The Tools That Exist to Hedge It

**Currency-hedged ETF share classes** exist for many popular US funds, using forward contracts or currency swaps internally to neutralize the currency movement, so the fund's return in your home currency tracks the underlying US return more closely. **Direct hedging** through forward contracts or currency futures is also available to more sophisticated investors, though with meaningfully more complexity than simply choosing a hedged fund share class.

**Both approaches carry a real, ongoing cost** - hedging isn't free, and the cost scales with the currency pair's volatility and interest rate differential between the two currencies, a cost that compounds over a long holding period the same way any other recurring fee does.

---

## Why Most Long-Term Investors Skip It

**Over long holding periods, currency movements tend to be less consequential relative to the underlying investment's total return than they appear over shorter periods.** A currency that strengthens or weakens 10% over a single year can easily reverse over a five- or ten-year holding period, while the ongoing cost of hedging is paid continuously regardless of which direction the currency eventually moves - the same asymmetry that makes market-timing generally unrewarding also tends to apply to currency-timing via hedging.

**The practical implication:** for a long-term, buy-and-hold international investor, unhedged direct exposure is the more common and often more reasonable default, with hedging costs simply eating into returns without a reliable offsetting benefit over sufficiently long horizons.

---

## When Hedging Genuinely Makes More Sense

**A known, specific future need in your home currency** changes the calculation meaningfully - an investor planning to withdraw a specific sum in a specific home currency within a known, relatively near-term window (the kind of situation covered in our [exit-strategy guide](/strategies/exit-strategy-life-events-leveraged-etf/)) has a genuine reason to reduce currency uncertainty as that date approaches, similar to how the same investor would reduce market-risk exposure ahead of a known need.

**Extremely volatile currency pairs** - where your home currency has a history of large, rapid swings against the dollar - can also shift the calculation, since the potential downside from an adverse currency move is larger than in a typical developed-market currency pair.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that unhedged USD exposure is a normal, unavoidable feature of holding US-listed assets from abroad, not something to solve by default
- [ ] Recognize that hedging carries a real, ongoing cost that compounds over long holding periods
- [ ] Consider hedging specifically ahead of a known, near-term need to convert back to your home currency, rather than as a standing default
- [ ] Factor in your home currency's historical volatility against the dollar when deciding whether the default (unhedged) approach fits your situation

---

## Bottom Line

Every international investor holding US-listed assets carries USD currency exposure, whether or not they've thought about it directly. For most long-term, buy-and-hold investors, the honest answer is that the ongoing cost of hedging that exposure outweighs the benefit over a sufficiently long horizon - hedging earns its cost mainly when a specific, known future need in your home currency is approaching, not as a permanent default.
