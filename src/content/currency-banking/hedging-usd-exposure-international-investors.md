Buying a US-listed stock or ETF with non-USD money creates two separate exposures stacked on top of each other: the return of the underlying investment, and the movement of the dollar against your home currency. Most individual investors only think about the first one. The honest answer on the second is that, for most long-term buy-and-hold investors, actively hedging it usually isn't worth the cost and complexity - but the reasoning matters more than the conclusion, and the reasoning changes depending on your specific currency pair and time horizon.

---

## What "Unhedged" Actually Means for Your Returns

If you buy a US stock that rises 10% in USD terms, but the dollar simultaneously weakens 5% against your home currency, your actual return in home-currency terms is closer to 5%, not 10% - the currency movement partially offset the investment gain. The reverse is also true: a weak year for the stock combined with dollar strength can produce a home-currency return better than the USD-denominated number suggests.

**Worked example:** a Canadian investor buys $10,000 of a US ETF. Over the following year, the ETF rises 12% in USD terms, to $11,200. If the Canadian dollar simultaneously strengthens 4% against the USD over the same period, the CAD value of that $11,200 position is worth about 4% less in CAD terms than the raw 12% USD gain would suggest - the investor's actual CAD-denominated return lands closer to 7.5%, not 12%. Nothing "went wrong" with the investment; the currency simply moved in the opposite direction of the equity gain, eating into it.

**This isn't a flaw in international investing - it's an unavoidable feature of holding assets denominated in a currency other than your own**, whether or not you think about it explicitly. Every unhedged cross-border position carries this second, independent variable.

## How Currency Hedging Actually Works, Mechanically

**Currency-hedged ETF share classes** exist for many popular US and global funds. Internally, the fund manager enters into forward contracts or currency swaps - agreements to exchange a set amount of one currency for another at a predetermined rate on a future date - and rolls these contracts forward on a regular schedule (often monthly). The effect is that the fund's return in your home currency tracks the underlying investment's USD (or local-currency) return more closely, with the currency component substantially neutralized.

**Direct hedging** through forward contracts or currency futures is also available to more sophisticated investors managing their own hedge outside of a fund structure, though this involves meaningfully more complexity, margin requirements, and active management than simply choosing a currency-hedged fund share class - not a practical option for most buy-and-hold individual investors.

**The cost of hedging is driven primarily by the interest rate differential between the two currencies**, a relationship known as covered interest rate parity. When your home currency's interest rates are lower than USD rates, hedging tends to cost more, because the forward contract has to compensate for the rate gap; when your home currency's rates are higher than USD rates, hedging can occasionally be closer to free or even generate a small positive carry. This rate differential moves over time as central banks adjust policy, so the cost of hedging a given currency pair isn't fixed - it shifts with the broader interest rate environment, and it's worth checking the current differential for your specific currency pair rather than assuming a flat cost.

## Why Most Long-Term Investors Skip It

**Over long holding periods, currency movements tend to be less consequential relative to the underlying investment's total return than they appear over shorter periods.** A currency that strengthens or weakens 10% over a single year can easily reverse over a five- or ten-year holding period - major developed-market currency pairs have historically moved through multi-year cycles in both directions relative to the dollar, rather than trending permanently one way. Meanwhile, the ongoing cost of hedging is paid continuously regardless of which direction the currency eventually moves, the same asymmetry that makes short-term market-timing generally unrewarding also tends to apply to currency-timing via hedging.

**The practical implication:** for a long-term, buy-and-hold international investor, unhedged direct exposure is the more common and often more reasonable default, with hedging costs simply eating into returns without a reliable offsetting benefit over sufficiently long horizons. This is the same logic, applied to currency risk, that this site generally applies to market risk elsewhere - time in the market, rather than tactical positioning, tends to do more of the work.

## When Hedging Genuinely Makes More Sense

**A known, specific future need in your home currency** changes the calculation meaningfully. An investor planning to withdraw a specific sum in a specific home currency within a known, relatively near-term window - the kind of situation covered in our [exit-strategy guide](/strategies/exit-strategy-life-events-leveraged-etf/) - has a genuine reason to reduce currency uncertainty as that date approaches, similar to how the same investor would reduce market-risk exposure ahead of a known need. A retiree planning to convert a fixed USD sum to home currency in eighteen months to fund a home purchase is in a fundamentally different position than an investor accumulating for a horizon measured in decades.

**Currency pairs with a history of large, rapid swings against the dollar** can also shift the calculation. Emerging-market currencies - the South African rand and Turkish lira are commonly cited examples - have historically shown meaningfully larger and faster moves against the dollar than pairs like EUR/USD or GBP/USD, driven by factors including local inflation differentials, political risk, and capital flow volatility. An investor whose home currency falls into this more volatile category faces a genuinely larger potential downside from an adverse currency move than an investor holding a typical developed-market currency, which can tip the cost-benefit calculation of hedging even for a longer time horizon.

**A concentrated position representing a large share of net worth** is a third case worth naming separately - an investor with the large majority of their wealth in a single unhedged cross-border position has more at stake from a large currency swing than an investor for whom the position is a smaller piece of a broader, more diversified financial life.

## What Hedging Doesn't Protect You From

**A currency-hedged fund still carries the full market risk of the underlying investment** - hedging neutralizes the currency component specifically, not the risk that the stock or ETF itself falls in value. This distinction sounds obvious stated directly, but it's a genuine source of confusion: some investors reach for a hedged share class expecting broader risk reduction than it actually provides.

**Hedging also doesn't eliminate tracking error entirely** - the rolling forward contracts used internally by hedged funds introduce their own small, ongoing basis risk and cost, meaning a hedged fund's home-currency return won't track the underlying USD return with perfect precision even when the hedge is working as intended.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I hedge only part of my position rather than all of it?**
Yes - partial hedging (hedging, for instance, half of a position) is a legitimate middle-ground approach some investors use to reduce currency-risk exposure without fully committing to the ongoing cost of hedging the entire position, particularly for an investor with a partial near-term need and a partial long-term holding period within the same position.

**Do currency-hedged ETFs cost meaningfully more in expense ratio than their unhedged equivalents?**
Currency-hedged share classes typically do carry a somewhat higher expense ratio than the unhedged version of the same underlying fund, reflecting the ongoing cost of maintaining the forward-contract hedge - this is in addition to, not instead of, the interest-rate-differential cost discussed above, so the two costs should be considered together when evaluating a hedged fund's total cost.

**If I already hold an unhedged position and want to start hedging now, is there a cost to switching?**
Switching from an unhedged fund to a hedged share class (or vice versa) typically involves selling the current position and buying the alternative, which can trigger a taxable event depending on your situation - covered by the rules in our [taxes category](/taxes/) - separate from the hedging cost itself. This transition cost is worth weighing against the expected benefit before switching an existing position.

**Does hedging make sense for a leveraged position like TQQQ specifically?**
Given how much market risk already dominates the return profile of a leveraged position - covered in our [currency risk vs market risk guide](/currency-banking/currency-risk-vs-market-risk-international-investors/) - the case for hedging currency risk specifically on a leveraged position is generally weaker than for an unleveraged one, since the currency component is a proportionally smaller driver of the total outcome either way.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that unhedged USD exposure is a normal, unavoidable feature of holding US-listed assets from abroad, not something to solve by default
- [ ] Check the current interest rate differential between your home currency and the USD, since it directly drives the ongoing cost of hedging
- [ ] Recognize that hedging carries a real, ongoing cost that compounds over long holding periods and generally isn't worth it for long-horizon buy-and-hold positions
- [ ] Consider hedging specifically ahead of a known, near-term need to convert back to your home currency, rather than as a standing default
- [ ] Factor in your home currency's historical volatility against the dollar - emerging-market currencies carry a meaningfully different risk profile than developed-market pairs
- [ ] Remember that a hedged fund still carries full market risk on the underlying investment - hedging addresses currency risk specifically, not overall investment risk

---

## Bottom Line

Every international investor holding US-listed assets carries USD currency exposure, whether or not they've thought about it directly. For most long-term, buy-and-hold investors, the honest answer is that the ongoing cost of hedging that exposure - driven by the interest rate differential between currencies - outweighs the benefit over a sufficiently long horizon. Hedging earns its cost mainly when a specific, known future need in your home currency is approaching, or when your home currency carries meaningfully higher volatility than a typical developed-market pair, not as a permanent default for a long-horizon portfolio.
