An investor who converts home currency to USD only when buying, then converts proceeds back to home currency on every sale, pays the FX spread twice per round trip. An investor who holds a standing USD cash buffer in their brokerage account - funded periodically rather than per-trade - pays that spread far less often. The trade-off is real currency exposure on the buffer itself, not a free lunch.

---

## The Repeat-Conversion Problem

Every currency conversion carries a cost, whether an explicit fee or an embedded spread below the interbank mid-market rate - the mechanics covered in detail in our [multi-currency accounts guide](/us-investing/multi-currency-investment-accounts/). An investor converting home currency to USD for every individual purchase, and back again on every sale, pays that cost on every single transaction rather than periodically.

**The practical fix:** convert a lump sum to USD periodically (say, quarterly or when making a planned contribution), hold it as USD cash in the brokerage account, and draw from that buffer for individual trades without a fresh conversion each time.

---

## What Holding USD Cash Actually Costs You

**A USD cash buffer isn't free.** Holding it means your uninvested cash carries currency risk relative to your home currency for as long as it sits there - if your home currency strengthens against the dollar while the cash sits uninvested, you lose purchasing power on that portion, independent of anything happening in the US stock market.

**This is a genuinely different risk than the conversion-cost problem it solves.** Reducing conversion frequency trades a small, certain, repeated cost (the FX spread) for a larger, uncertain, one-directional risk (currency movement on the buffer while it sits uninvested). Whether that trade is worth it depends on how long the cash actually sits idle and how large the buffer is relative to your total position.

---

## Cash Yield Changes the Calculation

**Some brokers pay meaningful interest on uninvested USD cash balances** - the specific rates and thresholds are covered in our [multi-currency accounts guide](/us-investing/multi-currency-investment-accounts/) for IBKR specifically. A USD buffer earning a competitive money-market-equivalent yield while it waits to be deployed is a genuinely different proposition than idle cash earning nothing, which meaningfully changes whether holding the buffer makes sense versus converting only when a specific trade is ready to execute.

---

## A Practical Framework

**Short idle periods (days to a few weeks) between funding and deploying capital:** the currency-risk cost of holding USD cash briefly is generally small relative to the conversion-cost savings from batching - lean toward holding the buffer.

**Long idle periods (months) where you're not sure when you'll deploy the cash:** the currency-risk exposure accumulates the longer cash sits uninvested, and the case for holding a large, indefinite USD buffer weakens - consider converting closer to when you actually plan to invest instead.

**Regular, planned contributions (e.g., monthly investing):** convert on a predictable schedule matched to your contribution cadence rather than either extreme, so the buffer never grows large or sits idle for long.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Batch currency conversions into periodic lump sums rather than converting on every individual trade
- [ ] Confirm whether your broker pays meaningful interest on uninvested USD cash before deciding how large a buffer to hold
- [ ] Match your conversion cadence to your actual investing cadence, rather than holding an indefinitely large, idle USD balance
- [ ] Recognize that a USD cash buffer carries genuine currency risk relative to your home currency for as long as it sits uninvested

---

## Bottom Line

Holding a USD cash buffer reduces how often you pay the FX conversion spread, but it isn't free - the buffer itself carries currency exposure for as long as it sits uninvested, partially offset by whatever interest your broker pays on the balance. Matching your conversion cadence to your actual investing timeline, rather than defaulting to either per-trade conversion or an indefinitely large standing buffer, is the more useful framing than treating either extreme as automatically correct.
