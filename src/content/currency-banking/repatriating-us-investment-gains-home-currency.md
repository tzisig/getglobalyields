Withdrawing proceeds from a US brokerage account and converting them back to your home currency involves largely the same FX-cost mechanics covered throughout this category for funding an account - but a few considerations are specific to the withdrawal direction, worth checking before you initiate a large repatriation.

---

## Same FX Mechanics, Reverse Direction

**The core lesson from this site's funding-side content applies directly in reverse:** minimize the number of conversions, use a transparent, competitive-rate service rather than accepting your broker's or bank's default conversion, and avoid the double-conversion trap covered in our [double conversion guide](/currency-banking/avoiding-double-currency-conversion-fees/) - which can occur just as easily on withdrawal as on funding if the money passes through more institutions than necessary.

**The Wise-to-IBKR path** covered in our [multi-currency accounts guide](/us-investing/multi-currency-investment-accounts/) generally works in both directions - withdrawing USD from IBKR to Wise, then converting and sending to your home bank, follows the same low-cost logic as funding.

---

## Timing a Large Withdrawal

**A large, one-time repatriation (closing a position for a major purchase, retirement, or relocation) is a genuinely different situation than routine periodic conversions**, and the exchange-rate-timing skepticism covered in our [timing guide](/currency-banking/exchange-rate-timing-effective-returns/) still applies - but a large lump-sum conversion is also a moment where the hedging considerations from our [hedging guide](/currency-banking/hedging-usd-exposure-international-investors/) become more relevant, given the size and finality of a single conversion versus routine periodic ones.

**Splitting a very large repatriation into several tranches over a period of weeks or months**, rather than converting the entire amount in a single transaction, can reduce the risk of converting the whole sum at a single unfavorable moment - a form of dollar-cost averaging applied to the withdrawal side, mirroring the same logic covered in our [exchange rate timing guide](/currency-banking/exchange-rate-timing-effective-returns/) for funding.

---

## Tax Considerations Are Separate From the FX Decision

**The tax consequences of selling a US position - capital gains treatment, any US withholding, and your home country's own tax treatment of the proceeds** - are governed by the rules covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) and are entirely separate from the currency-conversion decision. Sell and tax-reporting decisions should be made on their own merits; the currency-conversion method chosen afterward doesn't change the underlying tax treatment of the sale itself.

---

## Reporting and Regulatory Considerations

**Some countries require reporting large incoming international transfers**, and some banks apply additional scrutiny or delays to large, unusual-pattern deposits - worth confirming with your home bank before initiating a large repatriation, so an unexpectedly large transfer doesn't trigger delays or compliance holds you weren't anticipating.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Apply the same low-conversion-count, transparent-rate principles to withdrawals that apply to funding
- [ ] Consider splitting a very large, one-time repatriation into tranches rather than converting the full amount in a single transaction
- [ ] Keep the tax treatment of the underlying sale entirely separate from the currency-conversion method used afterward
- [ ] Check with your home bank in advance about any reporting requirements or holds on large incoming international transfers

---

## Bottom Line

Repatriating US investment proceeds follows largely the same cost-minimization logic as funding an account, applied in reverse - use a transparent, low-cost conversion path, and be deliberate about a large one-time conversion rather than treating it identically to routine periodic transfers. Keep the currency-conversion decision separate from the tax treatment of the underlying sale, which is governed by entirely different rules.
