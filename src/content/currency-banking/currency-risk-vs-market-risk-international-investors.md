An international investor's home-currency return on a US stock is the product of two genuinely independent variables: how the investment itself performed in USD, and how the dollar moved against their home currency over the same period. Treating these as one combined number - "my portfolio was up 8% this year" - obscures which of the two actually drove the result, and can lead to drawing the wrong lesson from your own returns.

---

## Two Independent Sources of Return

**Market risk** is the risk that the underlying investment - a stock, an ETF, a leveraged fund like TQQQ - moves against you in USD terms, driven by company performance, sector trends, interest rates, and broad market sentiment. This is the risk most investment content, including most of the rest of this site, focuses on directly.

**Currency risk** is the separate risk that your home currency's value relative to the USD shifts, changing what your USD-denominated gains (or losses) are actually worth once converted back - covered in more detail in our [USD hedging guide](/currency-banking/hedging-usd-exposure-international-investors/). These two risks are driven by almost entirely different factors and can move in the same direction, opposite directions, or independently of each other in any given period.

---

## Why Conflating Them Leads to Bad Conclusions

**An investor who sees a strong home-currency return might wrongly conclude their stock-picking or timing was skillful, when a meaningful share of that return actually came from dollar strength unrelated to the investment itself** - and the reverse mistake is just as common: blaming a disappointing home-currency return entirely on the investment, when currency weakness against the dollar was a real contributor.

**The practical fix is separating the two components when reviewing your own performance:** what was the USD return of the investment itself, and separately, what was the currency movement over the same period? Only the first number reflects anything about the investment's actual performance or your decision to hold it.

---

## Why This Matters for Portfolio Decisions

**Decisions about the investment** - whether to hold, sell, or add to a position - should generally be based on the investment's own merits and your own strategy, not on recent currency movements that happen to be flattering or unflattering your recent home-currency returns.

**Decisions about currency exposure** - whether to hedge, as covered in our [hedging guide](/currency-banking/hedging-usd-exposure-international-investors/), or how much USD cash to hold, as covered in our [USD cash strategy guide](/currency-banking/usd-cash-holding-strategy-international-investors/) - are a genuinely separate set of decisions, worth making deliberately rather than as an emotional reaction to a currency-driven swing in your recent reported returns.

---

## A Practical Habit

**When reviewing your portfolio's performance, check both numbers separately** - your brokerage statement typically shows USD-denominated gains directly, and you can calculate the currency component by comparing the exchange rate at the time of your original investment to the current rate. This two-number habit, rather than a single blended home-currency percentage, gives a clearer picture of what's actually driving your results.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Separate your investment's USD return from the currency movement when reviewing your own portfolio performance
- [ ] Avoid attributing currency-driven gains or losses to investment skill or timing
- [ ] Make investment decisions (hold, sell, add) based on the investment's own merits, not recent currency swings
- [ ] Make currency decisions (hedge, cash buffer sizing) as their own deliberate choice, separate from investment decisions

---

## Bottom Line

Currency risk and market risk are genuinely independent forces acting on an international investor's returns, and conflating them into a single blended number obscures what's actually happening. Separating the two - tracking the investment's own USD performance apart from the currency movement - gives a clearer, more useful picture for making both investment and currency decisions deliberately rather than reactively.
