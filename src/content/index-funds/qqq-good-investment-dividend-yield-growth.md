"Is QQQ a good investment" is really three different questions wearing one search query: is it a good place to put money at all, is it a good core holding to build a portfolio around, and is it a good source of income. The first two have real, evidence-based answers. The third one has a clear answer too, and it's not the one the fund's popularity might suggest.

---

## The Yield Number Everyone Sees First

QQQ's trailing dividend yield sits around 0.42-0.45% - low enough that an investor comparing funds on yield alone would likely rank it near the bottom of any broad-market list, well behind a typical S&P 500 fund's roughly 1% yield or a dividend-focused fund's 3%-plus. Judged purely as an income vehicle, QQQ is not competitive, and it was never built to be: financial companies and consumer staples, the sectors that typically pay the highest dividends, are structurally excluded or underweighted from the Nasdaq-100 by the index's own composition rules.

---

## The Number That Actually Matters More: Growth Rate

**QQQ's dividend has grown at roughly a 10.6% annualized rate over the trailing 10 years, and closer to 13% annualized over the trailing 5 years** - a meaningfully faster compounding rate than the dividend growth most broad-market or high-current-yield funds produce. This is the number that matters for a long-horizon holder using dividend reinvestment rather than someone drawing income today: a smaller yield compounding at a double-digit annual growth rate can, over a long enough holding period, produce a larger income stream on the original invested capital than a larger yield that grows slowly or not at all.

**Why the growth rate runs this high:** it's a direct reflection of the underlying companies' earnings growth. Firms in the Nasdaq-100 that pay dividends at all - a growing share of the index's largest constituents now do - tend to be raising them off a low, recently-initiated base, which produces higher percentage growth than a mature dividend payer raising an already-substantial payout by a modest amount each year. This isn't a guarantee the growth rate continues at the same pace going forward - a 10%-plus annual compounding rate applied indefinitely eventually outpaces what any company's actual earnings growth could support - but it does explain why judging QQQ purely on today's yield understates what a long-term holder has actually been receiving.

---

## Why the Fund-Level Number Hides Very Different Companies

The 0.42-0.45% figure is a blended average, and it obscures just how differently QQQ's largest holdings actually treat dividends. Among the fund's biggest constituents: Microsoft pays more in total dividend dollars than any other S&P 500 company and has raised its payout on a regular annual schedule for years. Apple pays a real, if modest, dividend - $1.08 per share annually as of its most recent declaration, a roughly 0.35% yield on its own, with the board raising the per-share payout by around 4% at its last increase. Alphabet only began paying a dividend at all in the past couple of years. Meta introduced its first-ever dividend in early 2024, a genuinely new development for a company that spent its first decade as a public company returning nothing to shareholders directly. NVIDIA, despite being one of the largest companies in the index by weight, pays a token dividend - a cent per share, working out to a yield near 0.02%, functionally irrelevant to any income calculation. Amazon pays no dividend at all.

**This spread matters for understanding where QQQ's blended yield actually comes from.** It isn't that every constituent pays a small amount - it's that a handful of the fund's largest positions by weight (Amazon, NVIDIA) contribute close to nothing, while others (Microsoft, Apple) contribute a real, if modest, amount, and a few of the largest names only entered the dividend-paying picture within the past couple of years. As more of the index's mega-caps mature from pure-growth into the "cash-generative enough to start paying something" phase Meta and Alphabet have both recently entered, the fund-level yield has room to keep climbing from here even without any change to the underlying index composition - which is part of what's driving the double-digit dividend growth rate covered above, and a trend worth watching rather than assuming has already fully played out.

## What You're Actually Paying For

The Nasdaq-100's price-to-earnings ratio sits around 33 as of early August 2026, meaningfully above the S&P 500's roughly 22-23. That gap is the market pricing in the same growth expectation that produces QQQ's above-average dividend growth rate - investors are paying more per dollar of current earnings because they expect those earnings to grow faster than the broad market's, not as an accident of tech-sector enthusiasm alone.

**Judged against its own history rather than against SPY, today's multiple isn't as extreme as the headline number might suggest.** The index's 5-year average P/E has been around 30.4, putting the current 33 modestly above that recent average but still inside one standard deviation of it. Against the longer-run historical range - roughly 11.6 to 38.8, with a median closer to 23 - today's valuation sits well above the long-term median but comfortably short of the top of the historical range. That's a genuinely different read than "at an all-time-high valuation," even though 33 is objectively a rich multiple in absolute terms.

**This valuation gap cuts both ways regardless of where it sits historically.** If the underlying companies keep growing earnings fast enough to justify the higher multiple, the premium is earned. If growth disappoints, a higher-multiple index has further to fall on a valuation reset than a lower-multiple one does, independent of what the underlying businesses actually do operationally.

## Why QQQ Reacts Harder to Interest Rate Moves

Growth-oriented companies - the kind that dominate the Nasdaq-100 - are valued heavily on earnings expected several years into the future, not primarily on what they're generating today. That matters for a specific, well-understood reason: valuation models discount future earnings back to a present value using a rate tied to prevailing interest rates, and the further out those earnings sit, the more a rising discount rate shrinks their present value. A mature, dividend-heavy company generating most of its value from current cash flow doesn't feel that same effect nearly as strongly. This is why QQQ, and growth-heavy indexes generally, tend to move more sharply than the broad market on days the market reprices interest rate expectations - not because the underlying businesses changed, but because the math used to value distant future earnings is directly sensitive to the discount rate applied to them.

---

## The Reinvestment Math Runs on the After-Withholding Amount

For an international investor planning to reinvest QQQ's dividends rather than draw them as income, one detail is easy to overlook: reinvestment happens on whatever amount is left after withholding, not the gross dividend declared. A non-resident alien facing the standard 30% default withholding, or a lower treaty rate depending on country of residence - covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - reinvests a smaller amount than a US-taxable investor holding the identical shares would. Over a long compounding horizon, that gap compounds too, in the same direction as - though smaller in magnitude than - the expense ratio drag covered in other comparisons on this site. It doesn't change the conclusion that QQQ's dividend growth rate is real and meaningful; it does mean the actual dollar amount being reinvested each quarter is smaller than the fund's stated per-share dividend once your specific withholding rate is applied, worth factoring into any long-run projection rather than assuming the full declared dividend compounds.

## So Is It a Good Investment

**For a long-horizon investor building a core growth position and comfortable with the volatility that comes with it** - covered in more concrete terms in our [QQQ concentration risk guide](/index-funds/qqq-top-10-holdings-concentration-risk/) - QQQ has a real, defensible case: exposure to companies with genuinely above-average earnings and dividend growth, at the cost of a richer valuation and sharper rate sensitivity than the broad market carries.

**For an investor specifically looking for current income**, QQQ is a poor tool for that job regardless of how the growth story is framed. A dividend yield under 0.5% doesn't become a reasonable income source because the growth rate is attractive - it means meaningful income from this position, if it ever arrives, is years away, not now.

**For an investor who wants "the market" as a single core holding** without a deliberate tilt toward growth and away from financials, energy, and other sectors QQQ structurally excludes, SPY - covered in our [QQQ vs SPY comparison](/index-funds/qqq-vs-spy-nasdaq-100-sp500-comparison/) - remains the more accurate single-fund answer to that specific goal.

---

## Bottom Line

QQQ's low current yield is real and isn't going to turn into a competitive income stream any time soon - but the dividend growth rate behind that small number, running in the 10-13% annualized range over the past decade, tells a genuinely different story than the yield alone. Whether that trade - faster-growing but smaller income, a richer valuation, and sharper interest-rate sensitivity, in exchange for exposure to the Nasdaq-100's earnings growth - is a good deal depends entirely on whether you're investing for income now or growth over a long horizon, not on a single yes-or-no answer to "is QQQ a good investment."
