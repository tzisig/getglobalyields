QQQ and SPY get lumped together as "the two big US index ETFs," as if the only real question is which one has performed better lately. That framing skips the actual decision. SPY holds 504 companies across every sector of the US economy. QQQ holds 100 - and by rule, none of them are financial companies. One of these is a diversified market position. The other is a concentrated bet that happens to be wrapped in the same index-fund packaging, and the difference shows up clearly once you look at the real holdings numbers instead of the ticker symbols.

---

## What Each Index Actually Includes

**SPY tracks the S&P 500** - 504 large-cap US companies (the count runs slightly above 500 because some constituents have multiple share classes), selected by a committee for size, liquidity, and profitability, spanning all eleven GICS sectors from technology to utilities to real estate.

**QQQ tracks the Nasdaq-100** - the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange. That "non-financial" rule isn't a minor footnote; it's a structural exclusion written into the index methodology. No banks, no insurers, no asset managers, regardless of size. Berkshire Hathaway, JPMorgan, Visa - none of them can appear in QQQ no matter how large they get, because the index rules exclude the entire sector by design.

That single rule is most of what separates these two funds. SPY's job is to reflect the broad US economy. QQQ's job is to reflect the largest non-financial companies trading on one specific exchange - which today means it's a fund built almost entirely from technology, semiconductor, and internet-platform companies, not because someone chose a tech theme, but because that's who's left once financials are removed from the largest-100 list.

Almost 46 cents of every QQQ dollar sits in ten companies. For SPY, spread across 504 holdings instead of 100, that same top-10 slice is closer to 38 cents - lower, but not dramatically lower than you'd expect from "the diversified one." SPY is still meaningfully concentrated at the top; QQQ is just more concentrated, on a smaller base of names to begin with.

## The Overlap Nobody Points Out

Look closely at those two tables and something becomes obvious: nine of QQQ's top 10 companies also appear in SPY's top 10. Apple, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon, both Alphabet share classes, Broadcom, Meta, and Micron show up on both lists. The only names that don't overlap are AMD (in QQQ's top 10, not SPY's) and JPMorgan (in SPY's top 10, not QQQ's, because JPMorgan is a bank and therefore ineligible for QQQ entirely).

This matters for anyone assuming that holding both funds "diversifies" a portfolio. It doesn't, not at the top. An investor who splits money evenly between QQQ and SPY isn't really holding two different baskets - they're holding one heavily-overlapping basket of mega-cap tech names, plus SPY's additional 494 non-overlapping holdings layered on top. The diversification SPY adds over QQQ comes entirely from what's outside the top 10, not from the top 10 itself.

QQQ doesn't publish an equally clean sector table in the same format, and third-party data providers don't agree closely on the exact number - estimates for QQQ's technology-sector weight range from roughly the high-50s to mid-60s percent, depending on which classification system a given provider uses and how they bucket companies like Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon (all of which sit in different GICS sectors from "Information Technology" despite being commonly thought of as tech companies). Rather than pick one disputed figure and present it as precise, the more reliable statement is the structural one already covered above: QQQ has zero financial-sector exposure by rule, and its remaining weight is dominated by technology, communication services, and consumer discretionary names - the same handful of sectors that also dominate its top 10 holdings.

## Cost and Yield

**QQQ's expense ratio is 0.20%.** SPY's is 0.0945% - roughly half. Neither is expensive by any historical standard, and the gap between them is small enough that cost alone isn't a strong reason to prefer one over the other, unlike the QQQ-vs-QQQM decision covered in our [QQQ vs QQQM vs VGT comparison](/strategies/qqq-vs-qqqm-vs-vgt-comparison/), where the entire point of choosing QQQM is capturing an identical index at a lower fee.

**The yield gap is the more interesting number.** SPY's trailing 12-month distribution yield runs around 0.98%. QQQ's trailing yield is roughly 0.42% - less than half. This isn't a fund-management difference; it's a direct consequence of what each index holds. Financial companies and consumer staples - sectors QQQ excludes or underweights - tend to pay meaningfully higher dividends than semiconductor and software companies, several of which (NVIDIA and Meta among the larger examples) pay small or no dividends at all. QQQ's low yield isn't a flaw in the fund. It's what a "no financials, heavy growth-tech" index looks like once you measure its income output instead of its price.

## What Each One Has Actually Done in a Crash

QQQ launched in March 1999, just in time for the dot-com bust, and the result is the single most important data point for anyone evaluating QQQ's real downside. From its 2000 peak, QQQ fell somewhere in the 80-83% range depending on the exact source and measurement window - and it did not recover to that prior peak until sometime after 2014, more than a decade later. SPY, holding a broader, less speculative-growth-weighted basket during the same period, fell hard too, but nowhere near as far or for as long.

Longer-run volatility measurements from portfolio-analytics providers consistently put QQQ meaningfully higher-risk than SPY on a standard-deviation basis - commonly cited figures run in the low-20s percent annualized for QQQ against roughly 15% for SPY, though exact numbers vary by provider and measurement period, so treat the specific figures as directional rather than a fixed constant. The pattern is consistent even where the precise numbers aren't: a 100-company, financial-free, tech-heavy index swings harder in both directions than a 500-company index built to represent the whole market.

## Dividend Withholding: No Difference Here

Both QQQ and SPY are US-domiciled funds, and the dividend withholding tax a non-US investor pays is identical for either one - governed entirely by your country of residence's treaty rate (or the 30% default without one) and your W-8BEN status, not by which specific fund you hold. This is covered in full in our [main dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/). Given QQQ's much lower yield, the absolute tax dollars withheld on a QQQ position will typically be smaller than on an equal-sized SPY position simply because there's less dividend income to withhold from - not because either fund gets preferential tax treatment.

## Which One Fits, and the Case for Both

**If you want exposure to the broad US economy** - financials, healthcare, energy, real estate, and everything else alongside technology - SPY is structurally built for that. QQQ cannot give you that exposure; the financial-sector exclusion alone makes it a fundamentally different bet, not a more concentrated version of the same one.

**If you specifically want maximum exposure to large-cap technology and growth names**, and you understand and accept that this means zero financials, a smaller basket of 100 companies, and a demonstrated history of much deeper, much longer drawdowns than the broad market, QQQ delivers that in a way SPY - with financials still making up over 12% of the fund - doesn't.

**If you're holding both because you want "more diversification,"** revisit that assumption given the top-10 overlap above. A blended QQQ-and-SPY portfolio is closer to "SPY, with extra weight added on top of its existing mega-cap tech names" than it is to two genuinely distinct exposures. That can still be a reasonable choice - deliberately overweighting mega-cap tech relative to the S&P 500's own weighting is a real, definable strategy - but it's worth choosing on purpose rather than assuming the two tickers cancel out into something more balanced than either one alone.

## QQQ vs SPY in a Paragraph

QQQ and SPY aren't competing versions of the same trade - they're different bets wearing similar index-fund packaging. SPY spreads 504 holdings across every sector of the US economy, financials included. QQQ concentrates 100 non-financial companies, with just ten of them accounting for nearly half the fund, nine of which already show up in SPY's own top 10. The real decision isn't cost, since both are cheap - it's whether you want the broad economy or a concentrated, financial-free bet on a much smaller set of large-cap names, and whether you understand that combining both funds adds less genuine diversification than the two separate ticker symbols suggest.
