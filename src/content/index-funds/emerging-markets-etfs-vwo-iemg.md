VWO (Vanguard FTSE Emerging Markets ETF) and IEMG (iShares Core MSCI Emerging Markets ETF) both provide broad exposure to developing economies - China, India, Brazil, Taiwan, and dozens of others - through a single US-listed fund. For most of this site's readers, the interesting question isn't which of the two funds to pick; it's whether emerging markets exposure means something genuinely different depending on where you actually live, a question that doesn't come up in the standard, US-resident-oriented version of this comparison.

---

## What Both Funds Hold

VWO and IEMG track similar, broad emerging markets indices, with meaningful overlap in top holdings - heavily weighted toward Chinese, Indian, and Taiwanese companies in most recent index compositions, reflecting the relative size of those economies' public equity markets within the broader emerging markets universe - and comparably low expense ratios for this category of fund. The differences between the two - slightly different index providers (FTSE vs MSCI) leading to minor country inclusion differences, most notably in how each classifies South Korea (treated as an emerging market by one major index provider and a developed market by another, historically a genuine point of divergence between similar emerging-markets fund families) - are genuinely secondary to the larger question below.

---

## The Genuinely Different Question for This Site's Readers

**For a US resident, emerging markets ETFs represent adding exposure to economies entirely disconnected from their own financial life** - genuine diversification into markets with different growth drivers, currencies, and economic cycles than their home market, adding a dimension of exposure they don't otherwise have.

**If your own home country is itself classified as an emerging market** - a description that applies to a meaningful share of this site's international readership, given the country guides covered throughout our [us-investing category](/us-investing/) spanning Brazil, India, Indonesia, and many others - **buying an emerging markets ETF raises a genuinely different question: are you diversifying, or are you adding exposure that correlates more closely with your existing home-country economic risk than a US-focused portfolio would?**

**This is not automatically a reason to avoid emerging markets ETFs** - a broad emerging markets fund still diversifies across many countries, not concentrated in any single one the way your home-country exposure already is through your income, home value, and daily economic life. But it's worth genuinely considering whether adding a fund heavily weighted toward, say, China or India provides real diversification value for an investor already living in an emerging economy, versus simply adding a different flavor of the same broad risk category your home-country life already carries by default.

---

## A More Useful Framing: Check Specific Country Weightings

**Check the specific countries a given emerging markets fund is weighted toward, relative to your own home country**, rather than treating "emerging markets" as a single undifferentiated category with uniform risk characteristics. A Brazilian investor adding an emerging markets fund heavily weighted toward Chinese and Indian holdings is adding genuinely different specific-country exposure than their home market, even though both fall under the broad "emerging markets" label - this is a meaningfully different situation than adding a fund that happens to include significant exposure to your own home country's market specifically.

**A worked illustration:** an Indian investor considering VWO or IEMG should check what percentage of the fund's weighting is allocated to India specifically. If Indian equities represent a meaningful double-digit percentage of the fund (a realistic figure given India's substantial and growing weight within major emerging markets indices), a portion of what looks like "international diversification" on the label is actually reinvesting in the investor's own home market through a US-listed wrapper - not wrong or bad, but a materially different thing than what the fund's name might suggest to someone assuming "emerging markets" automatically means "somewhere else."

**"Emerging markets" as a category also spans genuinely different economic profiles** - a large, increasingly developed economy like China or India is a structurally different investment than a smaller, more commodity-dependent emerging economy, even though both are grouped under the same broad classification for index purposes. Treating the category as internally uniform obscures more than it reveals.

## Practical Considerations

**Volatility and currency risk** in emerging markets ETFs tend to run higher than developed-market equivalents like [VXUS](/index-funds/vxus-international-diversification/) or a pure US fund, reflecting the underlying economies' own volatility, less mature capital markets, and generally more volatile currency dynamics relative to major developed-market currencies.

**Tax withholding** on the underlying non-US dividends follows each individual country's own rules, not a single unified rate - a considerably more fragmented picture than the straightforward US treaty framework covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/), and one that's genuinely difficult to summarize in a single figure given how many different countries' withholding regimes are blended together within a single fund.

**Political and regulatory risk** is a more prominent factor in emerging markets than in developed markets - policy changes, capital controls, and regulatory shifts in specific emerging economies can affect fund performance in ways that are less common (though not absent) in developed-market investing, worth factoring into position sizing rather than treating emerging markets risk as simply "more of the same" risk category as developed-market equities.

**For EU/UK investors**, the same PRIIPs restriction applies to US-domiciled emerging markets funds as to any other US-domiciled ETF, requiring a UCITS equivalent, covered in more detail in our [UCITS vs US-domiciled comparison](/index-funds/ucits-vs-us-domiciled-etfs-tax-comparison/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there a way to get emerging markets exposure while specifically excluding my own home country?**
Some providers offer single-country-excluded or more customized regional emerging markets funds, though availability is more limited than the broad, all-emerging-markets funds covered in this guide - worth researching specifically if excluding your home country's weighting is a genuine priority for your diversification goals.

**How much of a typical global equity portfolio is usually allocated to emerging markets?**
There's no universal figure - allocations in globally diversified portfolios vary based on the specific investor's risk tolerance and diversification philosophy, and for this site's readers specifically, the right figure depends heavily on the home-country consideration covered throughout this guide, not a number that can be quoted independent of your own situation.

**Do VWO and IEMG include frontier markets (economies even less developed than standard emerging markets)?**
Generally no - frontier markets are typically classified and offered as a separate, distinct fund category, not included within standard broad emerging markets funds like VWO or IEMG, which focus on the more established tier of developing economies.

**Does the classification of a country as "emerging" versus "developed" ever change?**
Yes - index providers periodically reclassify countries between developed, emerging, and frontier status as economies mature (South Korea's differing classification between major providers, mentioned above, is a live example of this kind of divergence), meaning a fund's country composition isn't permanently fixed and can shift as classifications are periodically reviewed.

## Bottom Line

VWO and IEMG are broadly similar, low-cost tools for emerging markets exposure - the more interesting question for many of this site's readers isn't which fund to choose, but whether "emerging markets diversification" means something different when your own home country is itself part of that broad category. Checking a specific fund's actual country weightings against your own situation gives a more useful answer than treating emerging markets as a single, undifferentiated diversification checkbox, and recognizing the internal diversity within "emerging markets" as a category sharpens that analysis further.
