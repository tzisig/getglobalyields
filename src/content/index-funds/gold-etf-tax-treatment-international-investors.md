GLD and IAU - the two most widely held physical gold ETFs - are structured as grantor trusts holding actual gold bars, the same structural category covered in our [crypto ETF tax guide](/taxes/crypto-etf-tax-treatment-international-investors/) for spot Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs. The parallel is genuinely useful: the same reasoning, and the same honest uncertainty about non-US treatment, applies here.

---

## The Grantor Trust Structure, Again

Like the spot crypto ETFs covered elsewhere on this site, GLD and IAU are grantor trusts, not Regulated Investment Companies. The IRS treats each shareholder as directly owning a proportional share of the trust's actual gold holdings, rather than shares in a taxable entity that distributes income. When the trust sells a small amount of gold to cover its expense ratio, shareholders are treated as having sold their proportional share - the same "phantom sale" mechanic covered in our crypto ETF guide.

---

## The US Taxpayer Rule: Collectibles, Not Standard Capital Gains

**For US taxpayers, gold ETF gains are taxed as collectibles - at a maximum federal rate of 28%, notably higher than the standard 15%/20% long-term capital gains rate that applies to ordinary stock ETFs.** This is a specific, well-documented rule that catches many US investors off guard, since most tax software defaults to the standard capital gains rate unless the sale is manually flagged as a collectible.

---

## What This Means for a Non-US Investor - By Reasoned Extension, Not Confirmed Guidance

**This is where this guide needs to be as careful as our crypto ETF coverage.** The 28% collectibles rate is a specific US domestic tax rate - it applies within the framework of US taxpayer capital gains taxation, a framework that doesn't apply to non-resident aliens in the first place, who are generally not subject to US capital gains tax on portfolio investments at all (the standard rule covered throughout this site's [main dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/)).

**The reasoned extension:** since gold, like Bitcoin, is personal property, and non-resident aliens are generally not taxed by the US on capital gains from personal property (absent effectively connected income or the 183-day presence test), the same logic that suggests spot crypto ETF gains are generally untaxed for NRAs would extend to gold ETF gains as well - including the phantom-sale events from the trust's fee-related gold sales.

**What this guide cannot confirm with the same confidence as the rest of this site's tax content:** whether this specific analogy has been definitively tested for gold ETF grantor trusts and NRA status, the same honest caveat applied in our crypto ETF coverage. This is less novel territory than spot crypto ETFs (gold ETFs have existed since 2004, giving more time for the analysis to be tested in practice), but this guide still cannot state the NRA treatment as definitively settled without a specific citation confirming it.

---

## Practical Guidance

**Treat the "generally not taxed for NRAs" conclusion as a reasoned starting point**, consistent with the general capital gains treatment covered throughout this site, but confirm with a cross-border tax advisor before assuming it with the same confidence as the well-established equity capital gains rules.

**Your home country's own tax treatment of gold ETF gains is a separate question entirely**, and some countries have their own specific rules for precious metals or collectibles that differ from their standard capital gains treatment - confirm this separately.

Gold ETFs like GLD and IAU share the same grantor trust structure - and the same category of genuine, honest uncertainty about specific non-resident alien treatment - as the spot crypto ETFs covered elsewhere on this site.
