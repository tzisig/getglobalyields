GLD and IAU - the two most widely held physical gold ETFs - are structured as grantor trusts holding actual, physical gold bars in vaults, the same structural category covered in our [crypto ETF tax guide](/taxes/crypto-etf-tax-treatment-international-investors/) for spot Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs. The parallel is genuinely useful: the same underlying reasoning, and the same honest uncertainty about non-US treatment, applies to both - which makes this article as much about explaining the reasoning process as about stating a conclusion.

---

## The Grantor Trust Structure, Again

Like the spot crypto ETFs covered elsewhere on this site, GLD and IAU are grantor trusts, not Regulated Investment Companies (the standard structure used by most equity and bond ETFs). The IRS treats each shareholder as directly owning a proportional share of the trust's actual gold holdings, rather than shares in a taxable entity that distributes income the way a standard RIC-structured fund does. When the trust sells a small amount of gold to cover its expense ratio - a routine, ongoing operational necessity for any fund with a management fee - shareholders are treated as having sold their own proportional share of that gold, the same "phantom sale" mechanic covered in more mechanical detail in our crypto ETF guide.

**Why this structural distinction matters at all:** a standard equity ETF's internal trading and fee mechanics generally don't create individual taxable events for shareholders the way a grantor trust's do - the grantor trust structure was originally adopted for commodity-backed funds like gold ETFs specifically because of how commodities (as opposed to securities) were treated under existing fund regulation at the time these products were first designed, not as a tax-optimization choice aimed at investors.

---

## The US Taxpayer Rule: Collectibles, Not Standard Capital Gains

**For US taxpayers, gold ETF gains are taxed as collectibles - at a maximum federal rate of 28%, notably higher than the standard 15%/20% long-term capital gains rate that applies to ordinary stock ETFs.** This is a specific, well-documented rule under US tax law treating gains from precious metals - including through a grantor-trust ETF structure like GLD or IAU - the same way gains from physical collectibles (art, antiques, and similar) are treated, rather than the way standard investment securities are treated. This catches many US investors off guard, since most tax software defaults to the standard capital gains rate unless the sale is manually flagged as a collectible-category holding.

**Why gold gets this treatment at all:** historically, US tax law has treated gains on tangible collectible property - physical gold, art, coins, antiques - differently from gains on financial securities, reflecting a policy distinction embedded in the tax code well before gold ETFs existed as a product. When gold ETFs were structured to hold physical gold directly (rather than, say, gold mining company stock, which would be an ordinary equity security), they inherited this collectibles classification by virtue of what they actually hold.

## What This Means for a Non-US Investor - By Reasoned Extension, Not Confirmed Guidance

**This is where this guide needs to be as careful as our crypto ETF coverage, and for the same underlying reason.** The 28% collectibles rate is a specific US domestic tax rate - it applies within the framework of US taxpayer capital gains taxation, a framework that doesn't apply to non-resident aliens in the first place, who are generally not subject to US capital gains tax on portfolio investments at all (the standard rule covered throughout this site's [main dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/), rooted in IRC §871(a)(2) and the general absence of US capital gains taxation for NRAs absent effectively connected income or the 183-day physical presence test).

**The reasoned extension:** since gold, like Bitcoin, is personal property, and non-resident aliens are generally not taxed by the US on capital gains from personal property, the same logic that suggests spot crypto ETF gains are generally untaxed for NRAs would extend to gold ETF gains as well - including the phantom-sale events from the trust's fee-related gold sales, which would be treated the same as any other personal-property capital gain for NRA purposes under this reasoning.

**Why this is a reasoned extension and not settled law, stated plainly:** the specific interaction between (a) the collectibles tax-rate rule (which is explicitly a US-taxpayer-specific provision) and (b) the general NRA capital gains exemption (which applies broadly to personal property) hasn't been laid out in a single, explicit piece of guidance addressing gold ETF grantor trusts and NRA status directly. The reasoning connects two well-established but separate principles by analogy - a sound analytical approach, but genuinely different from citing a specific ruling that addresses this exact combination of fund structure and taxpayer status.

**What this guide can say with somewhat more confidence than our crypto ETF coverage:** gold ETFs have existed since 2004, giving over two decades of practical operating history for this structure - meaningfully longer than spot crypto ETFs' much shorter track record - which increases the practical likelihood that this interaction has been considered and applied consistently in practice by tax professionals working with NRA clients, even without a single definitive public ruling this guide can point to directly.

## Practical Guidance

**Treat the "generally not taxed for NRAs" conclusion as a reasoned starting point**, consistent with the general capital gains treatment covered throughout this site, but confirm with a cross-border tax advisor before assuming it with the same confidence as the well-established equity capital gains rules that this site's other tax content covers more definitively.

**Your home country's own tax treatment of gold ETF gains is a separate question entirely**, and some countries have their own specific rules for precious metals or collectibles that differ from their standard capital gains treatment - some jurisdictions apply a specific, different rate to precious metals gains regardless of how the US treats the position, meaning even a favorable NRA conclusion on the US side doesn't resolve your total tax picture.

**Keep records of your gold ETF cost basis and holding period carefully**, the same discipline recommended for crypto ETF holdings, in case a future clarification or your home country's own reporting requirements require you to reconstruct this history later.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the collectibles classification affect US dividend withholding on gold ETFs the way it affects capital gains?**
Gold ETFs like GLD and IAU generally don't generate ordinary dividend income in the way equity ETFs do, since they hold physical gold rather than dividend-paying securities - there's typically no dividend withholding question to address in the same way as it applies to an equity fund like VOO.

**Is there a meaningful difference between GLD and IAU for tax purposes?**
No - both are structured as grantor trusts holding physical gold, and the tax analysis in this guide applies identically to both funds; the practical differences between them (expense ratio, trading volume, share price) don't affect the tax treatment discussed here.

**Do silver or other precious metals ETFs follow the same reasoning?**
Silver ETFs and other physical-precious-metals-backed funds generally use a similar grantor trust structure and face the same US collectibles classification for US taxpayers, so the same reasoned-extension logic for NRAs would likely apply - though this guide specifically addresses gold and hasn't independently verified the structural details of every other precious metals fund.

**Should I avoid gold ETFs entirely given this uncertainty, and hold physical gold or a different instrument instead?**
This guide isn't recommending against gold ETFs - the uncertainty here is about the specific NRA tax characterization, not about whether gold ETFs are a sound or unsound investment choice, which is a separate question this guide doesn't address. If the tax uncertainty specifically is a concern, discussing alternative structures with a tax advisor familiar with your situation is the appropriate next step, rather than defaulting to avoidance based on this article alone.
