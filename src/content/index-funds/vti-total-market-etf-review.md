VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) tracks the CRSP US Total Market Index - a much broader index than the S&P 500, covering essentially the entire investable US stock market, roughly 3,500-4,000 companies spanning large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, and micro-cap segments, rather than just the 500 largest companies the S&P 500 selects.

---

## What "Total Market" Actually Adds Beyond VOO

The S&P 500, tracked by VOO, SPY, and IVV, represents roughly 80% of total US market capitalization by design - the 500 largest companies, selected and weighted to represent the large-cap segment specifically. **VTI adds the remaining mid-cap, small-cap, and micro-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes entirely**, giving genuinely broader exposure across the full size spectrum of US public companies, including thousands of smaller names an S&P 500 fund never touches.

**In practice, this means:** VTI's performance closely tracks the S&P 500's over most periods, given how much of total market value the large-cap S&P 500 constituents represent - by some measures, the S&P 500 alone captures the large majority of VTI's total weighted exposure. But VTI includes meaningful additional exposure to smaller companies that can behave differently during specific market conditions, since small-cap stocks have historically shown different volatility and cyclicality patterns than large-caps at various points in market cycles - the mechanics covered in more depth in our [small-cap ETF guide](/index-funds/small-cap-etfs-diversification/).

**A useful way to think about the relationship:** VTI isn't really "VOO plus something different" so much as "VOO's holdings, plus a long tail of thousands of additional smaller companies layered on top, at market-cap-appropriate (i.e., small) weights." The overlap in top holdings between the two funds is extensive - the largest companies in VTI are the same largest companies in VOO, at similar relative weights, because market-cap weighting concentrates both funds' exposure in the same mega-cap names.

## Cost and Structure

VTI's expense ratio is very close to VOO's - both are among the lowest-cost broad equity ETFs available from any provider, and the cost difference between them, whatever it currently is, isn't a meaningful factor in choosing between them. This is a genuine choice about index breadth and diversification philosophy, not a cost trade-off, which sets it apart from a comparison like [VOO vs SPY](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/), where cost is actually the deciding factor for most investors.

## VTI vs VOO: The Actual Decision

**Choose VOO (S&P 500) if:** you want exposure specifically to established, large-cap US companies with a long public track record, or if you're running an options overlay strategy where the S&P 500's options liquidity - particularly through SPY specifically, covered in our [VOO vs SPY vs IVV guide](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/) - matters to your strategy. VTI's options market is meaningfully less liquid than SPY's, making it a weaker choice for an options-overlay approach.

**Choose VTI (Total Market) if:** you want the broadest possible single-fund exposure to the US stock market, including small, mid, and micro-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes entirely, and you're not specifically prioritizing options liquidity for this position. VTI is a genuinely more complete answer to "give me the entire US stock market in one fund" than VOO is.

**The practical difference in outcome is smaller than the conceptual difference might suggest.** Given how much of total US market value sits in large-cap companies already included in the S&P 500, VTI and VOO's returns correlate very closely over most periods - this is a genuine but incremental diversification decision at the margin, not a fundamentally different risk profile or return pattern. An investor choosing between the two isn't making a high-stakes decision the way, say, choosing between a broad index fund and a concentrated sector bet would be.

---

## Where the Difference Actually Shows Up

**During periods where small and mid-cap stocks meaningfully outperform or underperform large-caps**, VTI and VOO's returns will diverge by more than in a typical period - an investor holding VTI captures more of a small-cap rally (or more of a small-cap decline) than an investor holding VOO alone, purely as a function of VTI's broader inclusion. Neither direction is inherently "better"; it's simply a different, broader slice of the same overall market.

**During periods of unusually strong mega-cap concentration** - where a small number of very large companies drive a disproportionate share of total market returns, a pattern that has recurred at various points in market history - VTI and VOO's returns tend to converge even more closely than usual, since the mega-cap names dominating both funds' weighted returns are largely the same companies.

## For International Investors Specifically

VTI is a US-domiciled ETF, meaning the same PRIIPs restriction covered throughout this site's European [us-investing guides](/us-investing/) applies - EU/UK investors generally cannot buy VTI directly and would look to a UCITS equivalent instead. Several providers offer total-market or all-world UCITS funds tracking similar broad indices, covered in more detail in our [UCITS vs US-domiciled comparison](/index-funds/ucits-vs-us-domiciled-etfs-tax-comparison/). Investors in countries without this restriction, covered throughout our [us-investing category](/us-investing/), can access VTI directly like any other US-listed ETF, subject to the standard NRA dividend withholding framework covered in our [dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I already hold VTI, does adding VOO on top make sense?**
Generally no - since VOO's holdings are effectively a subset of VTI's largest positions, adding VOO to an existing VTI position simply overweights the large-cap segment relative to VTI's natural total-market proportions, rather than adding genuinely new diversification. This can be a deliberate tilt if intentional (similar to the value/growth tilt logic covered in our [value vs growth ETF guide](/index-funds/value-vs-growth-etfs-compared/)), but isn't a diversification improvement on its own.

**Does VTI include any non-US companies?**
No - VTI tracks a US-only index. International diversification requires a separate fund, covered in our [VXUS guide](/index-funds/vxus-international-diversification/), which is commonly paired with VTI in a simple two- or three-fund portfolio structure.

**Is VTI's dividend yield meaningfully different from VOO's?**
The two tend to be fairly close, since both are dominated by the same large-cap holdings that drive most of the aggregate dividend income in either fund - the inclusion of additional smaller companies in VTI has a modest effect on the blended yield, generally not large enough to be a primary decision factor between the two funds.

**Which fund is more commonly recommended as a single core holding in simple portfolio structures?**
Both appear frequently in simple index-portfolio recommendations, including in the classic [3-fund portfolio structure](/index-funds/building-3-fund-portfolio-international-investor/) - VTI is sometimes preferred specifically for its more complete single-fund market coverage, while VOO is sometimes preferred for its slightly more established track record and, in some fund families, marginally lower cost. Neither choice is a clear mistake.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that VTI and VOO's returns correlate closely, given how much of the total market VOO's large-cap holdings already represent
- [ ] Choose VTI specifically if broader small/mid/micro-cap inclusion matters to your diversification goals, not simply as a default "more complete" choice over VOO
- [ ] Recognize that VOO's options market (specifically via SPY) is meaningfully more liquid than VTI's, if an options overlay strategy is part of your plan
- [ ] If in the EU or UK, confirm PRIIPs applies to VTI (it does, as a US-domiciled fund) and look to a UCITS equivalent instead
- [ ] Do not treat the VTI-vs-VOO decision as cost-driven, since their expense ratios are typically very close
- [ ] Avoid holding both VTI and VOO simultaneously unless deliberately tilting toward large-cap on top of your total-market base

---

## Bottom Line

VTI offers genuinely broader US market exposure than VOO by including small, mid, and micro-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes entirely, at a very similar cost - a real but incremental diversification choice rather than a fundamentally different investment, given how much overlap exists in each fund's largest holdings. Either is a reasonable, low-cost core holding; the decision comes down to whether broader size-spectrum inclusion matters to your specific portfolio construction goals, and whether you need the deeper options liquidity that VOO's S&P 500 exposure (via SPY specifically) provides.
