VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) tracks the CRSP US Total Market Index - a much broader index than the S&P 500, covering essentially the entire investable US stock market, roughly 3,500-4,000 companies spanning large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap segments, rather than just the 500 largest.

---

## What "Total Market" Actually Adds Beyond VOO

The S&P 500, tracked by VOO, SPY, and IVV, represents roughly 80% of total US market capitalization by design - the 500 largest companies. **VTI adds the remaining mid-cap and small-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes entirely**, giving genuinely broader exposure across the full size spectrum of US public companies.

**In practice, this means:** VTI's performance closely tracks the S&P 500's over most periods, given how much of total market value the large-cap S&P 500 constituents represent - but VTI includes meaningful additional exposure to smaller companies that can behave differently during specific market conditions (small-cap stocks have historically shown different volatility and cyclicality patterns than large-caps at various points).

---

## Cost and Structure

VTI's expense ratio is very close to VOO's - both are among the lowest-cost broad equity ETFs available, and the cost difference between them is not a meaningful factor in choosing between them. This is a genuine choice about index breadth, not a cost trade-off.

---

## VTI vs VOO: The Actual Decision

**Choose VOO (S&P 500) if:** you want exposure specifically to established, large-cap US companies, or if you're running an options overlay strategy where the S&P 500's options liquidity (particularly through SPY specifically, covered in our [VOO vs SPY vs IVV guide](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/)) matters to your strategy.

**Choose VTI (Total Market) if:** you want the broadest possible single-fund exposure to the US stock market, including small and mid-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes, and you're not specifically prioritizing options liquidity for this position.

**The practical difference is smaller than it might seem.** Given how much of total US market value sits in large-cap companies already included in the S&P 500, VTI and VOO's returns correlate very closely over most periods - this is a genuine but incremental diversification decision, not a fundamentally different risk profile.

---

## For International Investors Specifically

VTI is a US-domiciled ETF, meaning the same PRIIPs restriction covered throughout this site's European [us-investing guides](/us-investing/) applies - EU/UK investors generally cannot buy VTI directly and would look to a UCITS equivalent instead (several providers offer total-market or all-world UCITS funds tracking similar broad indices). Investors in countries without this restriction, covered throughout our [us-investing category](/us-investing/), can access VTI directly like any other US-listed ETF.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that VTI and VOO's returns correlate closely, given how much of the total market VOO's large-cap holdings already represent
- [ ] Choose VTI specifically if broader small/mid-cap inclusion matters to your diversification goals, not simply as a default "better" choice over VOO
- [ ] If in the EU or UK, confirm PRIIPs applies to VTI (it does, as a US-domiciled fund) and look to a UCITS equivalent instead
- [ ] Do not treat the VTI-vs-VOO decision as cost-driven, since their expense ratios are very close

---

## Bottom Line

VTI offers genuinely broader US market exposure than VOO by including small and mid-cap companies the S&P 500 excludes, at a very similar cost - a real but incremental diversification choice rather than a fundamentally different investment. Either is a reasonable, low-cost core holding; the decision comes down to whether broader size-spectrum inclusion matters to your specific portfolio construction goals.
