VTV (Vanguard Value ETF) and VUG (Vanguard Growth ETF) split the US large-cap market into two roughly complementary halves based on a specific, rules-based methodology - value stocks (generally lower price relative to earnings, book value, and similar fundamentals) versus growth stocks (generally higher-priced relative to those same metrics, reflecting expected future growth). This is a genuine, measurable distinction with real historical performance and risk differences, not simply a stylistic label attached to otherwise similar companies.

---

## What Actually Distinguishes Value from Growth

**Value stocks** are classified based on trading at lower valuations relative to fundamentals like earnings, book value, and sales - historically including sectors like financials, energy, utilities, and industrials more heavily, reflecting the types of companies that tend to trade at more modest valuation multiples relative to their current earnings.

**Growth stocks** are classified based on higher valuations relative to those same fundamentals, generally reflecting the market's expectation of above-average future earnings growth - historically weighted more heavily toward technology and other sectors where investors are willing to pay a premium price today for anticipated future expansion.

**The overlap with this site's core content:** VUG's holdings overlap meaningfully with the mega-cap technology names that also dominate [QQQ](/strategies/qqq-vs-qqqm-vs-vgt-comparison/), given growth's natural tilt toward the same kind of companies. An investor already holding significant QQQ or TQQQ exposure and adding VUG on top may be adding less genuine diversification than the different ticker names suggest - worth checking actual holdings overlap before assuming two different funds provide two different exposures, rather than assuming distinct names mean distinct risk.

---

## The Dividend Yield Difference - A Practical, Not Just Theoretical, Distinction

**Value funds like VTV tend to carry a meaningfully higher dividend yield than growth funds like VUG**, a direct consequence of the underlying company characteristics driving each classification - mature, established companies with more modest growth expectations (the value profile) tend to return more capital to shareholders through dividends, while companies prioritizing reinvestment for future growth (the growth profile) tend to pay out less, or nothing at all, in dividends.

**Why this matters for this site's readers specifically:** an investor prioritizing current income - relevant to the dividend and income-focused strategies covered throughout our [strategies category](/strategies/) - may find VTV's yield profile more aligned with an income goal than VUG's, independent of any broader value-vs-growth performance debate. This is a genuinely practical, not purely theoretical, distinction between the two funds.

## Historical Performance Patterns - and Why They're Not Predictive

Value and growth have historically taken turns outperforming each other across different market cycles - extended periods where value stocks meaningfully outperformed growth, and other extended periods (including much of the 2010s and early 2020s) where growth dominated, driven substantially by the mega-cap technology sector's outsized returns during that stretch. **This cyclicality is real, but it does not mean either style reliably predicts the next cycle** - attempting to time a rotation between value and growth carries the same fundamental timing-difficulty problem covered in our [sector rotation guide](/strategies/sector-rotation-leveraged-etf-investors/), applied to a style tilt rather than a sector one, and subject to the same forecasting difficulty this site applies broadly to market-timing attempts.

**Why the cycles happen at all is worth understanding, even without being able to predict them:** value tends to do relatively better during periods of rising interest rates (which disproportionately pressure growth stocks' valuations, since more of their expected value sits further in the future and is more sensitive to discount-rate changes) and during economic recoveries favoring cyclical sectors. Growth tends to do relatively better during periods of falling or low interest rates and during stretches where a small number of large, fast-growing companies significantly outpace the broader economy. Knowing this mechanism doesn't make the next cycle predictable, but it does explain why the historical pattern exists at all, rather than treating it as arbitrary.

## When Tilting Toward One Makes Sense

**A deliberate value or growth tilt** can make sense as a genuine, informed allocation decision - some investors deliberately overweight value for its historically lower volatility, higher dividend yield, and more moderate valuation multiples; others deliberately overweight growth for its historical outperformance during specific economic conditions and its overlap with the technology-sector exposure they already have conviction in. **This is meaningfully different from a broad market fund like [VOO](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/) or [VTI](/index-funds/vti-total-market-etf-review/)**, which holds both value and growth stocks in their natural market-cap-weighted proportions without a deliberate style tilt either way, reflecting the market's own blend of the two styles.

**A common mistake worth avoiding:** holding a broad market fund alongside a growth-tilted fund like VUG without realizing the combination simply overweights growth relative to the broad market's natural proportions, rather than adding a genuinely new dimension of exposure - a deliberate tilt is fine if intentional, but should be a conscious choice, not an accidental byproduct of combining funds without checking the resulting overall allocation and its cumulative style bias.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do VTV and VUG's expense ratios differ meaningfully from a broad market fund like VOO?**
Style-specific funds like VTV and VUG typically carry a modestly higher expense ratio than the broadest, most heavily competed-on-cost funds, though the gap is generally small in absolute terms - confirm current figures directly with the provider.

**Can I combine VTV and VUG to roughly recreate a broad market fund?**
In principle, yes - combining value and growth in roughly the proportions they exist in the broader market approximates a broad-market fund's style composition, though this requires periodically checking and rebalancing the specific weights, since value and growth's relative market capitalization shifts over time as prices move, unlike a broad fund which rebalances this automatically.

**Does the value-vs-growth distinction apply the same way to non-US markets?**
Value and growth are general investing style concepts that apply to non-US equity markets as well, though this specific comparison (VTV vs VUG) covers US large-cap funds specifically - a separate research question if you're considering a style tilt within your non-US or international equity allocation.

**Is there a meaningful tax difference between VTV and VUG for a non-US investor?**
Both are subject to the same standard NRA dividend withholding framework covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - since VTV's higher yield means more dividend income overall, a non-US investor holding VTV will see more total withholding tax in dollar terms than an equivalent-sized VUG position, purely as a function of VTV distributing more dividend income, not because of any different withholding rate.

## Bottom Line

VTV and VUG represent a genuine, measurable split of the market based on real fundamental differences - including a meaningfully different dividend yield profile, not just a theoretical labeling exercise - and a deliberate tilt toward either can be a reasonable allocation decision. The main things to watch for are unintentional overlap with existing growth-heavy holdings like QQQ or TQQQ, and the temptation to time a rotation between the two styles based on recent performance, which carries the same fundamental difficulty as any other market-timing attempt covered elsewhere on this site.
