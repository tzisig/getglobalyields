VTV (Vanguard Value ETF) and VUG (Vanguard Growth ETF) split the US large-cap market into two roughly complementary halves based on a specific methodology - value stocks (generally lower price relative to earnings, book value, and similar fundamentals) versus growth stocks (generally higher-priced relative to those same metrics, reflecting expected future growth). This is a genuine, measurable distinction with real historical performance and risk differences, not simply a stylistic label.

---

## What Actually Distinguishes Value from Growth

**Value stocks** are classified based on trading at lower valuations relative to fundamentals like earnings, book value, and sales - historically including sectors like financials, energy, and industrials more heavily.

**Growth stocks** are classified based on higher valuations relative to those same fundamentals, generally reflecting the market's expectation of above-average future earnings growth - historically weighted more heavily toward technology and other high-growth sectors.

**The overlap with this site's core content:** VUG's holdings overlap meaningfully with the mega-cap technology names that also dominate [QQQ](/strategies/qqq-vs-qqqm-vs-vgt-comparison/), given growth's natural tilt toward the same kind of companies. An investor already holding significant QQQ or TQQQ exposure and adding VUG on top may be adding less genuine diversification than the different ticker names suggest - worth checking actual holdings overlap before assuming two different funds provide two different exposures.

---

## Historical Performance Patterns - and Why They're Not Predictive

Value and growth have historically taken turns outperforming each other across different market cycles - extended periods where value stocks meaningfully outperformed growth, and other extended periods (including much of the 2010s and early 2020s) where growth dominated. **This cyclicality is real, but it does not mean either style reliably predicts the next cycle** - attempting to time a rotation between value and growth carries the same fundamental timing-difficulty problem covered in our [sector rotation guide](/strategies/sector-rotation-leveraged-etf-investors/), applied to a style tilt rather than a sector one.

---

## When Tilting Toward One Makes Sense

**A deliberate value or growth tilt** can make sense as a genuine, informed allocation decision - some investors deliberately overweight value for its historically lower volatility and dividend characteristics, others deliberately overweight growth for its historical outperformance during specific economic conditions. **This is meaningfully different from a broad market fund like [VOO](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/) or [VTI](/index-funds/vti-total-market-etf-review/)**, which holds both value and growth stocks in their natural market-cap-weighted proportions without a deliberate style tilt either way.

**A common mistake worth avoiding:** holding a broad market fund alongside a growth-tilted fund like VUG without realizing the combination simply overweights growth relative to the broad market's natural proportions - a deliberate tilt is fine if intentional, but should be a conscious choice, not an accidental byproduct of combining funds without checking the resulting overall allocation.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Check actual holdings overlap between VUG and any QQQ/TQQQ position you already hold, since the overlap can be more significant than the different fund names suggest
- [ ] Treat a value or growth tilt as a deliberate allocation decision, not a default addition to a broad-market core
- [ ] Understand the historical cyclicality between value and growth outperformance does not make either style reliably predictable going forward
- [ ] If combining a broad market fund with a style-tilted fund, calculate your resulting overall style exposure rather than assuming the combination is neutral

---

## Bottom Line

VTV and VUG represent a genuine, measurable split of the market based on real fundamental differences, not just a labeling exercise - and a deliberate tilt toward either can be a reasonable allocation decision. The main things to watch for are unintentional overlap with existing growth-heavy holdings like QQQ or TQQQ, and the temptation to time a rotation between the two styles based on recent performance, which carries the same fundamental difficulty as any other market-timing attempt.
