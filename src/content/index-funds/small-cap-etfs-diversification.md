Small-cap ETFs like IJR (iShares Core S&P Small-Cap ETF) and VB (Vanguard Small-Cap ETF) hold hundreds of smaller US companies genuinely excluded from mega-cap-focused funds like [QQQ](/strategies/qqq-vs-qqqm-vs-vgt-comparison/) and largely underrepresented even in broad-market funds like [VOO](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/), given how heavily market-cap weighting concentrates those funds' actual exposure in the largest names despite technically including thousands of companies.

---

## Why Small-Caps Are Genuinely Different Exposure

**Market-cap-weighted funds concentrate heavily in the largest companies by design.** Even a fund technically including small-cap stocks, like [VTI](/index-funds/vti-total-market-etf-review/), allocates a small fraction of total weight to them relative to mega-cap names, since market-cap weighting means larger companies dominate the fund's actual exposure regardless of how many smaller companies are nominally included in the underlying index. A dedicated small-cap fund like IJR or VB is the only way to get meaningful, deliberate exposure to this segment specifically, rather than a token allocation buried within a broader fund's mega-cap-dominated weighting.

**The practical implication:** an investor holding QQQ, TQQQ, or even a broad fund like VOO is getting essentially no small-cap exposure, regardless of how "diversified" those funds might otherwise feel - genuine small-cap exposure requires a dedicated allocation to a fund specifically designed to capture this segment.

---

## The Risk Profile Is Genuinely Different

**Small-cap stocks carry higher volatility than large-cap stocks**, generally reflecting less analyst coverage (meaning prices can be slower to reflect new information, and can move more sharply once they do), less trading liquidity, more sensitivity to economic cycles and credit conditions (smaller companies often have less financial flexibility during downturns), and less-established business models compared to mega-cap companies with decades of operating history. This isn't a reason to avoid small-caps entirely, but it's a genuinely different risk profile than the large-cap-dominated core holdings covered throughout the rest of this category - not simply a smaller-dollar-value version of the same underlying risk.

**Small-cap companies also tend to carry a higher proportion of unprofitable or early-stage businesses** than large-cap indices, which have generally already been through a survivorship process filtering out weaker companies over time. This adds a dispersion-of-outcomes dimension to small-cap investing that a large-cap fund doesn't carry to the same degree - within a small-cap index, individual company outcomes vary more widely, even though the fund as a whole diversifies across hundreds of names.

---

## The Historical Size Premium - and Why It's Not Guaranteed

**Small-cap stocks have, over long historical periods, shown a tendency to outperform large-cap stocks** - a documented pattern sometimes called the "size premium," part of the broader factor-investing literature that also covers value, momentum, and other systematic return patterns studied in academic finance. **This premium has been genuinely inconsistent in more recent periods**, with extended stretches where large-caps (particularly mega-cap technology names) have significantly outperformed small-caps instead, sometimes for long enough that entire investing careers have unfolded without the historical size premium reasserting itself. Treat the historical size premium as a documented pattern worth knowing about, not a reliable, guaranteed return driver to plan around with confidence in any specific timeframe.

---

## A Different Dividend and Withholding Tax Profile

**Small-cap companies, as a group, tend to pay less in dividends than large-cap companies** - many smaller, growth-focused companies reinvest available capital into the business rather than returning it to shareholders, a pattern that shows up in a small-cap fund's blended dividend yield running lower than a comparable large-cap fund's. For a non-US investor, this has a direct, practical consequence: a small-cap-heavy allocation will generally generate less dividend income - and therefore less US withholding tax in dollar terms - than an equivalent-sized large-cap allocation, even though the standard NRA withholding framework covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) applies identically to both.

**This isn't a reason to choose small-cap funds for tax purposes specifically** - the withholding difference is a byproduct of the underlying companies' dividend policies, not a deliberate tax feature - but it's worth understanding as part of the full economic picture when comparing a small-cap allocation against a large-cap or dividend-focused alternative.

---

## IJR vs VB: A Minor Distinction

IJR and VB both track broad small-cap indices with similar overall characteristics - the specific index methodology differs slightly between them (IJR uses S&P's small-cap index, VB uses a CRSP-based methodology), which can produce modest differences in exactly which companies are included and at what weights, but the practical difference between the two for most investors is smaller than the difference between either fund and a large-cap-focused alternative.

---

## Practical Guidance

**A deliberate, sized small-cap allocation** can add genuine diversification value to a portfolio otherwise concentrated in large-cap names, given how different this segment's risk and return drivers are from the mega-cap-dominated core covered throughout most of this site's index-fund content.

**Sizing matters** - given small-cap's higher volatility, a large allocation carries meaningfully more risk than the same percentage in a broad large-cap fund, worth factoring into your overall risk budget rather than treating it as a straightforward, risk-neutral substitute for large-cap exposure. A common approach among investors who do add small-cap exposure deliberately is capping it at a modest minority of the total equity allocation, similar in spirit to the sizing discipline covered in our [sector ETF guide](/index-funds/sector-etfs-explained/) for sector tilts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does small-cap exposure add meaningful diversification if I already hold VTI, which technically includes small-caps?**
Some, but less than a dedicated allocation - VTI's small-cap weighting is present but proportionally small relative to its large-cap holdings, given market-cap weighting's concentration effect. An investor wanting a genuinely deliberate, meaningful small-cap tilt beyond VTI's default proportions would need a dedicated allocation like IJR or VB layered on top.

**Are small-cap ETFs more expensive than large-cap equivalents?**
Generally comparable for the broadest, most established small-cap index funds like IJR and VB - both are competitively priced relative to the broader index-fund market, though confirm current expense ratios directly with each provider.

**Is small-cap value a meaningfully different category from broad small-cap exposure?**
Yes - some providers offer small-cap value-specific funds, combining the size factor covered in this guide with the value factor covered in our [value vs growth guide](/index-funds/value-vs-growth-etfs-compared/). Academic factor research has historically found some evidence for a combined small-cap-value premium, though the same forward-looking uncertainty applies as to either factor in isolation.

**How does small-cap exposure interact with international small-caps, or is this US-only?**
IJR and VB specifically hold US small-cap companies; international small-cap exposure would require a separate fund, distinct from the US-focused funds covered in this guide and from the broad international exposure covered in our [VXUS guide](/index-funds/vxus-international-diversification/), which is itself weighted more toward larger international companies.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Recognize that broad market funds like VOO or VTI provide minimal genuine small-cap exposure, given market-cap weighting's concentration in large companies
- [ ] Size a dedicated small-cap allocation deliberately, accounting for its higher volatility relative to large-cap exposure
- [ ] Understand that small-cap funds generally generate less dividend income (and therefore less withholding tax) than large-cap funds, as a byproduct of underlying company dividend policy
- [ ] Treat the historical size premium as a documented pattern, not a guaranteed future return driver
- [ ] Choose between IJR and VB based on minor index methodology preference rather than expecting a meaningful practical difference

---

## Bottom Line

Small-cap ETFs like IJR and VB provide genuinely different market exposure than the large-cap-dominated funds covered throughout the rest of this category - a real diversification tool, not a redundant addition to an already-diversified-feeling portfolio. The trade-off is real, higher volatility, a somewhat different dividend and withholding profile, and a historical outperformance pattern that has been inconsistent enough in recent years to treat as a documented tendency rather than a dependable planning assumption.
