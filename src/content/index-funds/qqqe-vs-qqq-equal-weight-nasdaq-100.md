QQQE gets pitched as "QQQ without the concentration problem" - same 100 companies, but every one gets roughly equal weight instead of the market-cap-driven scheme covered in our [QQQ top 10 holdings and concentration guide](/index-funds/qqq-top-10-holdings-concentration-risk/), where a handful of mega-caps drive most of the fund. That framing is directionally correct, but the specific numbers usually cited to support it - QQQE's much smaller historical drawdown, in particular - don't hold up as cleanly as they look.

---

## What Equal-Weighting Actually Does to the Holdings

The mechanic itself is simple: instead of Apple's weight being determined by its market capitalization relative to the other 99 companies, every constituent gets set to roughly 1% at each quarterly rebalance, then drifts with price until the next reset. Using the real QQQ weights confirmed in our concentration guide, the effect is concrete: Apple's 8.15% weight in QQQ gets reset to roughly 1% in an equal-weight version - an eight-fold reduction. NVIDIA's 7.86% and Microsoft's 5.58% see similarly large cuts. Micron Technology, sitting right at 4.53% in QQQ, also drops to around 1%.

**The flip side is what actually makes the case for equal-weighting.** QQQ's roughly 90 constituents outside the top 10 each carry, on average, well under 1% individual weight in the market-cap version - some considerably less. Every one of those gets lifted to roughly 1% under equal-weighting, meaning the fund's return becomes far more dependent on how the Nasdaq-100's smaller, less-followed constituents perform, and far less dependent on whether Apple or NVIDIA specifically keeps delivering. This is the real mechanism behind the "less concentration risk" pitch - it isn't a vague diversification claim, it's a specific, large reallocation of influence away from ten names and toward roughly ninety others.

---

## Two Different Tickers Get Confused Here

**QQQE is the Direxion NASDAQ-100 Equal Weighted Index Shares ETF**, launched March 21, 2012, tracking the Nasdaq-100 Equal Weighted Index. It is not an Invesco product, despite the shared "QQQ" naming convention that trips people up.

**A genuinely new option entered this space in 2026: Invesco's own QEW**, an equal-weight Nasdaq-100 fund that assigns roughly 1% to each of the 100 non-financial constituents and rebalances quarterly to trim winners back down and boost laggards back up - the same mechanical idea as QQQE, from the company that actually runs QQQ itself. Anyone searching for "equal weight QQQ" today has a real second option that didn't exist a couple of years ago, not just QQQE by default.

| QEW | Invesco | 0.25% |
| QQQE | Direxion | 0.35% |

QEW's 0.25% undercuts QQQE's 0.35% by a meaningful margin for what is, functionally, the same equal-weighting concept. For an investor who has decided equal-weighting is the right approach and is choosing purely on cost, QEW is the cheaper way to implement that decision as of 2026 - though QEW's shorter track record is itself a real consideration, covered below.

## The Drawdown Comparison That Doesn't Hold Up

This is the number that gets cited most often in favor of equal-weighting: QQQ's maximum drawdown since inception runs around -83%, driven by the 2000-2002 dot-com crash. QQQE's maximum drawdown since its own inception is a much smaller -32%. Presented side by side, that looks like decisive proof that equal-weighting meaningfully cushions the downside.

**It isn't a fair comparison, and the reason is simple: QQQE didn't exist yet during the dot-com crash.** QQQ launched in 1999 and lived through it. QQQE launched in March 2012, more than a decade later, and has never experienced a Nasdaq crash of dot-com magnitude. Comparing "worst drawdown ever" between a fund with 27 years of history and one with 14 is comparing different events, not different risk profiles.

**A fairer test is looking at a period both funds actually lived through.** During the 2022 bear market, QQQ fell approximately 32.7% peak to trough. QQQE's entire since-inception maximum drawdown is -32%. In other words, during the one genuine broad tech selloff both funds have actually experienced, equal-weighting provided little to no real cushion - QQQE's worst-ever decline and QQQ's 2022 decline land in almost the same place. That makes sense once you think about the mechanism: equal-weighting only helps when a crash is concentrated in specifically the largest few names while the rest of the index holds up better. In 2022, the selloff was broad across nearly the entire Nasdaq-100, mega-caps and smaller constituents alike, which is exactly the scenario where reweighting away from the giants doesn't buy much protection.

## Where Equal-Weighting Actually Does Something Different

**Return profile over the past decade tells a clearer story than drawdown does.** Over the trailing 10 years, QQQ has significantly outpaced QQQE - annualized returns in the neighborhood of 22% for QQQ versus roughly 15.5% for QQQE, according to portfolio-analytics data (treat the exact figures as directional rather than fixed, since they shift with the measurement window). That gap is the direct cost of equal-weighting during a decade when the Nasdaq-100's largest names - the same handful covered in our concentration guide - meaningfully outperformed the index's smaller constituents. Equal-weighting systematically sells down winners and buys up laggards every quarter, which works against you specifically when the biggest names keep winning, which is what happened for most of the past ten years.

**Dividend yield runs slightly higher for the equal-weight version:** QQQE's trailing yield sits around 0.52%, against QQQ's roughly 0.42-0.45%. Still a low yield by any broad-market standard - this is still a tech-heavy fund - but a real, if modest, difference driven by giving proportionally more weight to the index's more mature, higher-yielding constituents relative to QQQ's tilt toward its largest (often lower-yielding) names.

---

## Who Equal-Weighting Actually Suits

**An investor specifically uncomfortable with how much of QQQ's return depends on a handful of names continuing to perform** gets something real from QQQE or QEW: less dependence on any single company, and more exposure to the Nasdaq-100's 90-plus smaller constituents that market-cap weighting gives comparatively little influence to. That's a legitimate reason to choose equal-weighting - it isn't nothing.

**An investor expecting equal-weighting to meaningfully soften the next broad market shock** should recalibrate that expectation based on what 2022 actually showed: when the whole sector falls together, equal-weighting doesn't provide much of the protection its drawdown-since-inception numbers seem to promise. The real trade-off is return profile and single-name dependence, not crash protection.

## The Turnover Cost Most Comparisons Skip

Resetting roughly 100 positions back to equal weight every quarter means QQQE and QEW trade far more than QQQ does. QQQ's market-cap weighting only requires trading when the index itself adds or removes a constituent, or during its own quarterly reweighting within the concentration limits covered in our concentration guide - relatively modest turnover for a fund of this size. An equal-weight fund, by contrast, has to systematically sell down whatever just outperformed and buy whatever just lagged, every single quarter, by design. That's a structurally higher-turnover strategy, and higher turnover inside a fund generally means more realized gains getting distributed to shareholders - equal-weight ETFs are well documented as less tax-efficient in a taxable account than cap-weighted index ETFs for exactly this reason.

**This matters less for a non-US investor than it would for a US taxable holder, and it's worth being specific about why.** A regulated investment company like QQQ, QQQE, or QEW can distribute two different kinds of payout: ordinary dividends and capital gain distributions. Ordinary dividends paid to a non-resident alien are subject to the standard 30%-or-treaty-rate withholding covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/). Capital gain distributions are treated differently under US tax law and are generally not subject to that same withholding for a non-resident alien who isn't otherwise engaged in a US trade or business. In practical terms: the extra capital gains distributions that come from QQQE or QEW's higher turnover are a real cost for a US taxable investor, but largely don't create the same extra withholding drag for the international investor this site is written for. It's still worth knowing the mechanism exists - and confirming the specific character of any distribution with your broker's tax reporting rather than assuming - but it's a smaller practical factor here than it would be in a typical US-investor comparison of the same two funds.

## Bottom Line

QQQE and the newer, cheaper QEW give real exposure to the Nasdaq-100's smaller constituents that QQQ's market-cap weighting mostly ignores, and that's a legitimate reason to consider either one. What they don't reliably deliver is the crash protection their headline drawdown numbers suggest - QQQE's smaller since-inception decline is largely an artifact of not having existed during the dot-com crash, and its actual 2022 performance landed close to QQQ's own. The real decision is whether you're comfortable trading away a decade of mega-cap-driven outperformance for reduced single-name concentration, not whether equal-weighting will cushion the next downturn.
