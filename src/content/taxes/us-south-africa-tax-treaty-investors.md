Before a South African resident's first dividend or capital gain from US stocks is taxed at all, there's a more basic hurdle most guides skip: getting the money out of South Africa in the first place. The South African Reserve Bank's Foreign Investment Allowance caps offshore investment at **R10 million per individual per year**, and anything above R2 million requires a separate SARS Approval for International Transfer (AIT) - not just a form your broker handles, but a step with the Reserve Bank before the money ever reaches a US brokerage account.

Once funded, the treaty side is more conventional: the US-South Africa tax treaty caps dividend withholding at **15%** for individual investors, claimed via [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/). This guide covers that treaty rate, the FIA/AIT mechanics of getting money offshore, and how SARS taxes the resulting capital gains and dividends once they're earned.

---

## Getting Money Out: The Foreign Investment Allowance

South African residents can use the **Foreign Investment Allowance (FIA)** to move up to **R10 million per calendar year** offshore for investment purposes - covering brokerage funding, offshore property, foreign bank accounts, and similar uses.

**The R2 million threshold that changes the process:** transfers up to R2 million per year can generally proceed through your bank using your standard tax compliance status. Above R2 million (up to the R10 million FIA cap), you need a specific **SARS Tax Compliance Status - Approval for International Transfer (AIT)**, a separate application confirming your tax affairs are in order before the larger transfer is permitted. This isn't a formality to skip - budget real lead time for it if you're moving a substantial amount to fund a US brokerage account in one transfer, rather than assuming your bank can process it the same way as a smaller transfer.

This is a South African exchange-control question, not a US tax question - it determines how much and how easily you can fund an account, before any treaty rate or IRS rule becomes relevant at all.

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends

The US-South Africa income tax treaty caps US withholding on dividends paid to South African resident individuals at **15%** for portfolio investors (under 10% ownership), down from the 30% non-treaty default - and down further to 5% for corporate holders meeting the 10%+ direct-investment threshold, which doesn't apply to an individual retail investor.

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Individual) |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends (portfolio) | 30% | **15%** |
| Dividends (10%+ corporate holder) | 30% | 5% |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-South Africa Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; PwC South Africa Tax Summaries (2026).*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form W-8BEN filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment - check your dividend statement to confirm which rate is actually being withheld.

## Capital Gains: SARS Taxes Worldwide, Including US Stocks

South African tax residents are taxed on worldwide income and gains, and a gain on US stocks doesn't become exempt just because you were physically outside South Africa when you realized it, or because the proceeds stayed in a US brokerage account rather than returning home. Foreign capital gains must be declared on your South African return regardless of where the money sits.

**How the tax is calculated:** South Africa uses an inclusion-rate system. For individuals, **40% of the net capital gain** is included in taxable income, then taxed at your normal marginal income tax rate (up to 45%) - producing a **maximum effective capital gains rate of 18%** (40% × 45%) for the highest earners, and a lower effective rate for anyone below the top bracket.

**Annual exclusion:** individuals get an annual exclusion on capital gains - **R40,000** for the year of assessment ending February 2026, rising to **R50,000** for the year ending February 2027. Gains below this threshold in a given tax year aren't taxed at all; only the excess is included at the 40% inclusion rate.

**Worked example:** a South African investor in the top 45% bracket realizes a R200,000 gain on US stocks in a year with no other capital gains. After the R50,000 annual exclusion, R150,000 is subject to inclusion. 40% of that (R60,000) is added to taxable income and taxed at 45%, producing R27,000 in tax - an effective rate of 13.5% on the full R200,000 gain, below the 18% maximum because the exclusion absorbed part of it.

---

## Dividends: 15% US, Then South African Tax With a Credit

**US side:** 15% withheld with a valid W-8BEN (30% without one).

**South African side:** foreign dividends received by a South African resident are generally included in taxable income and taxed at your marginal rate, with a foreign tax credit available for the US withholding already paid, capped at the South African tax otherwise due on that same income. (This differs from South African-source dividends, which fall under a separate domestic Dividends Tax regime at a flat rate - the mechanics described here are specific to foreign-source dividends like US stock payouts.)

Keep your broker's Form 1042-S (issued by March 15 each year) as documentation of the US withholding for your South African return and credit claim.

## Broker Access

Both Interactive Brokers and eToro are confirmed to accept South African residents for account opening. Neither broker changes the 15% treaty rate itself - that's fixed by the DTAA once W-8BEN is filed correctly - but account-opening requirements and documentation needs are worth confirming directly before applying, particularly given the FIA/AIT funding process described above, which happens on the South African side before the money ever reaches either platform.

---

## Practical Checklist

**South African resident investing in US stocks:**
- [ ] Track offshore transfers against the R10 million annual Foreign Investment Allowance
- [ ] Apply for SARS Tax Compliance Status (AIT) in advance if a single transfer will exceed R2 million - this takes real processing time, not a same-day approval
- [ ] Confirm W-8BEN is on file with your broker; verify 15%, not 30%, on dividend statements
- [ ] Declare foreign capital gains and dividends on your South African return regardless of whether the money is repatriated
- [ ] Track the annual capital gains exclusion (R40,000 for the year ending February 2026; R50,000 from the year ending February 2027) and the 40% inclusion rate on the excess
- [ ] Claim the foreign tax credit for US dividend withholding using Form 1042-S as documentation
- [ ] Confirm current account-opening requirements directly with Interactive Brokers or eToro before applying

## Bottom Line

The treaty itself is a solved problem once W-8BEN is filed: 15% on dividends, no US tax on capital gains, nothing further to optimize. What actually takes planning for a South African investor is upstream and downstream of the treaty - clearing the R10 million FIA and, above R2 million, the SARS AIT process to get money offshore in the first place, and then tracking worldwide capital gains and dividends on the South African return once returns start coming back, since SARS's reach doesn't stop at the border the way some investors assume.
