South Korea taxes profits from overseas stocks - including US shares - at a flat **22%** (inclusive of local tax), applied to gains above an annual **KRW 2.5 million** exemption. Unlike a domestically-listed Korean stock, where an individual investor typically pays no separate capital gains tax at all outside specific large-shareholder rules, a US stock held through a foreign broker faces this dedicated overseas-stock regime instead - a structural difference worth understanding clearly before assuming Korean-market tax habits carry over.

This guide covers that 22% rate, the treaty's standard 15% dividend withholding, and a notable temporary incentive introduced for 2026 that rewards repatriating overseas stock proceeds into domestic Korean equities.

---

## The 22% Overseas Stock Capital Gains Tax

For a Korean tax resident, capital gains on foreign stocks - US shares included - are taxed at a flat **22%** (this figure already includes the local income tax surcharge on the base national rate), applied to net trading profit realized in a given year **above a KRW 2.5 million annual exemption**.

**Loss offsetting is available within the tax year:** if you have both gains and losses across different overseas stock positions in the same year, losses can offset gains before the 22% rate and the exemption are applied - a real planning lever for an investor managing several positions rather than a single holding.

**Worked example:** a Korean investor realizes KRW 30 million in gains and KRW 5 million in losses on overseas stocks in the same year. Net gain: KRW 25 million. After the KRW 2.5 million exemption: KRW 22.5 million taxable. Tax at 22%: KRW 4.95 million.

## A 2026 Incentive: Repatriating Into Domestic Equities

South Korea introduced a temporary, notable incentive for 2026: individual investors who sell overseas stocks held as of a specified late-2025 reference date, repatriate the proceeds, and invest in domestic Korean equities for at least one year can access a capital gains exemption of up to **KRW 50 million** - a substantially larger allowance than the standard KRW 2.5 million exemption, specifically designed to encourage capital flowing from overseas holdings back into the Korean stock market.

**This is a policy-driven, time-limited measure**, not a permanent feature of how overseas stocks are taxed - confirm the current eligibility window, reference dates, and conditions directly with a Korean tax advisor or the National Tax Service before assuming it applies to a specific sale, since the exact qualifying dates and mechanics are the kind of detail that needs to be current rather than approximate.

## 15% on Dividends Under the South Korea Treaty

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) | Direct Corporate (10%+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** | **10%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Taxed under Korean domestic law (see above) | Taxed under Korean domestic law |

*Sources: US-Korea Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; IRS Technical Explanation of the Convention.*

Form W-8BEN is what turns the default rate into the treaty rate. Nothing changes until the form arrives; payments before it are withheld in full.

## Getting an Account Open from South Korea

Interactive Brokers and eToro both list South Korea among the countries they onboard. Neither changes the 22% overseas-stock capital gains calculation, which is a function of Korean domestic law applied to gains regardless of which broker holds the position.

## Your Next Steps as a South Korea Resident

**Before you place the first trade from South Korea:**
- [ ] Confirm W-8BEN is on file with your broker; verify 15%, not 30%, on dividend statements
- [ ] Track overseas stock trading gains and losses across the year - losses offset gains before the KRW 2.5 million exemption and 22% rate apply
- [ ] Don't assume domestic Korean stock tax habits (typically no separate individual capital gains tax) carry over to US stocks - they're taxed under the separate overseas-stock regime
- [ ] If considering selling overseas stocks and reinvesting domestically, confirm current eligibility for the 2026 repatriation incentive (up to KRW 50 million exemption) with a Korean tax advisor before relying on it
- [ ] Confirm the broker's Korean documentation requirements in advance - these tend to be stricter than average

## What All of This Means from South Korea

The treaty rate is standard - 15% on dividends, claimed with W-8BEN. The number that actually shapes a Korean investor's after-tax return on US stocks is the 22% overseas-stock capital gains tax, a dedicated regime distinct from how domestic Korean shares are typically taxed for individuals, with a modest standing KRW 2.5 million annual exemption and loss offsetting available within the year. The temporary 2026 repatriation incentive is worth checking if you're considering a large realization and reinvestment into Korean equities, but treat its exact terms as something to confirm currently rather than assume from general descriptions.
