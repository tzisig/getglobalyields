Ukraine taxes foreign dividends - a US stock's dividend included - at a combined **14%** (9% personal income tax plus a 5% wartime military levy), but taxes capital gains from selling securities at a combined **23%** (18% PIT plus the same 5% military levy). That's a **9-percentage-point gap** between holding for dividend income and selling for a gain, entirely a function of Ukrainian domestic law rather than anything US-side. The military levy itself is a temporary wartime measure - currently 5%, legally set to revert to its pre-war 1.5% level starting the year after martial law is lifted.

This guide covers that gap clearly, alongside the treaty's standard 15% dividend withholding rate.

---

## The Dividends-vs-Gains Split, and Why It's Wider Than It Looks

**Foreign-source dividends** - including dividends from US-listed companies - are taxed in Ukraine at **9% personal income tax**, plus the **5% military levy**, for a combined **14%**. This is a specific, lower rate carved out for foreign dividend income; domestic Ukrainian-company dividends face a different structure (5% PIT plus the 5% levy, for 10% combined, when the underlying profit already bore Ukrainian corporate tax).

**Capital gains from securities** - including gains from selling US stocks - fall under Ukraine's general **18% PIT** rate for capital gains, forex, and other investment assets, plus the same **5% military levy**, for a combined **23%**.

**The practical gap:** a Ukrainian investor holding US stocks primarily for dividend income faces a materially lower combined domestic rate (14%) than one realizing gains through active trading (23%) - a 9-point difference that has real tax-planning weight for anyone deciding between a buy-and-hold dividend approach and frequent trading.

**The military levy is explicitly temporary in law:** the current 5% rate applies during martial law; Ukrainian law provides for it to revert to its earlier 1.5% level starting the calendar year after martial law is officially lifted. Until then, the 5% add-on applies across dividends, capital gains, interest, and most other passive income categories alike.

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-Ukraine Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text.*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment.

**Worked example - a $2,000 US dividend:**
- US withholding at 15% (treaty rate, W-8BEN on file): $300
- Ukrainian PIT at 9% on the gross $2,000: $180
- Ukrainian military levy at 5% on the gross $2,000: $100
- Ukrainian domestic liability before credit: $280
- Foreign tax credit for the $300 already paid to the US: fully absorbs the $280 domestic liability
- Total tax paid: effectively the $300 US withholding, with no meaningful additional Ukrainian tax on top of it - confirm the exact credit mechanics with a Ukrainian tax advisor, since the US rate exceeds the combined Ukrainian rate here

## Bottom Line

The treaty rate is standard - 15% on dividends, claimed with W-8BEN, and more than enough to absorb Ukraine's own 14% combined domestic dividend rate via the foreign tax credit. The detail worth understanding is the structural gap between how Ukraine taxes dividend income (14% combined) versus capital gains from selling securities (23% combined) - a real, current difference driven partly by the temporary 5% wartime military levy layered on both. That levy is legally temporary, tied to the duration of martial law, which is worth keeping in mind for any long-horizon planning.
