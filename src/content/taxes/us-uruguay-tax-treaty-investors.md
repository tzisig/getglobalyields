Uruguay has long been described as a territorial-tax country - and for capital gains from foreign securities, that description just stopped being fully accurate. Since 2011, Uruguay has taxed foreign-source dividends and interest at **12%** under its Personal Income Tax (IRPF), but capital gains from selling foreign securities were outside that net. **As of January 1, 2026, that changed**: foreign-source capital gains from movable property - including gains from selling US stocks - are now included in the same 12% IRPF base, with withholding and advance-payment mechanisms starting from **July 2026**.

There's also no US-Uruguay income tax treaty, so full 30% US withholding applies regardless. This guide covers both pieces.

---

## No Treaty: Full 30% US Withholding

With no income tax treaty between the US and Uruguay, there's no treaty article to reduce the default rate. Every US dividend paid to a Uruguay-resident investor is withheld at the full **30% statutory rate**.

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing.** It won't bring the rate below 30%, but it certifies your non-US status to your broker and avoids the risk of default backup withholding treatment on an undocumented account.

---

## The 2026 Change: Capital Gains Join Dividends and Interest at 12%

**Since 2011**, Uruguay has taxed foreign-source dividends and interest received by resident individuals (outside a specific new-resident tax holiday, covered below) at a flat **12% IRPF** rate.

**What's new for 2026:** the scope was expanded to bring **foreign-source capital gains from movable property** - stocks, bonds, and similar securities held abroad - into the same 12% taxable base, alongside a parallel expansion covering foreign real estate gains. This closes a gap that previously let a Uruguayan resident's gains from selling US stocks fall outside Uruguay's tax net entirely, even while the dividends from those same stocks were already taxed.

**How it's collected:** withholding and advance-payment mechanisms began applying from 2026, with remittance obligations starting **July 2026**. Resident financial intermediaries can act as withholding agents, applying an **8% rate** on a monthly basis, with taxpayers able to elect to treat that withholding as a definitive, final payment rather than reconciling later on an annual return.

**The tax holiday for new residents:** Uruguay offers new tax residents a temporary exemption from this foreign-income tax, with the threshold recently raised - reported around **USD 2 million** in some coverage of the reform - though the exact current threshold and qualifying conditions are worth confirming directly with a Uruguayan tax advisor given how recently this changed, rather than relying on a single figure that itself may already be superseded.

## The Treaty Gap and What It Means Practically

Because there's no treaty, there's no built-in credit mechanism specifying exactly how the 30% already withheld by the US interacts with Uruguay's 12% IRPF on the same income. Uruguay's domestic law may still provide some form of unilateral relief for foreign tax paid, but - as with other no-treaty countries in this series - confirm the actual mechanics with a Uruguayan tax advisor rather than assuming a clean, automatic offset.

**Worked example - a $2,000 US dividend, outside the new-resident holiday:**
- US withholding at 30% (no treaty): $600
- Uruguayan IRPF at 12% on the gross $2,000: $240
- Combined exposure before any relief: $840 on a $2,000 dividend - confirm with a Uruguayan advisor how much of the US withholding offsets the domestic liability

## Bottom Line

There's no US-Uruguay tax treaty, so the full 30% US withholding applies with no reduction available - W-8BEN still matters for documentation, not for a lower rate. What genuinely changed for 2026 is on the Uruguayan side: capital gains from selling US stocks, previously untouched by Uruguay's foreign-income tax, are now folded into the same 12% IRPF that has applied to foreign dividends and interest since 2011. Anyone still working from an older description of Uruguay as a country that doesn't tax foreign capital gains at all is working from outdated information as of this year.
