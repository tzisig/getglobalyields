Every guide on this site talks about federal US tax - IRS withholding, treaty rates, Form 1042-S. A question that comes up less often but is worth addressing directly: does opening an account with a broker based in a specific US state - Illinois for Interactive Brokers, New York for many others - create any obligation to that state specifically? For the ordinary non-US investor, the answer is no, and this article explains the actual dividing line.

---

## State Tax Follows Your Own Connection to a State, Not Your Broker's

US state income tax systems generally tax two categories of people: residents of that state (on their worldwide income) and non-residents who have state-source income - most commonly wages earned by working physically within the state, income from a business operated in the state, or income from real property located in the state.

**A non-US investor with no US residence, who has never worked in the US, and who holds only intangible securities (stocks, ETFs, bonds) through a broker has none of these connections to any particular state.** The broker's state of incorporation or headquarters location is not, by itself, a basis for that state to tax you - your dividends and capital gains are not "sourced" to Illinois just because Interactive Brokers happens to be headquartered there, in the same way that a French company depositing your dividend into a French bank account doesn't make the dividend French-sourced.

---

## Why This Question Comes Up

The confusion is understandable. Federal tax withholding is genuinely tied to the broker's role as a withholding agent - your broker does withhold and remit federal tax on your behalf. It is a reasonable, if incorrect, extension to assume something similar happens at the state level based on where the broker is located. It doesn't, for the ordinary passive investor - state tax nexus is about your own activities and property, not your broker's location.

---

## The Situations Where State Tax Could Actually Apply

These are genuinely narrow and do not describe ordinary passive stock and ETF investing through a broker:

- **You own US real estate located in a specific state.** Real property income and gains are commonly taxed by the state where the property sits, regardless of your residency - this is a completely separate question from your brokerage account.
- **You spend substantial time physically present and working within a specific state**, potentially creating state residency or a state-source income question independent of your federal nonresident alien status.
- **You operate an actual business with a physical presence in a state** - again, unrelated to a passive brokerage account.

**None of these apply simply because your broker is headquartered somewhere, or because the US exchange on which your stock trades happens to be based in a particular state.**

---

## Delaware, Nevada, and "Tax-Friendly State" Confusion

A related but distinct question sometimes comes up: does it matter that many US companies (and some brokers or entity structures) are incorporated in Delaware or Nevada for corporate-law reasons? For an ordinary individual investor holding stock in a Delaware-incorporated company, the answer is the same - the company's state of incorporation does not create a state tax connection for you as a shareholder. This is a corporate-law choice made for reasons unrelated to shareholder taxation, and it does not follow through to create any state filing obligation for someone simply holding the stock.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Confirm you have no US real estate holdings, physical business presence, or extended physical presence in any specific US state
- [ ] If any of those apply, treat that specific situation as requiring dedicated advice - it is a genuinely different question from ordinary portfolio investing
- [ ] Do not assume your broker's headquarters location creates any state filing obligation - it generally does not for a passive investor
- [ ] Focus your compliance attention on the federal side (W-8BEN, Form 1042-S, home-country reporting) covered throughout this site, since that is where the actual obligations sit for most non-US investors

---

## Bottom Line

For the ordinary non-US investor holding US stocks and ETFs through a broker, state tax is simply not part of the picture - not because of some special exemption, but because the basic requirements for state tax nexus (residency, physical presence, in-state property or business activity) are not met by passive securities ownership, regardless of where the broker happens to be headquartered. The exceptions - US real estate, physical business presence, or extended time physically in a specific state - are distinct situations that call for their own dedicated advice, not an extension of ordinary brokerage investing.
