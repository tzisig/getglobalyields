Iceland taxes capital income - dividends, capital gains, interest, and rental income - at a single flat rate of **22%** (fjármagnstekjuskattur), with no holding-period discount for shares and no separate lower rate for dividends versus gains the way several other countries in this series apply. A change effective **January 1, 2026** removed one option investors previously had for softening that liability: unused personal tax deductions can no longer be offset against capital income tax.

This guide covers that 22% flat structure, the 2026 deduction change, and the treaty's standard 15% dividend rate.

---

## A Single Flat 22% on Dividends and Capital Gains Alike

Iceland's fjármagnstekjuskattur applies uniformly at **22%** across four categories of capital income: dividends, rental income, capital gains, and interest income. For a US stock investor, this means dividends and capital gains from selling shares are taxed at the identical rate - there's no incentive within Icelandic tax law to hold longer for a lower capital gains rate, since gains and dividends already sit at the same 22% regardless of timing.

**Mechanical difference between dividends and gains:** withholding tax on dividends and interest is deducted at the time of payment, while tax on capital gains (and rental income) is calculated and paid later, when you declare the gain on your return - a timing distinction worth knowing even though the ultimate rate is the same.

**The 2026 change:** as of January 1, 2026, the ability to offset unused personal deductions against capital income tax payments was eliminated. For investors who previously used this mechanism to reduce their effective capital income tax burden, this is a real, negative change worth factoring into current-year planning rather than assuming an older, more favorable calculation still applies.

**A narrow exemption that doesn't help most equity investors:** interest income up to ISK 150,000 per year per person is exempt, even where withholding was already deducted - but this specific allowance applies to interest, not to dividends or capital gains from shares, so it doesn't reduce the tax on US stock dividends or sale proceeds directly.

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-Iceland Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; PwC Iceland Tax Summaries and Skatturinn (Icelandic Tax Authority) guidance (2026).*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment.

**Worked example - a $2,000 US dividend:**
- US withholding at 15% (treaty rate, W-8BEN on file): $300
- Icelandic capital income tax at 22% on the gross $2,000: $440
- Foreign tax credit for the US withholding already paid: -$300
- Net Icelandic tax owed: $140
- Total tax paid (US + Iceland): $300 + $140 = $440 - Iceland's 22% rate applied once, no double taxation

## Practical Checklist

**Icelandic resident investing in US stocks:**
- [ ] Confirm W-8BEN is on file with your broker; verify 15%, not 30%, on dividend statements
- [ ] Declare capital gains from US stock sales on your annual return - unlike dividends, gains aren't withheld automatically and require separate declaration
- [ ] Don't assume any personal-deduction offset against capital income tax is still available - this was eliminated effective January 1, 2026
- [ ] Remember the ISK 150,000 interest exemption doesn't extend to dividend or capital gains income from shares
- [ ] Claim the foreign tax credit for the 15% US withholding against Iceland's 22% capital income tax on the same dividend
- [ ] Confirm current account-opening requirements with Interactive Brokers or eToro before applying

## Bottom Line

The treaty rate is standard - 15% on dividends, claimed with W-8BEN, cleanly credited against Iceland's 22% flat capital income tax. Iceland's system is notably simple compared to several neighbors in this series - one rate for both dividends and capital gains, no holding-period exemption to plan around - which makes the main thing worth tracking the 2026 elimination of the personal-deduction offset, a real reduction in previously available relief rather than a structural feature to plan long-term strategy around.
