There's no US-Jordan income tax treaty - the only bilateral tax arrangement between the two countries is a narrow 1974 agreement covering shipping and aircraft earnings, which has nothing to do with stock investing. That means the full **30% US statutory withholding** applies to every US dividend paid to a Jordan-resident investor. On Jordan's side, the picture turns out to be simpler than that gap might suggest: Jordan taxes residents on a broadly **territorial basis**, and **foreign-source dividends and capital gains generally aren't taxed for individuals** either, unless the income specifically originates from money or deposits inside Jordan.

This guide covers both pieces.

---

## No Treaty Relief for Jordanian Residents: the Full 30%

Jordan has no income tax treaty with the United States, so the statutory position applies without modification: **30%** withheld from every US dividend at source.

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing.** The 30% stands regardless; what the form does is establish non-US status and avoid a backup-withholding default.

---

## Jordan's Side: Territorial Taxation Reaches the Same Result as No Treaty

Jordan's Income Tax Law generally taxes residents and non-residents on Jordan-source income, with progressive rates from **5% to 30%** applying to Jordan-source income. **Dividends received by a Jordanian resident from a foreign (non-resident) payer are taxable in Jordan only if they originate from money or deposits held inside Jordan** - a US dividend paid on shares held through a foreign brokerage account, funded from outside that specific channel, generally falls outside this trigger.

**Capital gains from selling shares** - listed or unlisted - are generally not subject to personal taxation for individuals in Jordan, receiving treatment broadly aligned with how companies are taxed on the same kind of gain, which in practice means individual investors are not the target of this tax.

**What this adds up to:** for most Jordan-resident investors holding US stocks through a standard foreign brokerage account, **neither the dividend nor the capital gain is likely to trigger Jordanian domestic tax** - meaning the 30% US withholding, absent a treaty to reduce it, is likely close to the entire tax cost of the arrangement. Confirm your specific situation with a Jordanian tax advisor, since the "money or deposits from inside Jordan" trigger for dividends depends on exactly how your account is funded.

## A Note on the Dinar

The Jordanian dinar has been pegged to the US dollar since 1995, a long-standing and stable peg that - similar to a few other countries covered in this series - means currency conversion when funding or withdrawing from a USD-denominated brokerage account carries minimal exchange-rate uncertainty for a Jordan-resident investor.

---

## Pricing the Jordanian Position

The two Jordanian rules - dividends taxed only when they originate from money held inside Jordan, capital gains generally untaxed for individuals - are worth converting into numbers.

On a **$1,000** US dividend paid into a foreign brokerage account funded from outside Jordan:

- $300 is withheld in the US at the statutory 30%
- $700 reaches the account
- Jordan, on the usual reading, adds nothing

On a **$12,000** gain from selling US shares: the US withholds nothing from a non-resident individual on portfolio securities, and Jordanian personal taxation generally does not reach share gains. The gain arrives whole.

**Effective rates: 30% on dividend income, close to 0% on capital gains.** That asymmetry is the single most useful planning fact in this guide - it argues for holding growth-oriented positions rather than high-yielding ones, because in Jordan the tax only ever bites at the dividend.

**The caveat that decides it** is the funding channel. The dividend exemption hangs on the income not originating from money or deposits inside Jordan, which is a question about how your account was funded, not about the shares. Get that confirmed before assuming the zero.

## What It Comes Down To in Jordan

There's no US-Jordan tax treaty, so the full 30% US withholding applies with no reduction available - file W-8BEN for the documentation it provides and nothing more. What makes Jordan a comparatively simple case despite the treaty gap is its own territorial tax system: for most individual investors, neither the dividend nor the capital gain from a foreign brokerage account triggers Jordanian tax, which means the 30% US withholding is likely close to the entire cost - confirmed against your specific account-funding details with a Jordanian tax advisor.
