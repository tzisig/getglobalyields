Singapore has no income tax treaty with the United States. For dividends from US stocks, that means the full **30%** default withholding applies - there's no W-8BEN treaty box to check that brings it down. On its own, that would make Singapore one of the less attractive places in this series to hold US dividend-paying stocks.

But Singapore's own tax system changes the calculation substantially: there is **no capital gains tax** for individual investors, and foreign-sourced dividends and interest are generally **not taxed** even when brought into Singapore, under the territorial tax system. The US withholding is real and unavoidable - but it's the only layer of tax most Singapore-resident investors will face on US stock returns at all.

This guide covers that combination, plus a practical question that matters more here than the tax rate does: **Interactive Brokers accepts Singapore residents directly, but does not support CPF Investment Scheme or SRS funds** - retirement-linked money needs a different route entirely.

---

## No Treaty: The 30% Is Fixed

Without a treaty, Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) doesn't reduce your withholding rate the way it does for investors in treaty countries. It still serves its baseline purpose - certifying non-US person status and preventing the higher backup withholding regime from applying - but the number on your dividend statement will read 30%, not a reduced treaty figure, regardless of whether the form is filed correctly.

**Interest:** the same 30% default applies to most US-source interest, subject to the same domestic IRC §871(k) exemptions available to any non-treaty country for interest-related and short-term capital gain distributions from US-domiciled funds - see the [US dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/) for how those work.

**Capital gains:** the US generally does not tax capital gains realized by a non-resident alien on US securities under domestic law (IRC §871), independent of treaty status - one part of the picture that doesn't depend on Singapore having a treaty at all.

## Singapore's Side: No Capital Gains Tax, Territorial Treatment of Foreign Income

This is where Singapore's position genuinely differs from most other no-treaty countries covered in this series.

**No capital gains tax.** Singapore does not tax capital gains for individuals at all - not on shares, property, or any other asset class. Gains from selling US stocks are not subject to Singapore income tax. The one exception worth knowing: an individual whose buying and selling is frequent and systematic enough to look like a trading business can have those gains reclassified by IRAS as business income, assessed case by case - a real risk for very active traders, not a typical buy-and-hold investor.

**Foreign dividends generally aren't taxed either.** Under Singapore's territorial system, foreign-sourced income - including US dividends - is generally not subject to Singapore tax, whether or not it's remitted into Singapore. This is a genuinely favorable position: the 30% US withholding is, for most Singapore-resident investors, the only tax layer that applies to US dividend income at all.

**The net effect:** a Singapore-resident investor's after-tax outcome on US dividends (70% of the gross, after 30% withholding) and on capital gains (100% of the gain, no Singapore tax) can compare favorably to an investor in a country with a better treaty rate but meaningful domestic tax layered on top. The absence of a treaty here is a real cost only on the dividend side, and Singapore's own system does the rest of the work that a treaty would otherwise need to do.

## Interactive Brokers Singapore: What Works, and What Doesn't

Interactive Brokers accepts Singapore citizens and tax residents directly, with an online application that typically takes 15-30 minutes and account activation within a few business days of document verification - one of the more straightforward account-opening processes covered in this series.

**What IBKR Singapore does not support: CPF and SRS funds.**

- **CPF Investment Scheme (CPFIS):** funds in your Central Provident Fund cannot be directed through Interactive Brokers. CPFIS-eligible instruments must be purchased through a CPFIS-approved local broker (such as DBS Vickers, OCBC Securities, or UOB Kay Hian), and the range of CPFIS-eligible instruments is narrower than what a standard brokerage account offers.
- **Supplementary Retirement Scheme (SRS):** SRS funds also cannot go directly through IBKR. Platforms such as Endowus offer SRS-linked access to ETF-based portfolios, but that's a structurally different product from a self-directed IBKR account holding individual US stocks.

**What this means practically:** for money outside CPF and SRS - ordinary cash savings, for example - IBKR gives direct, low-cost access to the full range of US-listed stocks and ETFs, with dividends withheld at the standard 30% non-treaty rate. For CPF or SRS-linked money, the retirement-scheme rules govern which platform and which instruments are available, and IBKR is not currently one of the options for that specific pool of funds.

## Practical Checklist

**Singapore resident investing in US stocks (cash/ordinary account):**
- [ ] Expect 30% dividend withholding with no treaty reduction - file W-8BEN anyway to certify non-US status and avoid backup withholding
- [ ] Confirm whether any US bond ETF distributions qualify for the IRC §871(k) interest-related dividend exemption, which applies regardless of treaty status
- [ ] Remember Singapore does not tax the resulting capital gains for a standard buy-and-hold investor
- [ ] If trading frequently and systematically, be aware IRAS can reclassify gains as business income on a case-by-case basis
- [ ] Confirm Interactive Brokers (or your chosen broker) currently accepts Singapore residents before applying - broker policies can change

**Singapore resident with CPF or SRS funds earmarked for US stocks:**
- [ ] Confirm CPFIS-eligible instruments and approved brokers (DBS Vickers, OCBC Securities, UOB Kay Hian, and others) before assuming CPF funds can reach a US brokerage account directly
- [ ] For SRS funds, check platforms like Endowus for ETF-based access rather than assuming a self-directed IBKR account is available for this pool of money
- [ ] Understand that CPFIS/SRS product rules, not the US tax treaty question, are the binding constraint for retirement-linked funds

## Bottom Line

The lack of a US-Singapore tax treaty caps out at a real but contained cost: 30% withholding on US dividends, with no form or election that reduces it. Singapore's own tax system - no capital gains tax, no domestic tax on most foreign dividend income - means that 30% is often the only tax an ordinary Singapore-resident investor pays on US stock returns at all, which is a better overall position than the treaty rate alone would suggest. The more consequential practical question for many Singapore investors is not the tax treaty but the CPF/SRS access question: ordinary cash flows through Interactive Brokers cleanly, but CPF and SRS funds need a different platform entirely, and confirming that upfront avoids a wasted application.
