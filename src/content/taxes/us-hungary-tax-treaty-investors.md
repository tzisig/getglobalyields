Hungary is the one country in this series where searching for "the tax treaty rate" leads somewhere unexpected: **there isn't one anymore.** The US Treasury formally terminated the US-Hungary income tax treaty, effective **January 1, 2024**, after Hungary blocked the EU's adoption of the OECD's 15% global minimum corporate tax to protect its own 9% corporate rate. For a Hungarian investor holding US stocks, that termination is not a technicality - it means the **reduced treaty withholding rate individual investors used to get on US dividends is gone**, replaced by the full, un-reduced 30% US statutory rate.

This guide covers what actually changed, Hungary's own flat-tax treatment of dividends and capital gains on top of that, and why filing a W-8BEN still matters even though it no longer buys you a lower rate.

---

## What Termination Actually Changed

Before January 1, 2024, individual investors benefited from a reduced US withholding rate on Hungarian-resident dividend income under the treaty. **That reduction is gone.** US dividends paid to Hungarian tax residents are now subject to the **full 30% US statutory withholding rate** - there is no treaty article left to invoke to bring it down, because there is no treaty.

**Why it happened:** the termination stemmed from Hungary's veto of EU-wide implementation of the OECD's global minimum tax framework, which nearly 140 countries have signed onto. The US Treasury's position was that Hungary's continued 9% corporate rate, combined with the treaty, created an opening for tax avoidance the treaty was never designed to protect. Corporate investors are affected differently (Hungarian-source dividends to US corporate holders remain untaxed under Hungarian domestic law regardless of the treaty), but that carve-out doesn't help an individual holding US stocks from Hungary.

**No renegotiation is currently underway.** As of 2026, the treaty remains fully terminated, and there's no indicated timeline for a replacement.

---

## Hungary's Domestic Tax: Flat 15% PIT, Plus 13% Szocho on Capital Income

With no treaty to offer relief, Hungary's own flat-tax system is what actually applies on the Hungarian side.

**Personal income tax (SZJA):** Hungary taxes dividends, capital gains, and interest all at a single flat **15%** rate - the same rate that applies to employment income. There is no reduced rate for long-term holdings and no separate, lower capital gains category.

**Social contribution tax (Szocho):** a further **13%** applies to capital income - including dividends and capital gains - but only until an individual's *combined* income (employment plus capital income) for the year reaches **24 times the minimum wage**, **HUF 7,747,200** in 2026 (roughly €19,968). Once that combined threshold is crossed, the 13% Szocho stops applying to *additional* capital income for the rest of the year. In practice, this means a Hungarian investor's actual marginal rate on a given dividend depends on their total income position for the year - someone with modest overall income may face the full 15% + 13% = **28%** combined domestic rate, while someone who has already crossed the Szocho cap through salary or other capital income earlier in the year faces only the 15% PIT on the same dividend.

**Foreign-source dividends specifically:** Hungary's domestic law generally allows some form of unilateral credit for foreign tax already withheld on foreign-source income, though - without a treaty specifying the mechanism - the precise calculation and any cap on that credit is a question for a Hungarian tax advisor rather than something this guide can state with treaty-backed precision. Don't assume the full 30% US withholding is automatically creditable dollar-for-dollar against Hungarian tax; confirm the actual mechanism before relying on it.

## No Treaty Table Here - and Why W-8BEN Still Matters

Every other country guide in this series includes a table comparing the US default withholding rate against a reduced treaty rate. Hungary doesn't get one, because there's nothing to compare against - **30% is both the default and the final rate.**

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing anyway.** It doesn't reduce your rate below 30% - there's no treaty benefit left to claim - but it still certifies your foreign (non-US) status to your broker. Without it on file, some brokers default to **24% backup withholding** treatment for undocumented accounts, which creates its own reconciliation headache with the IRS, or may restrict account functionality entirely pending documentation. The form's job here shifts from "get the discount" to "prove who you are" - still necessary, just for a different reason than in every other guide in this series.

**Worked example - a $2,000 US dividend, Hungarian resident below the Szocho cap for the year:**
- US withholding at the full 30% statutory rate: $600
- Hungarian SZJA at 15% on the gross $2,000: $300
- Hungarian Szocho at 13% on the gross $2,000 (assuming still under the annual cap): $260
- Total Hungarian domestic liability before any foreign tax credit: $560
- Combined exposure before credit: $600 + $560 = $1,160 on a $2,000 dividend - confirm with a Hungarian advisor how much of the $600 US withholding can actually offset the $560 domestic liability, since no treaty mechanism governs the credit

Interactive Brokers and eToro each accept Hungarian residents. Neither broker's policies are affected by the treaty termination - it changes what happens at the tax-withholding and filing level, not account eligibility.

## What Needs Doing Before You Invest from Hungary

**Holding US stocks from Hungary - cover these:**
- [ ] File W-8BEN even though the treaty is gone - it certifies foreign status, and an undocumented account adds backup withholding on top of the 30%
- [ ] Expect 30% US withholding on every dividend payment, with no treaty-based reduction available
- [ ] Track your combined annual employment + capital income against the HUF 7,747,200 (2026) Szocho cap to know whether the extra 13% applies to a given dividend
- [ ] Confirm with a Hungarian tax advisor exactly how much of the US withholding is creditable against your Hungarian SZJA/Szocho liability, since the termination removed the treaty article that used to define this
- [ ] Don't rely on outdated guides or broker documentation citing a reduced Hungary treaty rate - anything referencing a rate below 30% predates January 1, 2024
- [ ] Confirm the broker's Hungarian documentation list first - and note the treaty is gone, so expect 30%

## Hungary, in a Paragraph

Hungary stands apart from every other country in this series: there is no treaty rate to claim, because the US Treasury terminated the underlying treaty effective January 1, 2024, over a dispute about the global minimum tax. A Hungarian investor now faces the full 30% US dividend withholding rate, stacked against Hungary's own flat 15% PIT plus a conditional 13% Szocho on capital income - all without a treaty mechanism spelling out how the two sides interact. W-8BEN is still worth filing for documentation purposes, but it no longer does the job it does everywhere else in this series. This is the rare case in the series where the honest answer to "what's the treaty rate" is that the question no longer has an answer.
