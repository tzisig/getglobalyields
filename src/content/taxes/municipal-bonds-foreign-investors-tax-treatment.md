Municipal bonds - debt issued by US states, cities, and local government entities - are famous among American investors for one reason: the interest is generally exempt from federal income tax. What is less widely known is that this exemption is not limited to US citizens. Most of it extends to non-US investors as well, and even the smaller category of municipal bonds that does not qualify for the core exemption is usually still free of US withholding under a separate rule.

This guide covers what is actually exempt, what mechanism makes it exempt, and what your home country still taxes even when the US does not.

---

## Tax-Exempt Municipal Bonds: Exempt Regardless of Who Owns Them

The core federal tax exemption for municipal bond interest, under IRC Section 103, excludes the interest from gross income - and this exclusion applies without regard to whether the bondholder is a US person or a foreign person. A non-US investor holding a standard tax-exempt municipal bond receives interest with no US withholding at all, for the same underlying reason a US investor pays no federal tax on it: the income is excluded from gross income at the source, not merely taxed at a reduced rate.

This is a meaningfully different mechanism from the treaty-rate reductions covered throughout this site's country guides. There is no W-8BEN treaty claim involved here and no rate to negotiate - the interest is simply outside the scope of US taxable income in the first place, for anyone.

---

## Taxable Municipal Bonds: A Second, Separate Exemption Usually Applies

Not all municipal bonds qualify for the federal tax exemption - some are issued as taxable municipal bonds, typically because the financed project does not meet the requirements for tax-exempt status under federal law. For these, the Section 103 exclusion does not apply.

**But a separate mechanism usually still gets a non-US investor to the same practical result: zero US withholding.** The portfolio interest exception (IRC §871(h)) eliminates the standard 30% NRA withholding on interest from most US debt obligations issued in registered form after July 18, 1984 - a category that includes US government bonds, US agency bonds, most corporate bonds, and taxable municipal bonds. Claiming this exception requires a valid Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) on file with the broker or paying agent, certifying foreign status.

**The practical result:** whether a municipal bond is tax-exempt (Section 103) or taxable (relying on the portfolio interest exception instead), a non-US investor with W-8BEN on file generally faces no US withholding on the interest either way - just through two different legal mechanisms.

---

## When the Portfolio Interest Exception Does Not Apply

The portfolio interest exception has specific exclusions worth knowing, even though they rarely affect an ordinary retail municipal bond investor:

- Interest that is contingent on the debtor's profits, receipts, or similar performance measures generally does not qualify
- Interest paid to a "10% shareholder" of the issuer (relevant mainly for corporate debt, not municipal issuers) is excluded
- The bond must be in registered form, not bearer form - virtually all municipal bonds issued today meet this requirement

For the overwhelming majority of retail municipal bond purchases, none of these exclusions apply.

---

## What This Does Not Change: Your Home Country's Tax

The US-side exemption - whether via Section 103 or the portfolio interest exception - has no bearing on how your home country taxes the same interest income. Most countries that tax worldwide income will still include municipal bond interest in your taxable base, at whatever rate applies to interest income domestically, regardless of the fact that the US itself did not withhold anything.

This is different from the dividend situation covered elsewhere on this site, where a foreign tax credit typically offsets double taxation between the US withholding and the home-country tax on the same income. Here, since the US generally withholds nothing, there is usually no US tax to credit against your home-country liability - the full interest amount typically just gets taxed once, by your home country, at its own rate.

---

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Confirm whether a specific municipal bond is tax-exempt (Section 103) or taxable before assuming either treatment
- [ ] File Form W-8BEN with your broker regardless - it is what secures the portfolio interest exception for taxable municipal bonds, and it is good practice to have on file in any case
- [ ] Do not assume the US exemption means the interest is tax-free everywhere - report it as part of your worldwide income under your home country's rules
- [ ] Confirm your specific bond is in registered form (essentially universal for bonds issued today) if relying on the portfolio interest exception
- [ ] Remember this exemption applies specifically to interest, not to any capital gain from selling the bond before maturity, which follows the standard capital gains treatment for non-resident aliens

---

## Bottom Line

Municipal bonds are one of the few areas of US investing where the tax treatment for a non-US investor is genuinely as favorable as it is for an American one - not through a treaty negotiation, but because the interest is either excluded from taxable income entirely (tax-exempt munis) or covered by the portfolio interest exception (taxable munis), with W-8BEN as the only documentation required either way. The one thing this does not change is your own country's tax on the same income, which most worldwide-income tax systems will still apply in full.
