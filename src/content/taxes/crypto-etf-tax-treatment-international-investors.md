Every other US-listed ETF discussed on this site - VOO, QQQ, TQQQ - is a Regulated Investment Company (RIC), and the withholding rules for foreign investors covered throughout this site's [dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/) are built around that structure. Spot crypto ETFs like IBIT, FBTC, and GBTC are not RICs. They are **grantor trusts**, and that structural difference genuinely changes how they are taxed - including, in ways that are less well-documented, for non-US investors.

---

## The Structural Difference: Grantor Trust vs. RIC

A grantor trust is tax-transparent. Rather than the fund itself being a taxable entity that distributes income to shareholders (the RIC model), the IRS treats each investor as directly owning a proportional fractional interest in the trust's actual underlying assets - in this case, Bitcoin or Ethereum held by the trust. Every dollar of income and expense the trust incurs is allocated straight through to you, the shareholder, as if you owned that fraction of the underlying asset personally.

**The practical consequence US investors have found surprising:** when the trust sells a small amount of its Bitcoin holdings to pay its own sponsor fee (0.25% annually for IBIT and FBTC, higher for GBTC), each shareholder is treated as having sold their proportional share of that Bitcoin - a taxable event, reported on Form 1099-B, that happens automatically every year regardless of whether the investor bought or sold anything themselves.

---

## What This Means for a Non-US Investor

This is where the analysis becomes genuinely less settled than the well-documented rules covering ordinary dividend-paying stocks and RIC-structured ETFs.

**The reasoning, by analogy to well-established principles:** non-resident aliens are generally not subject to US tax on capital gains from the disposition of personal property - including securities - as long as the gain is not effectively connected to a US trade or business and the investor does not meet the 183-day substantial presence test in the relevant year (the same general rule covered in the [main dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/) for ordinary stock capital gains). Since a grantor trust looks through to treat the investor as owning the underlying asset directly, and Bitcoin itself is personal property rather than FDAP income, the same general "capital gains not taxed for NRAs" principle would logically extend to both the periodic fee-driven phantom sales and any actual sale of the ETF shares themselves.

**What this guide cannot confirm with the same confidence as the rest of this site's tax content:** whether this analogy has been specifically tested, ruled on, or is applied consistently in practice by brokers and the IRS for non-US holders of spot crypto ETFs. This is a newer product category (spot Bitcoin ETFs launched in January 2024, spot Ethereum ETFs later that year), and the specific NRA tax treatment has not been as thoroughly documented in public guidance as the decades-old rules covering ordinary equities and RIC-structured funds.

**What to actually do with this:** treat the "probably not subject to US withholding" conclusion as a reasoned starting point, not a confirmed fact - and get current, specific advice from a cross-border tax advisor before assuming your crypto ETF holdings are automatically free of both the phantom-sale issue and any US withholding, particularly given how quickly guidance in this area could develop.

---

## What Definitely Doesn't Change: Your Home Country's Tax

Regardless of how the US treats these products, your home country's own tax rules for cryptocurrency-linked investments apply independently, and vary enormously by country - some tax crypto gains as ordinary income, some apply a capital gains regime, and some have specific rules for indirect crypto exposure through a fund structure versus direct coin ownership. This is worth confirming separately from the US-side analysis above.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the "phantom sale" reporting on Form 1099-B create an actual tax liability for a non-US investor, or just a reporting event?**
Based on the reasoned analogy discussed in this guide, the reporting itself (which happens automatically regardless of your tax status) likely doesn't translate into an actual US tax liability for a non-resident alien, since the underlying capital gain from that phantom sale would fall under the same generally-untaxed NRA framework - but confirm this specifically with a tax advisor rather than assuming the 1099-B reporting is purely cosmetic without verification.

**Is there a meaningful difference between IBIT, FBTC, and GBTC for this analysis?**
No - all three use the same grantor trust structure, meaning the tax analysis in this guide applies equally to each; the practical differences between them (expense ratio, trading volume, specific sponsor) don't affect the underlying tax characterization discussed here.

**Should I wait for clearer guidance before investing in spot crypto ETFs, given the tax uncertainty?**
This guide addresses the tax treatment specifically, not whether spot crypto ETFs are a sound investment choice, which is a separate question this guide doesn't address - if the tax uncertainty specifically is a concern, discussing it directly with a tax advisor familiar with your situation is the appropriate next step, rather than this guide making an investment recommendation either way.

**Does holding a spot crypto ETF create any additional US reporting obligations beyond what a standard equity ETF requires?**
The standard non-resident alien reporting framework (primarily driven by your broker's 1042-S and any 1099-B activity) generally applies, though given the grantor trust structure's phantom-sale mechanic, confirm with your broker and a tax advisor whether any additional documentation specific to this product category applies to your situation.

## Putting Crypto ETFs Into Practice

- [ ] Understand that spot crypto ETFs are grantor trusts, not RICs - a fundamentally different tax structure from every other ETF discussed on this site
- [ ] Be aware that US brokers may report periodic "phantom sale" events on Form 1099-B for the fund's fee-related Bitcoin sales, even for a non-US holder, regardless of the ultimate US tax outcome
- [ ] Do not assume the same interest-related dividend or capital gain distribution exemptions that apply to RIC-structured ETFs under IRC §871(k) apply here - the mechanism is entirely different
- [ ] Get current, specific advice from a cross-border tax advisor before relying on the "capital gains generally untaxed for NRAs" analogy for this specific product category
- [ ] Separately confirm how your home country taxes indirect crypto exposure through a US-listed fund, independent of the US-side analysis

Spot crypto ETFs are structurally different from every other fund covered on this site, and that difference matters for tax purposes. The reasoning that would extend the standard "NRAs generally don't pay US capital gains tax" rule to these products is sound by analogy, but this is newer territory than the well-established rules governing ordinary equities - treat it as a starting point for a conversation with a tax advisor, not a settled conclusion.
