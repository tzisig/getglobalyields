Every other country in this series taxes what you actually earned - a real dividend, a real capital gain. The Netherlands, through **Box 3**, does something structurally different: it taxes a **fictitious, government-assumed return** on your investment assets, currently around **6.04%**, at a rate of **36%** - regardless of what your US stocks actually did that year. If your portfolio was flat or down, Box 3 doesn't care; the deemed return is taxed as if you'd earned it anyway. If your portfolio doubled, you're still taxed on the same assumed 6.04%, not your real gain.

This is worth understanding before anything else in this guide, because it means the usual "how much withholding, how much home-country tax" framework doesn't quite apply the way it does elsewhere. This guide covers Box 3's mechanics, the treaty's standard 15% dividend rate, and a real reform coming in 2028 that would change all of this.

---

## Box 3: Taxed on a Fictitious Return, Not Your Actual One

Box 3 is the Dutch tax category covering savings and investments (as opposed to Box 1 for employment income or Box 2 for substantial shareholdings). For 2026-2027, it works like this:

- The tax authority assumes your investment assets - including US stocks - generated a **fictitious return of approximately 6.04%** for the year.
- That assumed return is taxed at **36%**, working out to an **effective rate of roughly 2.16% of your asset value**, applied regardless of your real investment performance.
- An annual **tax-free allowance of €59,357 per individual** (doubled for tax partners who elect to combine allowances) reduces the asset base subject to this calculation.
- **You cannot claim a refund or reduction based on actually earning less than the assumed rate** - a portfolio that lost money in a given year still generates a Box 3 tax bill based on the fictitious 6.04%, not the real loss.

**What this means concretely:** Box 3 is closer to a wealth tax than an income tax, despite being labeled and administered alongside income tax. Your actual dividends and actual capital gains on US stocks are not separately taxed under Box 3 - the deemed-return calculation on your asset value replaces that entirely, for better or worse depending on how your actual returns compare to the assumed rate in a given year.

## The 2028 Reform: A Real Change on the Horizon

Following Dutch Supreme Court rulings that found the fictitious-return system unlawful in certain circumstances (taxing assumed income that exceeded what a taxpayer could show they'd actually earned), the Dutch government has a law in progress to replace Box 3 with a system taxing **actual returns**, including unrealized gains, at 36%, with a lower **€1,800 tax-free threshold**. This has passed the House of Representatives but still requires Senate approval, with implementation planned for **January 1, 2028**.

**What this means for planning now:** through 2027, the fictitious-return system described above remains in effect - this isn't a "check back later" footnote, it's the operative rule for at least the next two tax years. But the direction of travel is clear enough that Dutch investors in US stocks should expect the calculation to change materially by 2028, moving toward something closer to how most other countries in this series tax investment income - including the detail that unrealized gains would become taxable, which is a meaningfully different (and for some investors, worse) outcome than a purely realized-gain system.

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends, Interacting Oddly With Box 3

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) | Direct Corporate (10%+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** | 5% (or lower for qualifying parent-subsidiary holdings) |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-Netherlands Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; Dutch Tax and Customs Administration (Belastingdienst) guidance (2026).*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment.

**Because Box 3 taxes the deemed return on the underlying asset rather than the actual dividend received, the interaction between US withholding and Dutch tax is structurally different from the credit mechanisms described elsewhere in this series.** A foreign tax credit for the 15% US withholding is generally available against the Box 3 liability attributable to that asset, but the calculation runs through the fictitious-return framework rather than a straightforward "credit the US tax against actual Dutch tax on the same dividend" comparison. Given the complexity this introduces, confirming the exact credit mechanics with a Dutch tax advisor is worth doing rather than assuming a 1:1 comparison with how other treaty countries in this series handle it.

## Bottom Line

The treaty rate is standard - 15% on dividends, the same as most of Western Europe. What makes the Netherlands genuinely different from every other country in this series is Box 3: a deemed-return wealth tax that ignores your actual US stock performance entirely, for better or worse depending on the year. That system is under real legal and legislative pressure and is scheduled to be replaced with an actual-returns model from 2028 - through 2027, though, plan around the fictitious 6.04%/36% calculation as the operative rule, not the reform that hasn't taken effect yet.
