The US-Poland tax treaty caps dividend withholding at a standard **15%** for individual investors, claimed with [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/). That part is routine. What determines a Polish investor's actual net return is what happens next: Poland's **Belka tax** - a flat 19% levy on investment income - applies on top of the US withholding, on every dividend and every capital gain, with no exceptions for size or income level the way some countries offer a small allowance.

This guide covers the treaty rate, how the Belka tax and the US withholding interact through the foreign tax credit, and broker access for Polish residents using Interactive Brokers or eToro.

---

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) | Direct Corporate (10%+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** | **5%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-Poland Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; PwC and Dudkowiak & Putyra Poland tax guidance (2026).*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form W-8BEN filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment - check your dividend statement to confirm which rate is actually withheld.

## The Belka Tax: 19% on Everything, No Allowance

Poland taxes capital gains and dividends - domestic and foreign alike - at a flat **19%** under the so-called Belka tax (named after the finance minister who introduced it). Unlike Germany's Sparerpauschbetrag or several other European countries covered in this series, Poland offers **no annual tax-free allowance** on investment income - the 19% applies from the first złoty of gain or dividend.

**Worked example - a $2,500 US dividend:**
- US withholding at 15% (treaty rate, W-8BEN on file): $375
- Polish Belka tax at 19% on the gross $2,500: $475
- Foreign tax credit for the US withholding already paid: -$375
- Net Polish tax owed: $100
- Total tax paid (US + Poland): $375 + $100 = $475 - Poland's 19% flat rate applied once, no double taxation

Because Poland's domestic rate (19%) is lower than the US treaty withholding rate on dividends (15% is actually below 19%, so this works in the investor's favor) - the credit absorbs the full US withholding and the remaining Polish liability is simply the gap between the two rates. This is a more favorable interaction than in countries where the domestic rate is far above the treaty rate, since the total combined burden here stays close to Poland's own 19%, not meaningfully higher.

**Capital gains work the same way:** since the US generally doesn't tax capital gains for non-resident aliens at all (independent of treaty status), a Polish investor's US stock capital gains face only the 19% Belka tax, with no US withholding to credit against it in the first place.

---

## Filing and Reporting

Polish tax residents must declare foreign-source investment income, including US dividends and capital gains, on their annual PIT-38 return (the form used for capital income), and claim the foreign tax credit for US withholding using documentation from Form 1042-S, issued by your broker by March 15 each year. Unlike domestic Polish dividends - where the paying institution typically withholds and remits the Belka tax automatically - foreign investment income generally requires self-reporting and self-calculation, since a foreign broker doesn't interface with the Polish tax system the way a domestic institution does.

## Bottom Line

The treaty delivers a standard 15% dividend rate, and because it sits below Poland's 19% Belka tax, the foreign tax credit works cleanly - the combined burden stays close to Poland's own flat rate rather than stacking meaningfully higher. The part that actually requires attention is procedural: Poland offers no allowance on investment income, and foreign-source dividends and gains require self-reporting on PIT-38 rather than the automatic withholding domestic Polish investments benefit from.
