Irish investors are famously well-versed in one tax quirk: the **8-year deemed disposal rule** on ETFs, which treats an Irish or EU-domiciled fund as sold - and taxes the growth - every eight years, whether or not you've actually sold anything. What's less widely understood is that this rule, and the exit tax rate that goes with it (recently reduced from 41% to **38%**, effective January 1, 2026), applies specifically to Irish and EU/EEA-domiciled funds - not to direct shares or, in many cases, to US-listed ETFs bought directly.

That distinction is worth understanding before it costs you a materially higher tax rate than necessary. This guide covers the treaty's standard 15% dividend rate, then goes deep on when Ireland's exit tax regime applies to a US-stock-focused portfolio and when the lower, deemed-disposal-free CGT rate applies instead.

---

## Exit Tax vs. Capital Gains Tax: The Distinction That Matters

Ireland taxes investment gains through two entirely different regimes depending on what you hold, and the gap between them is substantial.

**Irish and EU/EEA-domiciled funds (ISINs beginning IE or LU, among others):** subject to the **exit tax** regime at **38%** (reduced from 41%, effective for chargeable events from January 1, 2026), with the 8-year deemed disposal rule forcing a taxable event on unrealized growth every eight years regardless of whether you've sold. This is the regime most Irish investors default into when they buy a popular UCITS ETF without checking its domicile.

**Direct shares and non-equivalent US-listed ETFs:** subject to ordinary Capital Gains Tax at **33%**, with **no deemed disposal rule** - tax is due only when you actually sell, the same realization-based principle most investors expect by default. Revenue treats a US-listed ETF this way when it determines the fund is not "equivalent" to an Irish fund under the relevant legislation - a determination that generally favors direct US-domiciled ETFs and individual US stocks bought through a broker like Interactive Brokers.

**The practical gap:** 38% with a forced 8-year taxable event vs. 33% with tax due only on an actual sale. For a long-term buy-and-hold investor, avoiding the 8-year deemed disposal event alone - separate from the 5-percentage-point rate difference - can be worth more than the headline rate gap suggests, since it preserves compounding on the full unrealized gain rather than forcing a partial cash-out for tax every eight years.

**What this doesn't change:** dividends from a US-listed ETF or direct US stock are still taxed at your marginal income tax rate in Ireland (not at the exit tax or CGT rate), a detail worth factoring in separately from the capital gains comparison above.

## The Treaty: 15% on Dividends

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) | Direct Corporate (10%+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** | 5% (or lower for qualifying parent-subsidiary holdings) |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only | Residence country only |

*Sources: US-Ireland Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text; PwC and Revenue.ie guidance (2026).*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) filed with your broker. Without it, the full 30% default applies from the first dividend payment.

**As noted above, dividends from US stocks are taxed in Ireland at your marginal income tax rate**, not a flat rate - with a foreign tax credit available for the 15% US withholding already paid, capped at the Irish tax otherwise due on that income.

---

## Reporting: Self-Assessment for US Holdings

Direct US stocks and non-equivalent US ETFs held through a foreign broker generally require self-assessment: calculating and declaring CGT liability yourself (with Ireland's CGT payment and filing deadlines, which differ from the standard income tax return timeline), and reporting dividend income on your annual return. A domestic Irish broker or fund provider may handle more of this automatically for Irish/EU products; a foreign broker holding US-listed instruments generally does not interface with Revenue on your behalf the way a local provider would.

## Broker Access

Both Interactive Brokers and eToro are confirmed to accept Irish residents for account opening - IBKR in particular is a common choice specifically because it provides direct access to US-listed stocks and ETFs, the category that can access the lower CGT-with-no-deemed-disposal treatment described above.

## Practical Checklist

**Irish resident investing in US stocks:**
- [ ] Confirm W-8BEN is on file with your broker; verify 15%, not 30%, on dividend statements
- [ ] Before buying an ETF, check its domicile (ISIN prefix) - Irish/EU-domiciled funds face the 38% exit tax and 8-year deemed disposal; US-listed ETFs and direct shares generally face 33% CGT with no deemed disposal
- [ ] Track your 8-year deemed disposal dates for any Irish/EU-domiciled funds you do hold
- [ ] Remember dividends from US holdings are taxed at your marginal income tax rate, not the CGT or exit tax rate
- [ ] Self-assess and file CGT and income tax declarations for US holdings, since a foreign broker doesn't report to Revenue on your behalf
- [ ] Claim the foreign tax credit for US dividend withholding, using Form 1042-S as documentation

## Bottom Line

The treaty rate is standard and solved once W-8BEN is filed: 15% on dividends. The decision that actually shapes an Irish investor's long-term outcome is fund selection - Irish and EU-domiciled ETFs carry the 38% exit tax and a forced taxable event every eight years, while direct US stocks and US-listed ETFs can access ordinary 33% CGT with tax due only when you actually sell. For a US-market-focused, buy-and-hold investor, that structural difference matters more than almost any other single decision covered in this guide.
