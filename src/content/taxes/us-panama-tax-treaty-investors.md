Uruguay just brought foreign capital gains into its tax net for 2026, closing a gap in its own territorial system. Panama's territorial system hasn't seen an equivalent expansion: **dividends, capital gains, interest, and other returns from assets held outside Panama remain fully exempt from Panamanian tax**, for residents and non-residents alike, with no recent narrowing of that exemption. There's also no US-Panama income tax treaty - only a 2010 Tax Information Exchange Agreement that shares information but grants no rate relief - so full 30% US withholding applies regardless.

This guide covers both pieces.

---

## No Treaty: Full 30% US Withholding

The US and Panama have never signed an income tax treaty. The two countries do have a **Tax Information Exchange Agreement**, in force since November 30, 2010, which lets the IRS and Panamanian authorities share tax information - but it provides no reduced withholding rate. Every US dividend paid to a Panama-resident investor is withheld at the full **30% statutory rate**.

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing.** It won't bring the rate below 30%, but it certifies your non-US status to your broker and avoids the risk of default backup withholding treatment on an undocumented account.

---

## Panama's Side: A Clean, Unmodified Territorial Exemption

Panama taxes individuals only on Panama-source income - a straightforward territorial principle with none of the foreign-passive-income carve-backs some neighboring countries in this series have introduced. **Foreign-source dividends, capital gains, interest, and other investment returns - a US stock's dividends and sale proceeds included - are not taxable in Panama at all**, regardless of how long you've lived there or how large the amounts involved.

**This applies uniformly to residents and non-residents alike** - there's no separate, narrower exemption reserved for new arrivals or subject to a holding-period condition. A Panama-resident investor holding US stocks through Interactive Brokers or eToro owes Panama nothing on either the dividends or any capital gain from selling.

**One structural note worth flagging clearly:** this is Panama's side of the equation only. **US citizens and green card holders remain subject to US worldwide taxation regardless of Panamanian residency** - Panama's territorial exemption doesn't change US filing obligations for US persons; it's relevant here specifically to non-US-person investors who are Panama tax residents.

## The Treaty Gap and What It Means Practically

Because there's no treaty, and because Panama doesn't tax the income in the first place, there's no double-taxation problem to solve here - unlike most no-treaty countries in this series, where a domestic tax layer stacks on top of the US withholding. **For a Panama-resident, non-US-person investor, the 30% US withholding is the entire tax cost of the arrangement**, with nothing further owed to Panama.

**Worked example - a $2,000 US dividend:**
- US withholding at 30% (no treaty): $600
- Panamanian tax on the same dividend: $0
- Total tax paid: $600 on a $2,000 dividend - the full amount is the US side alone

## Bottom Line

There's no US-Panama tax treaty - only an information-sharing TIEA - so the full 30% US withholding applies with no reduction available. On the Panamanian side, there's genuinely nothing further to account for: dividends, interest, and capital gains from a foreign brokerage account remain fully outside Panama's tax system, with no equivalent to the foreign-income expansion some neighboring territorial-tax countries have recently introduced. For a non-US-person investor, the 30% US withholding is the whole story.
