If you've read more than one country guide on this site, you've seen a version of the same sentence: "a foreign tax credit is generally available for the US withholding already paid, capped at the [home country] tax otherwise due on that income." It appears in almost every guide where the investor's home country also taxes the same dividend the US already withheld from.

This article is the mechanism behind that sentence - what a foreign tax credit actually is, how the cap works, and the specific situations where it does not fully offset what you paid the IRS.

---

## The Problem the Credit Solves

When a non-US investor receives a US dividend, the IRS withholds tax at the source - 30% by default, or a reduced treaty rate (commonly 10-15%) if [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is filed. That is the US side.

Most investors' home countries also tax worldwide income, which typically includes that same dividend. Without any relief mechanism, the same dollar of income would be taxed twice - once by the US at the source, and again by the home country on the full amount. The foreign tax credit exists specifically to prevent this.

---

## How the Credit Actually Works

The standard mechanism, used in some form by most countries with a functioning tax system and consistent with the OECD Model Tax Convention (Article 23), works like this:

1. You receive a $1,000 US dividend.
2. The US withholds tax at the applicable rate - say 15% under a treaty, or $150.
3. You report the full $1,000 as income on your home-country tax return.
4. Your home country calculates the tax it would otherwise charge on that $1,000 at its own rate.
5. You credit the $150 already paid to the US against that home-country liability.
6. You pay your home country only the difference, if any.

**The critical detail: the credit is capped.** You cannot credit more foreign tax than your home country would have charged on that same income in the first place. If your home country's tax on that $1,000 works out to $190, you credit the full $150 and pay $40 to your home country. If your home country's tax on that $1,000 is only $100, you can only credit $100 of the $150 you paid the US - the remaining $50 is generally not refundable or usable elsewhere, unless your country allows carrying the excess forward or back (rules vary significantly by country).

---

## Worked Example: When the Credit Absorbs Fully

A Japanese investor receives a $1,000 US dividend with 10% US withholding under the treaty ($100). Japan's flat rate on this income, outside a tax-advantaged account, is 20.315% ($203). Since Japan's own rate exceeds the US withholding, the full $100 credit is absorbed cleanly, and the investor pays Japan the remaining $103. Total combined tax: $203 - Japan's rate applied once, with the US withholding fully credited.

This is the common, cleaner outcome across most of the country guides on this site: when the home country's rate on investment income exceeds the US treaty withholding rate, the credit typically absorbs the full US tax with no leftover.

---

## When the Credit Does Not Fully Offset

The reverse situation - where the home country's tax on that income is lower than what the US withheld - is less common but does happen, and it is worth understanding because it means real money is left on the table.

**This can happen when:**
- The investor's country has a low or zero rate on the specific type of investment income involved (some countries tax capital gains preferentially or exempt certain income categories)
- The investor has no treaty with the US and faces the full 30% default, which can exceed even a moderately-taxed home country's own rate
- The credit calculation is done on a category-by-category or country-by-country basis (common in many systems) and excess credits from one high-withholding country cannot offset a shortfall on another

**What typically happens to the excess:** in most systems, it is simply lost for that tax year unless the country's rules specifically allow carrying it forward to a future year or back to a prior one. This varies enough by country that a blanket answer is not reliable - confirming the carryforward/carryback rules with a local tax advisor is worth doing if you are in a no-treaty or low-tax country and expect this situation to recur.

---

## Why the Credit Isn't Automatic

Unlike the US withholding itself - which your broker applies automatically once [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is on file - the credit on the home-country side almost always requires action from you:

- **Documentation:** you generally need to show proof of the US tax actually paid, most commonly your broker's Form 1042-S (issued by March 15 each year), to support the credit claim on your home-country return.
- **Declaration:** the underlying foreign dividend income typically needs to be declared as part of your worldwide income filing, not just the credit itself - you cannot claim a credit for tax paid on income you never reported.
- **Filing:** in some countries, brokers or domestic financial institutions handle this kind of reconciliation automatically for local investments but not for foreign ones - a pattern that shows up repeatedly across the country guides on this site (Poland's PIT-38, Mexico's self-assessment requirement, and others).

## How This Interacts With Different Income Types on This Site

**The credit mechanism described above applies specifically to dividend withholding, but the same general logic extends to other US-source income types covered elsewhere on this site**, with important variations. Interest income qualifying for the portfolio interest exemption - covered in our [bond ETF guide](/index-funds/bond-etfs-compared-bnd-agg-tlt/) - generally faces no US withholding to begin with, meaning there's no foreign tax credit to claim on that portion at all, since nothing was withheld in the first place. Options premium, covered in our [options trading tax treatment guide](/taxes/options-trading-tax-treatment-non-us-residents/), is generally not subject to US withholding for a non-resident alien either, following the standard NRA capital gains framework - again, no credit mechanism needed since there's no US tax paid to credit.

**This means the foreign tax credit mechanism is most relevant specifically to equity dividend income**, the one major category where non-resident aliens do face meaningful, routine US withholding - worth keeping in mind when reading this site's broader tax content, since not every US-source income type generates the same credit-eligible withholding dividends do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the foreign tax credit apply to capital gains as well as dividends?**
Since non-resident aliens generally aren't subject to US capital gains tax in the first place (the standard rule covered throughout this site's [main dividend withholding guide](/taxes/taxes-us-dividends-foreign-investors/)), there's typically no US tax withheld on capital gains to credit against your home country's tax - your home country taxes the gain (if it does) without any US-side offset needed, since none was paid.

**What happens if my broker's Form 1042-S contains an error - can I still claim the credit?**
An incorrect 1042-S should be addressed directly with your broker to obtain a corrected form before relying on it for your home-country tax filing, since claiming a credit based on inaccurate documentation can create its own complications with your home country's tax authority.

**Does the foreign tax credit mechanism differ for a tax-advantaged account in my home country versus a standard taxable brokerage account?**
Potentially significantly - some home-country tax-advantaged accounts (pension wrappers, ISA-style accounts, and similar) may not generate a home-country tax liability on the dividend at all, in which case there's no domestic tax to credit the US withholding against, and the US withholding becomes a real, unrecoverable cost specific to holding US securities inside that particular tax-advantaged wrapper - worth checking specifically for any tax-advantaged account you're considering holding US dividend-paying securities within.

**Is the foreign tax credit the only mechanism for avoiding double taxation, or are there alternatives?**
Some countries use an exemption method instead of a credit method for certain income categories - exempting foreign-source income from domestic tax entirely rather than taxing it and then crediting foreign tax paid - a structurally different mechanism with different practical implications, worth confirming which approach your specific country uses rather than assuming the credit method (the more commonly described mechanism in this guide) universally applies.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Keep your broker's Form 1042-S every year - it is your primary documentation for any foreign tax credit claim
- [ ] Confirm whether your home country requires you to declare the gross dividend (before US withholding) or the net amount received - this affects both the credit calculation and how the cap is applied
- [ ] Understand that the credit is capped at your home country's own tax on that income, not automatically equal to what the US withheld
- [ ] If you are in a no-treaty country facing 30% US withholding, check specifically whether your home country's rate on that income is lower - if so, some of what the US withheld may not be recoverable
- [ ] Confirm your country's specific carryforward or carryback rules for excess credits, since this varies significantly and is not safe to assume either way

---

## Bottom Line

The foreign tax credit is the mechanism that keeps most international investors from being taxed twice on the same dividend - but it is a credit against your home country's tax on that income, capped at what your home country would have charged in the first place, not a guarantee of a dollar-for-dollar refund of everything the US withheld. In most of the country-specific situations covered on this site, where home-country rates on investment income meet or exceed the US treaty withholding rate, the credit works cleanly. The exception - a no-treaty country with a lower domestic rate than the 30% US default - is the scenario worth double-checking rather than assuming works out evenly.
