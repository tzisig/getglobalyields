Venezuela is a genuine outlier among the sanctioned or sanctions-adjacent countries in this series: **the US-Venezuela income tax treaty is still formally in force**, capping dividend withholding at 15% for portfolio investors - unlike Russia, where the treaty's core provisions are suspended. That said, Venezuela is not a normal case either. US sanctions are **targeted rather than comprehensive** - unlike Iran, Venezuela isn't under a full embargo - but roughly 150-200 Venezuela-related individuals and entities remain on OFAC's Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list, and the country is mid-transition following **Nicolás Maduro's ouster on January 3, 2026**, with sectors of the economy being selectively, conditionally reopened since.

This guide states plainly what's known and what genuinely isn't yet.

---

## The Treaty Is Real: 15% on Portfolio Dividends

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Portfolio) | Treaty Rate (Qualifying Corporate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **15%** | **5%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only | - |

*Sources: US-Venezuela Income Tax Convention; IRS treaty text and treaty-rate references.*

Claiming the 15% rate requires Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) filed with your broker. **This part of the picture is genuinely straightforward and unaffected by the sanctions situation** - the treaty itself has not been suspended, unlike the US-Russia treaty's core provisions.

---

## The Part That Isn't Straightforward: Sanctions and Broker Access

**US sanctions on Venezuela are targeted, not comprehensive** - they're built around specific designated individuals, entities, and (until recently) sectors like oil, gas, and petrochemicals, administered through a series of OFAC general and specific licenses, rather than a blanket prohibition on transactions with Venezuela as a country.

**Since January 2026**, following Maduro's removal from power, the US has begun a structured, conditional reopening of Venezuela's energy and minerals sectors to American and allied companies - a real, significant shift, but one OFAC itself describes as conditional rather than a wholesale lifting of sanctions. **The SDN list still includes an estimated 150-200 Venezuela-related designations**, and any transaction connected to a listed individual or entity remains prohibited regardless of the broader sectoral easing.

**What this means for an ordinary individual investor:** US persons - which generally includes US-regulated brokers - may not transact with anyone on the SDN list, must block any holdings connected to blocked persons, and need OFAC authorization for anything falling outside a general license's scope. For most ordinary Venezuelans with no connection to a designated individual or entity, this legal framework alone shouldn't prevent opening a personal brokerage account - **but broker risk tolerance and compliance policy is a separate, practical question from the legal sanctions framework**, and international brokers have historically varied widely in how willing they are to accept Venezuelan clients at all, given the compliance burden of screening in a fast-changing environment.

**This guide will not attempt to tell you whether your specific situation is affected**, and does not provide guidance on working around any sanctions restriction. If your circumstances involve any connection to a government entity, state-owned company, or anyone who might be designated, or if you're uncertain, **consult a sanctions-specialist lawyer before proceeding** - this is genuinely not a question a general investing guide can safely answer for you, especially given how recently and rapidly the situation has changed.

## Venezuela's Domestic Tax Picture

Venezuela taxes residents on worldwide income. Dividends are subject to a **34%** domestic withholding rate on the positive difference between book and fiscal income - a genuinely steep figure, though Venezuela's own treaties with various countries can reduce this in specific cases, and a credit against Venezuelan tax is generally available for foreign tax already paid (such as the US withholding). Capital gains generally fall under ordinary income tax rates, except gains from shares specifically listed on the Venezuelan stock exchange, which get a separate 1% rate that doesn't apply to US-listed holdings.

**Given the practical uncertainty around banking and currency access described above, the precise domestic tax calculation is, realistically, a secondary concern for most readers right now** compared to first confirming whether you can open and fund an account at all.

## Bottom Line

The US-Venezuela tax treaty itself is real, active, and offers a genuinely favorable 15% dividend rate - a surprise to readers expecting Venezuela's situation to mirror Russia's suspended treaty. What makes Venezuela different from a routine country in this series isn't the tax rate; it's that targeted OFAC sanctions, an extensive SDN list, and a fast-moving political transition since Maduro's January 2026 ouster mean practical broker and banking access genuinely can't be stated with confidence in a general guide. Confirm your specific situation - and get sanctions-specific legal advice if there's any doubt - before anything else here matters.
