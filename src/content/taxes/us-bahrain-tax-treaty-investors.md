There's no US-Bahrain income tax treaty - the two countries have never signed one, so the full **30% US statutory withholding** applies to every US dividend paid to a Bahrain-resident investor. Bahrain's own side is simple: **no personal income tax, no capital gains tax, and no withholding tax** on dividends, interest, or royalties, for anyone resident there. The detail worth knowing beyond the tax picture: the **Bahraini dinar has been fixed to the US dollar since 1980**, at roughly **1 BHD ≈ $2.65**, with no interim devaluations - one of the longest-standing, most stable currency pegs in the world, which matters directly for anyone funding a USD-denominated brokerage account from Bahrain.

This guide covers all three pieces.

---

## No Treaty: Full 30% US Withholding

With no income tax treaty between the US and Bahrain, there's no treaty article to reduce the default rate. Every US dividend paid to a Bahrain-resident investor is withheld at the full **30% statutory rate**.

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing.** It won't bring the rate below 30%, but it certifies your non-US status to your broker and avoids the risk of default backup withholding treatment on an undocumented account.

---

## Bahrain's Side: 0% Across the Board

Bahrain imposes no personal income tax, no capital gains tax, and no withholding tax on dividends, interest, or royalties for individuals. This applies uniformly - there's no distinction between Bahraini nationals and foreign residents for this purpose. **The entire tax cost of investing in US stocks from Bahrain is the 30% US withholding itself** - there's no Bahraini layer to add or credit against.

## Why the 1980 USD Peg Actually Matters Here

Most guides in this series need to address currency risk somewhere - a domestic currency that moves against the US dollar changes the real cost of funding a brokerage account and the real value of what you withdraw later. **Bahrain is one of the few countries in this series where that consideration essentially disappears.**

The Bahraini dinar has been pegged to the US dollar at a fixed rate since 1980, currently maintained at approximately **1 BHD ≈ $2.65**, with the Central Bank of Bahrain having held that rate through more than four decades without a devaluation. Converting BHD to USD to fund an Interactive Brokers or eToro account, and later converting USD proceeds back to BHD, carries essentially none of the exchange-rate uncertainty that investors in floating-currency countries elsewhere in this series need to plan around.

**This isn't unique to Bahrain among Gulf states** - Saudi Arabia and the UAE maintain similarly long-standing USD pegs - but it's worth stating plainly for a Bahrain-resident investor: currency risk is one variable you can largely take off the table when budgeting for US stock investing.

## Bottom Line

There's no US-Bahrain tax treaty, so the full 30% US withholding applies with no reduction available - W-8BEN still matters for documentation, not for a lower rate. On the Bahraini side, there's nothing to add: 0% personal income tax, 0% capital gains tax, 0% withholding tax. The genuinely useful detail for a Bahrain-resident investor is currency-related rather than tax-related - the dinar's unbroken USD peg since 1980 means funding and later withdrawing from a US brokerage account carries close to none of the exchange-rate uncertainty investors elsewhere in this series have to plan around.
