There's no US-Guatemala income tax treaty - Guatemala currently has no comprehensive double tax treaties in force with any country - so the full **30% US statutory withholding** applies to every US dividend paid to a Guatemala-resident investor. Guatemala's own system runs on a strict **territorial basis**: income tax applies only to Guatemala-source income, and **foreign-source dividends and capital gains - a US stock's returns included - are 100% exempt from Guatemalan tax.** Separately, the Guatemalan quetzal is genuinely notable among Latin American currencies for its stability, which matters directly for anyone converting funds to invest in US stocks.

Here is how each of those plays out.

---

## Guatemala Is Not on the Treaty List

There is no US-Guatemala income tax treaty, which means no reduced rate to claim and no article to cite. Every US dividend paid to a Guatemalan resident is withheld at **30%**.

**Filing Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/) is still worth doing.** The rate is unaffected. What the form prevents is your account being treated as undocumented and backup-withheld.

---

## Guatemala's Territorial System: 0% on Foreign Investment Income

Guatemala applies the territoriality principle cleanly: income tax is levied on Guatemala-source income only, and **foreign-source income is not taxed at all** - a category that explicitly includes investment dividends from abroad and capital gains realized outside the country. For comparison, Guatemala-source capital gains are taxed at a flat 10% - but that rate simply doesn't reach a US stock sale executed through a foreign broker, since the gain isn't Guatemala-source income in the first place.

**This means the entire tax cost of investing in US stocks from Guatemala is the 30% US withholding itself** - there's no Guatemalan layer to add or credit against.

## The Quetzal: Unusually Stable for the Region

Currency conversion is a real, practical concern for investors in several countries covered in this series - but the Guatemalan quetzal (GTQ) is a genuine outlier for stability among Latin American currencies. Managed by the Banco de Guatemala, the quetzal has traded within a notably tight band against the US dollar for years - broadly in the **Q7.60 to Q7.80** range recently, with even shorter-term swings staying within a narrow band.

**What supports that stability:** Guatemala's central bank holds robust international reserves (reported above **$20 billion**), and the country receives a steady, large inflow of family remittances - also exceeding **$20 billion annually** - which together give the Banco de Guatemala real capacity to manage the currency rather than let it swing freely. This isn't an official fixed peg the way Bahrain's or Saudi Arabia's currencies work, but the practical result for an investor converting GTQ to USD to fund a brokerage account is meaningfully lower exchange-rate uncertainty than in many peer economies in the region.

---

## What the Territorial Rule Is Worth, in Money

Guatemala's exemption sounds abstract until it is priced. Take a **$1,000** US dividend and a **$8,000** gain on a US share sold through a foreign broker:

- **US side:** $300 withheld on the dividend at the 30% statutory rate. Nothing withheld on the gain - the US does not tax capital gains of non-resident individuals on portfolio securities.
- **Guatemalan side:** nothing on either. Foreign-source investment income sits outside the tax base entirely.
- **Total tax:** **$300**, all of it American.

The effective rate on the dividend is exactly 30%, and on the capital gain it is **zero**. For contrast, that same $8,000 gain realised on a Guatemala-source asset would attract the domestic 10% - $800 - which is precisely the point of the distinction: the location of the asset, not your residence, decides it.

**What that means practically:** the entire tax-planning question for a Guatemalan investor is the 30% withholding, and the only lever on it is which US securities you hold, not which forms you file. Funds that distribute little and grow instead simply meet the withholding less often.

## Guatemala: What to Sort Out First

**Working through it as a Guatemalan resident:**
- [ ] File W-8BEN regardless - the 30% does not move, and the form's purpose here is keeping the account out of backup withholding
- [ ] Expect 30% US withholding on every dividend payment, with no treaty-based reduction available
- [ ] Don't expect a Guatemalan-side tax filing obligation on US dividend or capital gains income - Guatemala's territorial system exempts both
- [ ] Budget with reasonable confidence in GTQ-USD conversion stability, though not the certainty of an officially fixed peg
- [ ] Confirm current account-opening requirements with Interactive Brokers, eToro, or XTB before applying

## What This Means for Guatemalan Investors

There's no US-Guatemala tax treaty, so the full 30% US withholding applies with no reduction available - W-8BEN earns its place as documentation, not as a discount. Guatemala's clean territorial system means there's no domestic layer on top: foreign dividends and capital gains are fully exempt. The genuinely distinctive detail here is the quetzal itself - one of the more stable currencies in Latin America, backed by strong reserves and steady remittance flows, which takes real currency risk off the table for a Guatemalan investor funding a US brokerage account, even without an official dollar peg.
