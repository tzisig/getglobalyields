Every REIT article on this site has referenced interest rate sensitivity somewhere - Realty Income's bond-like predictability, AGNC's leveraged spread compression, Welltower's growth-versus-yield trade-off. This article is the mechanism underneath all of it: the actual reason rates move REIT prices, with real numbers, and an honest look at how much less mechanical that relationship actually is than the common "REITs fall when rates rise" shorthand suggests.

---

## The Mechanism: Cap Rates, Not Vibes

**A commercial property's value is calculated, mechanically, as net operating income divided by a capitalization rate: Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate.** A lower cap rate means investors are willing to pay more per dollar of income the property generates; a higher cap rate means they demand more income per dollar paid - a higher yield to compensate for perceived risk. Because interest rates set the baseline cost of capital and the return available on safer alternatives like bonds, rising rates theoretically push cap rates up too, which mechanically pushes property values down even if the underlying NOI hasn't changed at all. That's the entire chain of logic behind "REITs are rate-sensitive" - not sentiment, a specific valuation formula.

---

## The Correlation Is Real, But Less Consistent Than the Shorthand Suggests

**The long-run relationship is genuinely strong: REIT yields and the 10-year Treasury yield have shown a 77% monthly correlation from 1978 through mid-2018** - a real, well-documented, decades-spanning relationship, not a recent or coincidental one. But the honest complication: looking at daily data since 2000, the correlation between REIT total returns and Treasury yield changes has actually been *positive* on 53.3% of days and *negative* on 46.7% - close enough to a coin flip that "REITs always fall when rates rise" overstates a relationship that, day to day, flips direction almost as often as it holds. The multi-decade correlation is real; the day-to-day mechanical certainty implied by common financial media shorthand isn't.

---

## Some REIT Sectors Are Measurably More Sensitive Than Others

**This is where the mechanism gets genuinely precise, and it connects directly to several other articles on this site.** Interest rate "beta" measures how much a REIT sector's yield moves for a given move in the 10-year Treasury - and triple net lease REITs, the category covered in our [Realty Income guide](/real-estate/net-lease-reits-realty-income-explained/), carry a measured beta around 1.5: for every 1-percentage-point rise in the 10-year Treasury yield, triple net lease REIT yields have historically risen roughly 1.5 percentage points, implying a proportionally larger price decline than the rate move alone would suggest. That's not a coincidence relative to what the net lease article covered qualitatively - a REIT whose income is a long-term fixed rent stream, priced and compared directly against bond yields, is exactly the kind of asset this beta measurement would predict to be highly sensitive.

**Not every REIT sector behaves the same way, and the exceptions are real too.** Hotel REITs have historically shown *negative* interest rate betas - moving up when rates fall, the opposite pattern from net lease REITs - reflecting that hotel income is short-duration and repriced constantly (room rates reset nightly), behaving more like an operating business tracking the economic cycle than a bond-like fixed income stream. The mechanism isn't uniform across "REITs" as a category; it depends specifically on how fixed or variable the underlying income stream actually is, which is exactly why the healthcare REIT guide on this site draws such a sharp distinction between Welltower's operating-heavy SHOP portfolio and a fixed-rent net lease structure.

---

## How This Plays Out Across Every REIT Type Covered on This Site

**Net lease REITs (Realty Income, NNN REIT):** highest measured sensitivity, per the 1.5 beta figure above - long, fixed-rent contracts valued like bond substitutes.

**Mortgage REITs (AGNC):** sensitivity runs even more directly and severely, covered in our [mortgage REIT guide](/real-estate/mortgage-reits-vs-equity-reits-agnc-explained/) - not just a cap-rate valuation effect, but a direct hit to the borrowing-cost side of a leveraged spread business, amplified by 7x-plus leverage.

**Healthcare REITs with heavy RIDEA/operating exposure (Welltower):** lower direct rate sensitivity than net lease, since a meaningful share of returns comes from operating performance (occupancy, per-resident fees) rather than a fixed contractual spread against bond yields - covered in our [healthcare REIT guide](/real-estate/healthcare-reits-welltower-ventas-compared/).

**Non-traded REITs:** the mechanism still applies to the underlying property values, but shows up differently - through periodic appraisal-based NAV adjustments rather than a daily traded price, meaning the rate sensitivity is real but slower and less visible day to day than for an exchange-listed REIT, a distinct wrinkle covered in our [non-traded REIT guide](/real-estate/non-traded-private-reits-explained/).

**International REIT ETFs (VNQI, RWX):** subject to the same cap-rate mechanism, but driven by each underlying country's own interest rate environment rather than US Treasury yields specifically - a genuinely separate rate-sensitivity question from anything else on this list, covered in our [VNQI vs RWX guide](/real-estate/vnqi-rwx-international-reit-etfs/).

## The Magnitude Can Be Large

**One widely-cited analysis found that roughly 79% of REITs' relative performance against the S&P 500 could be explained by 10-year Treasury yield movements over the period studied, with every 100-basis-point move in the Treasury yield associated with a roughly 1,170-basis-point swing in REITs' relative performance against the broader market.** That's a genuinely large multiplier - a relatively modest 1-percentage-point rate move translating into a double-digit percentage swing in how REITs perform relative to stocks generally. Figures like this vary by measurement period and shouldn't be treated as a fixed, universal constant, but they illustrate why rate sensitivity gets discussed as a defining REIT characteristic rather than a minor secondary factor.

---

## What How Interest Rates Move REIT Prices Comes Down To

REIT interest rate sensitivity is a real, mechanically-grounded relationship - rising rates push cap rates up, which mechanically pushes property valuations down, and the 77% long-run correlation with Treasury yields reflects that real link. But the relationship is sector-specific (net lease REITs far more sensitive than hotel REITs), inconsistent day to day (correlation direction flips close to half the time), and expressed through different mechanisms depending on structure (mortgage REITs through leveraged borrowing costs, non-traded REITs through periodic NAV appraisals rather than daily price). Understanding which specific REIT type you're holding, and through which of these mechanisms rates actually reach it, matters more than a blanket "REITs go down when rates go up" rule that's true on average and wrong close to half the time on any given day.
