Section 1031 of the tax code lets an investor sell US real property and defer the capital gains tax by rolling the proceeds into a replacement property - and yes, a non-resident alien can use it exactly like a US investor can. The statute itself doesn't distinguish by citizenship. What does distinguish a foreign investor's exchange from a domestic one is a real, underdiscussed timing problem: the standard 1031 clock and the paperwork a foreign seller needs to avoid a 15% withholding hit on sale proceeds run on two different tracks that have to be sequenced correctly, and most 1031 guides are written entirely from a US investor's perspective without mentioning it.

---

## The Core Mechanics - Same for Everyone

**Since the 2017 tax reform, Section 1031 applies only to real property held for investment or business use** - personal property like equipment or vehicles no longer qualifies, and property held primarily for resale (a flip) doesn't either. Two deadlines govern every exchange, foreign or domestic: **45 calendar days** from closing on the relinquished property to identify replacement property in writing, and **180 calendar days** total (not 45 plus 180 - 180 total) to close on the replacement, or your tax return's due date if earlier. Both are calendar days with no extensions for weekends, holidays, or financing delays.

**A Qualified Intermediary (QI) - an independent third party - is legally required to hold the sale proceeds throughout the exchange.** If you ever have actual or "constructive receipt" of the funds yourself, even briefly, the exchange is disqualified entirely. Identifying replacement property follows one of three rules: the **3-property rule** (identify up to three properties of any value, acquire at least one), the **200% rule** (identify unlimited properties if their combined value doesn't exceed 200% of what you sold), or the **95% rule** (identify unlimited value, but you must actually acquire at least 95% of what you identified). A standard forward exchange typically costs **$750-$1,500** in QI fees, more for multi-property exchanges, on top of the escrow, title, and legal costs any real estate sale carries.

## Where a Foreign Investor's Exchange Actually Diverges

**The divergence isn't in Section 1031 itself - it's in FIRPTA.** The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act requires a buyer to withhold 15% of the gross sale amount on a foreign seller's disposition of US real property (lower tiers apply to owner-occupied residences under specific price thresholds), regardless of whether the seller actually owes tax on a gain, and regardless of whether that seller intends to roll the proceeds into a 1031 exchange. Left unaddressed, that 15% withholding ties up capital that a delayed exchange needs available to acquire the replacement property, on a deadline that doesn't wait for withholding to get sorted out.

**The mechanism that fixes this: a Form 8288-B Withholding Certificate, filed with the IRS before the relinquished-property closing**, along with a copy of the replacement-property contract. The IRS treats the application like an abbreviated tax return estimating the actual gain and liability, and can approve reduced or zero withholding when a legitimate exchange is in progress - freeing the capital that would otherwise sit tied up. Official guidance cites roughly 90 days for processing, though real-world times reported by practitioners range from about 30 to 90 days depending on how complete the initial filing is; the buyer can hold off remitting the withheld amount until 20 days after the IRS rules on the application.

**There's a narrower alternative for simultaneous exchanges specifically**: if the exchange closes simultaneously with zero "boot" (no cash or debt relief received), no withholding is required at all if the foreign seller signs a FIRPTA Notice of Non-Recognition Transfer before closing. This doesn't help the far more common delayed (forward) exchange structure, where the 8288-B path above is what actually matters.

**One more layer some investors miss entirely: several states impose their own separate withholding on foreign sellers, on top of federal FIRPTA.** California is the clearest example - a 3.33% state withholding under Form 593, an entirely independent statute from the federal 15%, that can apply simultaneously. If your relinquished property is in a state with its own nonresident-seller withholding regime, budget for both layers, not just the federal one.

## The Real Squeeze: Four Deadlines Stacking on Top of Each Other

**This is the part most 1031 content never addresses, because it only applies to a foreign exchanger:** completing the Form 8288-B application generally requires an ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number), obtained via Form W-7 - and ITIN processing alone can take anywhere from about six weeks to several months. That has to happen, then the 8288-B application has to be filed and processed (another ~30-90 days), all while the unmovable 45-day identification clock and 180-day completion clock from Section 1031 itself are already running in parallel, not waiting for the ITIN or the withholding certificate to clear first.

**The practical implication: a foreign investor planning a 1031 exchange needs to start the ITIN and withholding-certificate process well before listing the relinquished property**, not after finding a buyer. A domestic investor can reasonably start their 1031 planning once a sale is under contract; a foreign investor working backward from the same 45-day identification deadline is often already behind if they wait that long, because the ITIN and 8288-B steps alone can consume most or all of the time a domestic investor would spend simply searching for replacement property.

## Delaware Statutory Trusts as Replacement Property

**A DST lets an investor buy fractional, passive ownership in institutional-grade real estate and use that interest as valid 1031 replacement property** - a common choice for an investor who wants to exit active property management without giving up the tax deferral. Typical minimum investments run **$25,000-$100,000**, and DSTs are generally sold as Regulation D private placements, which usually means accredited-investor status is required (net worth over $1 million excluding a primary residence, or income over $200,000 individually/$300,000 jointly for the two preceding years).

**Whether a non-US investor can actually invest in a specific DST offering is a genuine open question this article can't answer generally - it depends on the individual sponsor's own offering terms and securities-law structure, not a blanket rule.** Some Reg D offerings extend to foreign investors, others restrict participation by residency or apply additional cross-border securities compliance; confirm directly with a specific DST sponsor and, ideally, your own securities counsel before assuming a DST is available to you as a foreign exchanger, rather than treating it as automatically accessible the way it is for a US investor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the IRS treat a foreign investor's 1031 exchange any differently once it's actually underway, beyond the FIRPTA withholding issue?**
No - once the withholding question is resolved (or a certificate is approved), the exchange mechanics themselves (identification rules, the 45/180-day clocks, the Qualified Intermediary requirement) apply identically regardless of the investor's residency or citizenship status.

**Can I use a Qualified Intermediary based outside the US for a foreign investor's exchange?**
The QI's location isn't the constraint - the requirement is an independent third party that meets the IRS's QI standards. In practice, most exchanges on US property use a US-based QI already familiar with the FIRPTA/8288-B sequencing described above, since a QI unfamiliar with the foreign-investor timeline is a real risk to introduce into an already deadline-heavy process.

**If I miss the ITIN/8288-B timeline, is my exchange simply dead, or is there a fallback?**
The 45-day identification and 180-day completion deadlines themselves don't extend for FIRPTA paperwork delays. If withholding certificate approval doesn't come through in time, the practical fallback is typically closing with the 15% (or applicable state-plus-federal) withholding actually applied, then reclaiming any excess withheld once your actual tax liability is calculated on your return - a real cost in tied-up capital, which is exactly why starting the ITIN/8288-B process early matters so much more for a foreign exchanger than for a domestic one.

**Do I need a US bank account to receive exchange proceeds or hold funds during the process?**
The Qualified Intermediary holds the funds throughout the exchange, not you directly - so the account question is really about the QI's own banking setup, and separately about how you'll eventually receive distributions from the replacement property, which is a broader question covered in our [do you need a US bank account guide](/currency-banking/do-you-need-us-bank-account-to-invest/).

## The 1031 Exchanges for Foreign Investors and FIRPTA Checklist

- [ ] Confirm your relinquished property qualifies as real property held for investment or business use, not personal property or resale inventory
- [ ] Start your ITIN application (Form W-7) and Form 8288-B withholding certificate process well before listing the property, not after finding a buyer
- [ ] Engage a Qualified Intermediary experienced specifically with foreign-seller exchanges, given the added FIRPTA sequencing
- [ ] Check whether your property's state imposes its own separate nonresident-seller withholding on top of federal FIRPTA
- [ ] If considering a DST as replacement property, confirm directly with the sponsor whether non-US investors are eligible for that specific offering before counting on it as a fallback
- [ ] Track the 45-day identification and 180-day completion deadlines as fixed regardless of how your FIRPTA paperwork is progressing

## Closing the Loop on 1031 Exchanges for Foreign Investors and FIRPTA

A non-resident alien can legally complete a 1031 exchange on US real property under exactly the same rules as a US investor - the statute doesn't distinguish by citizenship, and once the withholding question is resolved, the mechanics are identical. The real difference is entirely about sequencing: FIRPTA's 15% withholding requirement, and the ITIN plus Form 8288-B process needed to reduce or eliminate it, run on a timeline that has to be started early and managed alongside the unmovable 45-day and 180-day 1031 clocks - not after them. Foreign investors who treat this as "the same exchange with extra paperwork" and start that paperwork early tend to get through it fine; those who plan on a purely domestic timeline are the ones who end up losing capital to withholding they could have avoided.
