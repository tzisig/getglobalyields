Data center REITs get talked about as a single AI-boom trade, but the two largest names in the space aren't playing the same game. American Tower is fundamentally a wireless-infrastructure company with a fast-growing data center division bolted on; Digital Realty is a global, pure-play data center operator with no tower business at all. Both benefit from the same AI-driven demand surge - and both run into the exact same real-world bottleneck that has nothing to do with how much anyone wants to rent server space.

---

## American Tower: A Tower Company Leaning Into Data Centers

**American Tower's core business remains wireless communication towers, but its CoreSite data center subsidiary is now a real, fast-growing part of the story** - data center property revenue growing around 12% (excluding non-cash straight-line accounting effects), driven by demand for the low-latency connectivity data centers need to serve cloud and AI workloads efficiently. The company raised its 2026 AFFO guidance for the second time this year, to a range of $11.00-$11.17 per share, citing record leasing at CoreSite alongside solid demand across its broader global tower portfolio.

**The dividend track record is long and real:** American Tower has raised its dividend for roughly 15 consecutive years, with a 10-year dividend growth rate around 19.1% annualized, and a current yield of approximately 4.29% - a meaningfully higher yield than the pure-play data center name covered next, reflecting American Tower's more mature, diversified, income-oriented profile relative to a company still deploying heavy capital into new development.

---

## Digital Realty: The Pure-Play, Global Bet

**Digital Realty carries no tower business at all - its entire portfolio is data centers, at a scale most REITs in any sector don't approach:** roughly 3.1 gigawatts of IT capacity currently in service, plus another 8.5 gigawatts of buildable capacity either under active development or held for future development, spread across 310 data centers on six continents. Second-quarter 2026 revenue grew 29% year over year, driven directly by AI-related demand, with the company raising its full-year revenue and net income guidance in response.

**The trade-off for that growth is a lower current yield:** Digital Realty's dividend yields around 2.45% as of late July 2026, against American Tower's 4.29% - the same income-versus-growth pattern that shows up across other REIT subsectors covered on this site, where a company plowing more capital into active expansion (Digital Realty has roughly $16.5 billion in active development alone) pays a smaller current yield than a more mature, income-focused peer. Analyst projections put Digital Realty's AFFO-per-share growth around 8.5% in 2026 and 12.2% in 2027 - real, if not guaranteed, growth to weigh against the smaller current payout.

## Why Hyperscaler Leases Are Unusually Sticky

**The tenant base for both companies is dominated by hyperscale cloud providers - Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and a handful of others - and the lease structure they sign is genuinely different from a typical commercial lease.** Hyperscaler contracts commonly run 5 to 15 years, and renewal rates for hyperscale data center leases have historically exceeded 90%. Part of the reason: hyperscaler tenants typically invest two to three times the facility's original construction cost into their own equipment, cooling systems, and network architecture once they move in, making relocation genuinely expensive and operationally disruptive rather than a simple lease-expiration decision. That sunk investment is what makes these leases behave more like long-term infrastructure partnerships than conventional real estate rentals.

**Lease terms have also been shifting in a specific, real direction recently: toward more balanced, financeable contracts.** The era in which hyperscalers could unilaterally dictate lease terms - hair-trigger termination rights, aggressive non-disclosure demands - has given way to more standard commitments, including hyperscalers agreeing to firm rent commencement dates regardless of construction delays on the landlord's side, a meaningful shift in who bears construction-timeline risk.

## The Actual Bottleneck: Electricity, Not Demand

**Every growth projection above assumes the industry can secure enough power to build what it's planning, and that assumption is the real risk in the sector right now.** Data centers consume somewhere between 10 and 50 times more power per square foot than a standard commercial building. Securing that power through a power purchase agreement can take 12 to 36 months depending on equipment backlogs, transmission upgrades, and transformer availability - a timeline that doesn't move faster just because demand is urgent. In specific, high-demand markets like Northern Virginia and Dublin, outright power moratoriums are already limiting new development regardless of how much capital or tenant demand exists.

**This constraint currently works in existing operators' favor, which is worth naming directly.** Scarce power access has been a key lever letting data center operators command elevated pricing and stronger development yields on new projects - meaning the same bottleneck constraining supply is also part of what's supporting returns for companies like American Tower and Digital Realty that already have secured power capacity in place. A meaningful loosening of power constraints down the road, whatever form that takes, would work against both the pricing power and the development yields the sector currently enjoys.

---

## The Concentration Risk Underneath the Growth Story

**A small number of hyperscaler tenants account for a large share of data center REIT revenue across the sector**, not just at these two companies specifically. That concentration is precisely what makes the long lease terms and high renewal rates so valuable - but it also means any material shift in a major hyperscaler's own capital spending strategy, build-versus-lease preference, or cloud capacity planning could ripple through these REITs' financials in a way a more tenant-diversified REIT sector, like the net lease retail properties covered elsewhere on this site, wouldn't experience from any single relationship.

## Bottom Line

American Tower and Digital Realty both benefit from the same AI-driven data center demand surge, but they're different vehicles for capturing it: American Tower a diversified, higher-yielding tower company with a genuinely growing data center wing, Digital Realty a global, pure-play operator trading current income for faster reinvestment-driven growth. Both face the same real ceiling on how fast either one can actually expand - not tenant demand, which appears abundant, but the multi-year process of physically securing enough electricity to build the next wave of capacity.
