Every publicly traded REIT covered elsewhere on this site - Realty Income, Welltower, American Tower - trades on an exchange, with a price you can see in real time and shares you can sell the moment you decide to. Non-traded REITs deliberately don't work that way, and the largest real-world test of what that difference actually means in a crisis happened recently enough to study directly: Blackstone's BREIT, in late 2022.

---

## What "Non-Traded" Actually Means

**A non-traded REIT owns real estate and pays distributions the same way a public REIT does, but its shares don't list on an exchange.** Instead, the fund itself sets a periodic net asset value (NAV) - typically monthly - based on appraisals of its underlying properties, and investors buy and sell shares through the fund directly at that NAV, rather than through a stock exchange at whatever price the market happens to be paying that second. The pitch is straightforward: without a public listing, the share "price" doesn't swing with daily stock market sentiment the way a publicly traded REIT's share price does, so investors get real estate returns without equity-market-style volatility layered on top.

**The trade-off is liquidity, and it's a structural one, not a minor inconvenience.** Because the fund can only actually convert real estate into cash by selling properties - a process that takes months, sometimes longer - it can't guarantee instant redemptions the way an exchange-listed REIT's shares can be sold in seconds. Non-traded REITs handle this with share repurchase programs, not open-ended redemption rights: current industry-standard limits cap total repurchases at 5% of aggregate NAV per calendar quarter, meaning if enough investors want out at the same time, the fund is contractually allowed to simply not process everyone's request in full.

---

## What Actually Happened at BREIT

**Blackstone's BREIT - at roughly $69-70 billion, the largest fund of its kind - is the clearest real case study of that limit actually binding.** As interest rates climbed through 2022 and commercial property values came under real pressure, redemption requests surged well past what the fund's standard limits were built to absorb. Blackstone began capping monthly repurchases at 2% of aggregate NAV (and 5% per quarter) starting in November 2022 - an unprecedented move for a fund of this scale, and exactly the mechanism described above actually triggering in real time rather than remaining a theoretical risk in a prospectus.

**The numbers involved were large enough to matter well beyond BREIT's own investors.** Redemption requests peaked in January 2023 at $5.3 billion in a single month. The freeze - or more precisely, the proportional-fulfillment limit - continued for well over a year. It wasn't until February 2024 that BREIT fulfilled 100% of a monthly repurchase request ($961 million that month) for the first time since the crisis began, having returned more than $15 billion in liquidity to shareholders across the preceding 15 months as it worked through the backlog.

**The lesson isn't that BREIT was mismanaged - it's that the redemption limit worked exactly as designed, which is itself the point.** An investor who needed that money on a specific timeline in late 2022 or through most of 2023 simply couldn't get full access to it, regardless of how sound the underlying real estate holdings actually were. The structural liquidity mismatch between monthly-ish redemption requests and multi-month property sale timelines isn't a bug that better fund management avoids - it's the mechanical reality of owning illiquid real estate inside a structure that periodically offers partial liquidity.

---

## The Fee Side of the Trade-Off

**Non-traded REITs have historically carried real, substantial upfront costs that a publicly traded REIT bought through a standard brokerage simply doesn't have.** Traditional share classes commonly charged 7% upfront selling commissions plus another 2-3% in dealer manager fees - up to 10% of the initial investment gone before a dollar goes to work in real estate. Newer share classes launched since roughly 2016 have moved toward eliminating upfront commissions entirely for certain investor classes, a real structural improvement, though the ongoing management and incentive fee structure still generally runs higher than a simple public REIT ETF's expense ratio.

---

## When a Non-Traded REIT Might Still Make Sense

**An investor with a genuinely long time horizon, no near-term liquidity need for the specific capital allocated, and a real preference for reduced day-to-day price volatility over instant liquidity** has a coherent, if narrow, case for non-traded REIT exposure - accepting the redemption-limit risk in exchange for a return profile less tied to daily equity market sentiment. **An investor who might plausibly need that capital back on a specific timeline, or who hasn't specifically budgeted for the possibility of a multi-quarter delay in a stressed market**, is taking on a real, demonstrated risk that BREIT's own investors lived through directly, not a hypothetical worst case.

---

## Dividend Withholding, Same as Any Other REIT

**Non-traded REITs distributing to non-US investors follow the same NRA dividend withholding framework** covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - treaty rate or 30% default - though the practical mechanics of receiving and reporting those distributions, and confirming your specific broker or platform actually supports non-traded REIT access for international accounts in the first place, are worth confirming directly before investing, since non-traded REIT distribution is generally handled through different channels than a standard exchange-listed security.

---

## Bottom Line

Non-traded REITs offer real estate exposure with less day-to-day price volatility than a publicly traded REIT, funded by a genuine structural trade-off: periodic, capped liquidity rather than instant access. BREIT's 2022-2024 redemption limit isn't ancient history or a cautionary tale from a different era - it's a recent, well-documented, real demonstration of exactly what that trade-off means in practice when it actually gets tested, complete with specific numbers ($5.3 billion in peak monthly requests, over a year to fully unwind) rather than an abstract warning.
