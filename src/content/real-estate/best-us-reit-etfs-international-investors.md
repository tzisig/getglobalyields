An investor who's decided REITs deserve a spot in the portfolio runs into the same problem that shows up everywhere else in ETF selection: several large, liquid, well-established funds all claim to do roughly the same job, and the actual differences between them come down to what each one specifically holds, not which one is "best" in the abstract. REIT ETFs follow the same pattern - the real decision is less "which fund is superior" and more "which basket of properties matches what the rest of the portfolio is missing."

---

## Broad Market-Cap-Weighted REIT ETFs

**Funds like Vanguard's VNQ, Schwab's SCHH, and iShares' IYR hold a broad basket of US-listed REITs weighted by market capitalization**, spanning the major property sectors - retail, residential, industrial, healthcare, data centers, and more - in proportions that roughly track the sector's actual composition rather than any deliberate sector tilt. **These broad funds are the closest real estate equivalent to a total-market equity index fund**: no attempt to pick winning property sectors, just diversified exposure to the REIT market as a whole.

**The practical differences between VNQ, SCHH, and IYR come down to small variations in index methodology and total fund size, rather than fundamentally different investment approaches** - all three are large, liquid, broadly diversified options that a REIT-focused investor could reasonably choose between based on which is more conveniently available at their specific broker, rather than a meaningful strategic difference between them.

---

## Sector-Focused Real Estate ETFs

**XLRE (the Real Estate Select Sector SPDR) tracks the real estate sector specifically as defined within the S&P 500** - meaning its holdings are limited to REITs large enough to be included in the S&P 500 itself, a narrower, larger-cap-tilted slice of the REIT universe than the broader funds above, which include mid-cap and smaller REITs outside the S&P 500's scope.

**This narrower scope has a real consequence for diversification**: XLRE's holdings skew toward the largest, most established REITs, while a broader fund like VNQ captures a wider slice of the REIT market including smaller companies that may behave differently during sector-specific stress. Neither approach is inherently superior - a narrower, large-cap-focused fund and a broader fund including smaller REITs are simply different bets on where a real estate allocation should sit on the size spectrum, the same size-spectrum question covered generally in our [small-cap ETFs guide](/index-funds/).

## Global REIT ETFs: The Non-US Alternative

**REET (iShares Global REIT ETF) and similar global real estate funds extend the diversification argument one step further, holding REITs and real estate companies from outside the US alongside domestic ones.** For an international investor who already holds enough US-specific equity risk through a core stock or leveraged-ETF position, a global REIT fund diversifies the real estate sleeve of the portfolio geographically as well as by property type - rather than adding yet another purely US-concentrated position on top of an already US-heavy portfolio.

**The trade-off is currency and country-specific complexity**: a global REIT fund introduces exposure to non-US real estate markets, each with its own local dynamics, and to non-US currencies, layering in the currency risk covered in our [currency risk vs market risk guide](/currency-banking/currency-risk-vs-market-risk-international-investors/) on top of the property-sector risk itself. For an investor who specifically wants US real estate exposure to complement a US-focused portfolio, a US-only REIT fund keeps that exposure more precisely targeted; for an investor looking to diversify real estate exposure geographically as well, the global option does something the US-only funds structurally can't.

---

## What Actually Differs Between These Options

**Sector composition is the single most consequential difference.** A broad fund like VNQ holds a meaningfully different mix of property types than a narrower fund like XLRE, and a global fund like REET adds an entirely different dimension - country exposure - that none of the US-only options touch at all. Reviewing a fund's actual current holdings and sector breakdown before buying matters more here than for most equity index funds, since real estate sector weightings shift over time as different property types go in and out of favor.

**Total fund size and trading volume matter for cost of entry and exit** - the large, established funds referenced here are all liquid enough for a retail-sized position to trade without meaningfully moving the price, which isn't universally true of every REIT-focused fund on the market, including smaller or more specialized ones not covered in this comparison.

---

## How This Fits Alongside a US Equity or TQQQ-Heavy Portfolio

**For a portfolio already concentrated in US technology exposure through core index funds or leveraged positions like TQQQ**, a broad, market-cap-weighted US REIT fund adds genuine sector diversification without introducing additional country or currency risk on top of what's already there. **For an investor specifically looking to reduce overall US concentration** rather than just tech-sector concentration, a global REIT fund does more of that work, at the cost of adding currency exposure the US-only options don't carry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I hold more than one REIT ETF, or is one broad fund enough?**
For most investors, a single broad, diversified REIT fund covers the core diversification goal without needing multiple overlapping funds - holding both a broad fund and a narrower sector-specific fund mainly makes sense if there's a specific reason to deliberately overweight a particular property type, rather than as a default approach.

**Do REIT ETFs rebalance their sector weightings automatically as property types go in or out of favor?**
Market-cap-weighted REIT ETFs adjust their weightings passively as the underlying REITs' market values change and as the tracked index itself is periodically reconstituted, rather than through active manager decisions - the same passive rebalancing mechanism that applies to any other market-cap-weighted index fund.

**Is a global REIT ETF a good substitute for direct international diversification if I'm already US-equity-heavy?**
It's one piece of that picture specifically for the real estate sleeve of a portfolio, but it doesn't substitute for broader international equity diversification outside of real estate - the two address different parts of a portfolio's geographic concentration.

**How do REIT ETF dividends get taxed compared to the REITs held within them?**
The same non-resident withholding framework applies whether holding a REIT ETF or individual REIT shares directly, covered in our [US REITs tax guide](/real-estate/us-reits-international-investors-guide/) - the ETF wrapper itself doesn't change the underlying REIT dividend tax treatment.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Decide between broad market-cap-weighted exposure and a narrower, sector- or size-specific fund based on what the rest of the portfolio already lacks
- [ ] Check whether a US-only or global REIT fund better matches the goal - US-specific diversification versus broader geographic diversification
- [ ] Review a fund's actual current sector composition rather than assuming even weighting across property types
- [ ] Confirm the fund's total size and trading volume are large enough for a retail-sized position to trade efficiently
- [ ] Consider how a REIT allocation fits specifically alongside any existing concentration in US tech or leveraged ETF positions

## Bottom Line

The major US REIT ETFs are broadly similar in structure and mostly differ in what they actually hold - a broad market-cap-weighted basket, a narrower large-cap sector slice, or a global mix that adds non-US real estate and currency exposure. The right choice depends less on picking a "winner" and more on identifying what a real estate allocation is actually meant to accomplish alongside the rest of the portfolio.
