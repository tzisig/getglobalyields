Every REIT covered elsewhere on this site - Realty Income, Welltower, American Tower - is a US company. An investor specifically wanting real estate diversification *outside* the US has a narrower, less-covered set of options, and the two most commonly compared - VNQI and RWX - aren't actually close once you look at the real numbers.

---

## What Each Fund Actually Holds

**VNQI (Vanguard Global ex-U.S. Real Estate ETF)** tracks the S&P Global ex-U.S. Property Index, holding close to 425 real estate stocks - publicly traded REITs and real estate management/development companies - spread across developed markets in Europe and the Asia-Pacific region, with some Canadian exposure rounding out the portfolio. As the name states plainly, US real estate is deliberately excluded entirely, making this a genuine complement to (not overlap with) any US REIT holding covered elsewhere on this site.

**RWX (SPDR Dow Jones International Real Estate ETF)** covers similar territory - international real estate outside the US - but tracks a different underlying index (the Dow Jones Global ex-U.S. Select Real Estate Securities Index rather than S&P's version), which means a different specific country and company weighting even though the general universe of "developed-market real estate outside the US" sounds the same on paper.

---

## Where the Exposure Actually Concentrates

**"International real estate" sounds evenly spread across the globe; in practice, it's concentrated in a handful of specific countries.** VNQI's largest single-country weighting is Japan at roughly 23.3% of the fund - nearly a quarter of the entire portfolio in one country - followed by Australia at around 11.0%, the UK at about 7.4%, Hong Kong at 7.2%, Singapore at 5.9%, China at 5.5%, and Germany and India each around 3.9%. An investor picturing "diversified global real estate" should understand this is, in practice, a heavily Asia-Pacific-tilted fund with Japan alone representing a bigger single-country bet than most investors would assume from the fund's broad name.

---

## The Numbers Aren't Close

**VNQI's expense ratio is 0.12%. RWX's is 0.59%** - RWX costs roughly five times as much annually for what is, at a high level, a similar investment thesis. **VNQI's dividend yield runs around 4.7-4.9% recently, against RWX's roughly 3.7-4.0%.** On the two numbers most investors compare funds on directly - ongoing cost and current income - VNQI wins clearly on both, not just marginally.

**The one real, practical difference in RWX's favor is distribution frequency, not size.** RWX pays quarterly, giving an income-focused investor four payments a year to work with; VNQI pays semi-annually, just twice a year. For an investor who values more frequent cash flow timing - reinvesting quarterly, or simply preferring more regular income - that's a genuine, if secondary, consideration against otherwise favoring VNQI on cost and yield alone.

---

## Why the Cost Gap Is This Large

**The five-fold expense ratio gap mirrors a pattern that shows up elsewhere on this site whenever an older, actively-benchmarked fund gets compared to Vanguard's typically lower-cost index approach** - the same broad dynamic covered in our [VOO vs SPY vs IVV comparison](/index-funds/voo-vs-spy-vs-ivv-sp500-etf-comparison/) for domestic index funds. International real estate indexing carries genuinely higher operational costs than a US-only index fund - foreign market access, currency considerations, and less standardized reporting across the many countries involved all add real expense - but a 0.12% expense ratio versus 0.59% for a broadly similar investment universe is a large enough gap that it's worth confirming you're not paying a real, ongoing premium without a correspondingly larger benefit.

---

## Why This Category Exists at All

**An investor holding only US REITs, however diversified across US property types, still carries concentrated exposure to a single country's real estate cycle, interest rate environment, and currency** - the US dollar, which happens to be the currency of the investor's other US holdings too, so a US-only real estate allocation doesn't diversify currency exposure the way adding international real estate can. VNQI and RWX both address that specific gap, giving exposure to real estate cycles and property markets that don't move in lockstep with the US commercial real estate market covered throughout the rest of this category.

**For EU and UK investors specifically**, both VNQI and RWX face the same PRIIPs-driven access restriction covered in our [UCITS vs US-domiciled ETFs guide](/index-funds/ucits-vs-us-domiciled-etfs-tax-comparison/) - neither fund publishes an EU-compliant Key Information Document, so retail access generally requires either a UCITS-domiciled international real estate equivalent or qualifying as a professional investor under MiFID II, the same pattern covered for other US-domiciled funds elsewhere on this site.

---

## Dividend Withholding for International Investors

**Both VNQI and RWX are US-domiciled funds, so the standard NRA dividend withholding framework applies** - treaty rate or 30% default, based on your country of residence and W-8BEN status, the same mechanics covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/), regardless of the fact that the underlying real estate itself sits outside the US. The fund's domicile, not the location of the properties it holds, is what determines the withholding treatment.

---

## Bottom Line

VNQI beats RWX clearly on the two numbers that matter most for a straightforward buy-and-hold comparison - a 0.12% expense ratio against 0.59%, and a meaningfully higher current yield - with RWX's only real practical edge being quarterly rather than semi-annual distributions. For an investor specifically wanting real estate exposure outside the US as a genuine diversifier against the domestic REITs covered elsewhere on this site, VNQI is the more cost-efficient default unless distribution frequency specifically matters to your income planning.
