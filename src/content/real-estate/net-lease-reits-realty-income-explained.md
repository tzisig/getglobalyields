Net lease REITs get pitched as the closest thing to a bond that still trades as a stock, and the structure behind that reputation is worth actually understanding rather than taking on faith. The mechanism is a specific lease structure - triple net - that shifts almost every property-level cost onto the tenant, which is exactly why these REITs' cash flows, and their dividends, are unusually predictable.

---

## What "Triple Net" Actually Means

**In a standard triple net (NNN) lease, the tenant pays base rent plus all three major property-related cost categories: property taxes, building insurance, and maintenance** - not just their own interior upkeep, but common-area maintenance, landscaping, and day-to-day operational costs. The landlord's remaining responsibility is narrower than most people assume: typically just the structural elements - the roof, exterior walls, and foundation - and major capital repairs needed to keep the building code-compliant.

**These leases also tend to run long: commonly 10 to 20 years**, often with built-in rent escalations at fixed intervals. Combine a long lease term, a tenant covering nearly all operating costs, and contractual rent increases, and the result is a landlord whose income looks a lot more like a bond coupon than a typical rental property's more variable cash flow - collect the rent, let the tenant handle the building's day-to-day costs. That predictability is the entire investment case for this REIT subsector, and it's also why net lease REITs tend to trade with unusually high sensitivity to interest rates, a topic covered in more depth in our companion guide on how rate moves affect REIT valuations.

## Realty Income: The Streak Everyone Knows

**Realty Income has declared 672 consecutive monthly common stock dividends without a miss**, a streak the company markets aggressively under its trademarked "The Monthly Dividend Company" identity. As of mid-2026, the stock's dividend yield runs in the 4.9% to 5.4% range depending on the exact measurement date, backed by an AFFO (adjusted funds from operations) payout ratio of roughly 73% - a healthy cushion, since a REIT paying out closer to 100% of AFFO in dividends has far less room to absorb a bad quarter without cutting the payout.

**Realty Income is also a member of the S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats index**, a status requiring at least 25 consecutive years of dividend increases - Realty Income has delivered 31, with its 135th individual increase since its 1994 NYSE listing declared in mid-2026. The company's portfolio spans thousands of properties leased to a diversified mix of retail, industrial, and other commercial tenants across multiple countries, not a single-tenant or single-sector concentration.

---

## The Smaller Peer With the Longer Streak

**Here's the detail Realty Income's own marketing doesn't lead with: NNN REIT (formerly National Retail Properties) has a longer streak of consecutive annual dividend increases - 37 years, against Realty Income's 31.** NNN REIT is one of only three publicly traded REITs anywhere with 37 or more consecutive years of increases, a track record that held through both the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic without a break. Its most recent increase, declared in 2026, raised the quarterly dividend to $0.62 per share, a 3.3% bump, with the stock yielding around 5.2% as of late July 2026 and 2026 AFFO guidance in the $3.55-$3.59 per share range.

**The two streaks measure genuinely different things, which is worth being precise about.** Realty Income's 672 figure counts consecutive monthly *payments* - a streak that continues even in years the payment amount doesn't rise. NNN REIT's 37 figure counts consecutive annual *increases* specifically. A company can have an extremely long unbroken payment streak without necessarily having the longest increase streak in its own peer group - which is exactly the case here. Realty Income remains larger, more diversified, and more heavily covered; NNN REIT's less-marketed track record is arguably the more specifically impressive statistic of the two, on the metric it actually leads in.

## How Realty Income Actually Grows: Sale-Leaseback Deals

**Net lease REITs don't typically develop new properties from scratch - they grow largely through sale-leaseback transactions.** An operating company owning its own real estate sells the property to the REIT and simultaneously signs a long-term lease to stay in place as the tenant. For the seller, this is usually a financing move as much as a real estate one - converting an illiquid owned asset into cash while retaining full operational use of the property, without needing to relocate or interrupt the business. Realty Income continues sourcing deals this way at real scale, including expanding beyond its traditional retail base: a July 2026 joint venture with Cloud Capital brought three hyperscale, tenant-leased data centers in Northern Virginia into the portfolio, a sign these companies are actively diversifying property types even as the underlying triple net lease mechanics stay the same.

**Tenant concentration is lower than the "single big-box anchor" image many people have of net lease REITs.** Realty Income's top 20 clients combined account for just 31.9% of annualized base rent, with no single tenant dominating - Dollar General leads at 3.3%, followed by 7-Eleven at 3.1% and Walgreens at 3.0%. Investment-grade-rated tenants make up roughly a third of total rent, a real credit-quality signal, and the portfolio spans essential-retail categories like grocery (11.1% of rent) and convenience stores (9.4%) alongside home improvement and other sectors - businesses with a real case for staying open and paying rent through an economic downturn, which is precisely the tenant profile a net lease REIT wants underlying its long-term contracts.

## Why the Predictability Comes With a Real Trade-Off

**The same structural features that make net lease REITs bond-like in good conditions make them behave like one in bad conditions too.** A REIT whose income is a long-term, fixed (or slowly escalating) rent stream doesn't benefit from unexpected inflation or a hot rental market the way a REIT with shorter leases and more frequent rent resets can - the landlord is locked into the rate set years earlier. And because net lease REITs get valued partly by comparison to bond yields, a rising-rate environment that makes bonds more attractive tends to pressure net lease REIT share prices even when the underlying rent checks keep arriving exactly on schedule. The predictability is real; it isn't the same thing as insulation from broader interest rate moves.

## What Gets Withheld From Net Lease REITs Explained Dividends

**Realty Income, NNN REIT, and other US-domiciled net lease REITs pay dividends subject to the same NRA withholding framework** covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - the standard treaty rate or 30% default based on your country of residence and W-8BEN status. REIT dividends carry one detail specific to the sector worth knowing: a portion of REIT distributions is often classified as return of capital rather than ordinary income, which can affect the character (though generally not the withholding mechanics) of what you receive - covered in more depth in our broader [REITs for international investors guide](/real-estate/us-reits-international-investors-guide/).

## Where This Fits in a Portfolio

**An investor specifically looking for the most bond-like, income-predictable corner of the REIT universe** has a real case for net lease REITs over the broader, more operationally-variable REIT sectors also covered on this site. **An investor expecting net lease REITs to be immune to interest rate volatility because their underlying cash flows are so stable** is drawing the wrong conclusion from the right premise - stable cash flow and stable share price are two different things, and net lease REITs have historically delivered the first without reliably delivering the second.

---

## Summing Up Net Lease REITs Explained

Net lease REITs earn their bond-like reputation honestly - the triple net structure genuinely does shift almost all operating cost and variability onto the tenant, leaving a long, predictable rent stream behind. Realty Income's monthly-payment streak is the number everyone knows, but NNN REIT's longer annual-increase streak is the more precise version of the same story, delivered by a company that markets itself far less loudly. Neither streak, however long, changes the sector's real sensitivity to interest rates - the predictability lives in the rent checks, not in the stock price.
