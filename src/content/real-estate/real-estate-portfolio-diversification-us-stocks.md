An investor with a $200,000 portfolio split between a core US index fund and a smaller leveraged TQQQ position, considering adding REIT exposure for the first time, runs into a question this site can't answer with a single universal number: how much? The honest answer depends on what the rest of the portfolio already looks like and what the investor actually wants real estate to do for it - a question worth working through deliberately rather than picking a round number because it sounds reasonable.

---

## What Real Estate Is Actually Being Added to Solve

**Before sizing a real estate allocation, it's worth being specific about the actual goal**, since "diversification" alone is vague enough to justify almost any percentage. The two most common, genuinely distinct reasons an investor adds REIT exposure: **reducing concentration in a portfolio dominated by one sector** (particularly relevant for the tech-heavy exposure common throughout this site's TQQQ and QQQ-focused content), and **adding an income-oriented holding** given REITs' structurally higher dividend yields, covered in our [US REITs guide](/real-estate/us-reits-international-investors-guide/). These two goals can call for different sizing - an investor purely chasing sector diversification might want a modest allocation, while one specifically building an income stream might weight it more heavily, accepting the concentration trade-off that comes with a larger allocation to any single sector, real estate included.

---

## A Worked Example: Sizing a First Real Estate Allocation

**Consider the $200,000 portfolio above - $150,000 in a core US index fund, $50,000 in TQQQ.** Adding a 10% real estate allocation ($20,000 in a broad REIT ETF) would mean trimming the existing positions proportionally, or directing new contributions toward the REIT allocation until it reaches the target weight, rather than necessarily selling existing holdings and triggering a taxable event to fund the purchase immediately.

**Why 10% specifically isn't a universal answer - it's simply large enough to matter to the portfolio's overall behavior without being large enough to become a dominant single-sector bet.** An investor with a stronger conviction in real estate's diversification benefit, or a stronger income focus, might reasonably choose a larger allocation; an investor mainly interested in a token diversification gesture without meaningfully diluting their core equity thesis might choose less. There's no academically "correct" percentage the way there sometimes is for, say, an emergency fund's size - this is a genuine judgment call informed by the investor's actual goals.

---

## What Real Estate Diversification Doesn't Do

**A real estate allocation doesn't protect against a broad market downturn the way an uncorrelated asset class in theory would** - REITs are still equities, still traded on the same exchanges, and still subject to the same broad market sentiment shifts that move the rest of the stock market during genuine crisis periods. The diversification benefit shows up more reliably in normal market conditions, where real estate's different underlying economics (rental income, occupancy, interest-rate sensitivity) drive periods of divergence from the broader market - not as a guaranteed hedge during a sharp, broad selloff.

**A real estate allocation also doesn't reduce a leveraged position's own volatility.** An investor holding TQQQ specifically for its amplified exposure isn't changing that position's risk profile by adding REITs elsewhere in the portfolio - the two positions simply sit alongside each other, with the REIT allocation affecting the portfolio's overall blended behavior rather than altering how the leveraged position itself behaves.

## Rebalancing a Real Estate Allocation Over Time

**Once a target allocation is set, the same rebalancing logic that applies to any other portfolio sleeve applies here**: as REITs and the rest of the portfolio move at different rates, the real estate percentage will drift from its target over time, and periodically rebalancing back toward the original target - whether through new contributions directed toward the underweight sleeve, or an occasional sell-and-reallocate - keeps the allocation intentional rather than accidental.

**A meaningful REIT rally relative to the rest of the portfolio would push the real estate weight above its 10% target**, at which point trimming back toward target realizes some of the gain and redirects it toward the rest of the portfolio - the same discipline that applies to letting any single position or sector run unchecked, regardless of which one it happens to be.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there a standard "correct" real estate percentage that financial advisors generally recommend?**
There's no single universal figure - allocation recommendations vary by source and depend heavily on an individual investor's broader portfolio, risk tolerance, and goals. Treat any specific percentage you encounter, including the 10% example used in this guide, as an illustration of the reasoning process rather than a number to copy without adapting it to your own situation.

**Should the real estate allocation come from trimming the core index fund, the leveraged position, or both proportionally?**
This depends on which existing position the real estate allocation is meant to offset. An investor motivated mainly by reducing tech-sector concentration might trim proportionally from wherever that concentration sits; an investor simply adding real estate as a new sleeve without a specific existing position in mind might prefer funding it from new contributions rather than selling anything.

**Does a smaller real estate allocation (5% or less) still provide meaningful diversification benefit?**
A smaller allocation provides a smaller version of the same effect - it moves the portfolio's overall behavior somewhat in real estate's direction without dominating it, though the effect on the portfolio's overall return and volatility becomes correspondingly harder to detect at very small allocation sizes.

**Should the real estate allocation be reduced as an investor approaches a specific financial goal or retirement?**
This follows the same general logic covered in our [sequence of returns risk guide](/strategies/sequence-of-returns-risk-leveraged-etf-withdrawals/) for other portfolio sleeves - reducing exposure to any single volatile sector, including real estate, as a specific near-term spending need approaches is a reasonable general principle, though the specific timing and amount depends on the individual's full financial picture.

## Putting How Much Real Estate Should Be in a US Stock-Focused Portfolio Into Practice

- [ ] Identify the specific goal behind adding real estate exposure - sector diversification, income, or both
- [ ] Choose an allocation percentage deliberately based on that goal, rather than defaulting to a round number without reasoning through it
- [ ] Understand that real estate diversification works more reliably in normal markets than as a hedge during a broad crisis selloff
- [ ] Recognize that a real estate allocation doesn't change the risk profile of a separate leveraged position elsewhere in the portfolio
- [ ] Set a rebalancing approach in advance so the allocation stays intentional as REITs and the rest of the portfolio move at different rates

---

## How Much Real Estate Should Be in a US Stock-Focused Portfolio: The Takeaway

There's no universally correct real estate allocation percentage - the right size depends on what an investor actually wants the allocation to accomplish, whether that's sector diversification away from a tech-heavy core, an income-oriented holding, or some combination of both. Sizing it deliberately, and rebalancing back to that target over time, matters more than finding the theoretically optimal number.
