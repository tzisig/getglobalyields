A Singaporean investor with roughly $80,000 available to allocate to US real estate exposure faces two genuinely different paths. One is a wire transfer, a brokerage order, and a REIT ETF position sitting in the same account as the rest of a US equity portfolio. The other is a search for a US real estate agent, a property manager, a mortgage broker willing to work with a foreign buyer, and a multi-month closing process ending in a single physical asset in one city. Both are legitimate ways to gain US real estate exposure. They are not remotely the same undertaking.

---

## Capital Requirements and Divisibility

**A REIT or REIT ETF position can be built with any amount of capital**, from a single share upward, and can be sized precisely to fit a specific percentage of a broader portfolio - the same divisibility that makes any publicly traded security easy to allocate deliberately. **A direct property purchase requires the full price of an actual physical asset**, plus closing costs, typically running several percent of the purchase price on top of the sale price itself, and for a foreign buyer without US credit history, financing (if used at all) often comes with less favorable terms than a US resident would receive, when it's available at all - many foreign buyers end up purchasing in cash specifically because financing is harder to secure from abroad.

**This divisibility difference has a direct portfolio consequence:** $80,000 in a REIT ETF can represent, say, exactly 15% of a portfolio, rebalanced as easily as any other position. $80,000 toward a single property is often not even enough for a full purchase in many US markets once closing costs and a maintenance buffer are accounted for, and once bought, the position can't be partially trimmed to rebalance - it's one illiquid asset, not a percentage that can be adjusted with a sell order.

---

## Liquidity: Minutes vs Months

**A REIT ETF trades on a public exchange during market hours, with typical settlement in one to two business days** - the same liquidity profile as any other US-listed ETF. **A direct property sale, even in an active market, commonly takes weeks to find a buyer and additional weeks to close**, and in a slow market or for an unusual property type, can take considerably longer. For a foreign seller specifically, FIRPTA withholding at closing, covered in our [FIRPTA guide](/real-estate/firpta-explained-foreign-investors/), adds a further mechanical step that a REIT sale simply doesn't involve.

**This liquidity gap matters most in exactly the situation where liquidity matters most - needing to access the capital on short notice.** A REIT position can be sold to cover an unexpected need within days. A physical property generally cannot, regardless of how much equity is theoretically available in it.

---

## Ongoing Management Burden

**A REIT ETF position requires literally no ongoing management** beyond the same periodic portfolio review any other holding gets - no tenants, no maintenance calls, no local property tax filings, no need to hire (and vet, and pay) a property manager from abroad.

**Direct ownership of a rental property is an operating business, not a passive holding**, even when a property manager is hired to handle day-to-day tasks - vacancies, repairs, tenant disputes, and periodic capital expenditures (a new roof, an HVAC replacement) are the owner's financial and, often, logistical responsibility, made meaningfully harder to handle from a different country and time zone than it already is for a local owner.

---

## Tax Complexity: A Meaningfully Different Filing Situation

**REIT dividend income for a non-US investor is generally handled through withholding at the broker level**, reported on a 1042-S, with no separate US tax return required in most cases - the same relatively simple compliance picture that applies to holding any other US dividend-paying stock or ETF, covered generally in our [taxes category](/taxes/).

**Direct ownership of US rental property creates a genuinely more complex filing obligation.** Rental income from US property is generally treated as income "effectively connected" with a US trade or business once the investor elects (or is deemed) to treat it that way, which typically requires filing an annual US non-resident tax return (Form 1040-NR) reporting rental income and allowable expenses, rather than simple withholding at the source. On sale, FIRPTA withholding and eventual capital gains reporting add further complexity beyond what a REIT sale involves. This is a substantively larger ongoing tax compliance commitment than REIT ownership, generally justifying professional tax preparation help that a REIT holding typically doesn't require.

---

## Diversification: Single Asset vs Basket

**A single directly owned property concentrates risk entirely in one location, one property type, and one local market** - a job-market downturn in that specific city, a natural disaster, or a local oversupply of similar units all directly threaten the value of that one asset with no offsetting exposure elsewhere. **A REIT ETF spreads exposure across dozens or hundreds of properties, multiple property types, and multiple geographic markets** within a single position, the same diversification principle covered throughout this site's [index funds category](/index-funds/) applied specifically to real estate.

---

## When Direct Ownership Still Makes Sense

**Direct ownership isn't simply the "worse" option in every case** - an investor who wants an eventual personal-use property (a future retirement home, a property for a relocating family member), who has genuine local market expertise or an existing on-the-ground network, or who specifically wants leveraged property-level returns through a mortgage (a form of leverage structurally different from the leveraged ETFs covered elsewhere on this site) has real, non-REIT-replaceable reasons to consider direct ownership. The comparison in this guide is about exposure to US real estate as an asset class through a brokerage account versus a physical purchase - not a claim that direct ownership is never the right choice for a specific individual situation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a REIT ETF replicate the leverage a mortgage provides on a direct property purchase?**
Not directly in the same mechanical sense - a mortgage lets a buyer control a full property's worth of exposure with a fraction of the capital down, amplifying both gains and losses on the property's value. A REIT ETF doesn't carry this kind of asset-level leverage by default, though some real estate-focused leveraged ETFs exist as a separate, higher-risk category outside the scope of a standard REIT holding.

**Is it harder for a foreign investor to get a US mortgage than a US resident?**
Generally yes - foreign buyers without US credit history or US-based income documentation often face more limited financing options, higher down payment requirements, and less favorable terms than a US resident would, when financing is available at all, which is part of why many foreign real estate purchases end up being all-cash.

**Does owning a REIT ETF create any US filing obligation at all?**
In most cases, no separate US tax return is required for REIT ETF holdings beyond the withholding already applied at the broker level and reflected on the 1042-S - this is a meaningfully simpler compliance picture than direct property ownership, though individual circumstances vary and confirming with a tax advisor is still worthwhile.

**Can I combine both approaches - some REIT exposure and a direct property later?**
Yes - the two aren't mutually exclusive, and many investors use REITs specifically as the more liquid, lower-effort real estate allocation within a broader portfolio while separately considering a direct property purchase for reasons unrelated to portfolio diversification, like eventual personal use.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Compare the actual capital required for a meaningful direct property purchase against the ease of building a REIT position at any size
- [ ] Weigh the liquidity difference seriously if the capital might need to be accessed on short notice
- [ ] Account for the real ongoing management burden of direct ownership, including from a different time zone and country
- [ ] Understand that direct property ownership generally requires a more complex US tax filing obligation than REIT ownership
- [ ] Consider a REIT ETF's diversification across properties, types, and markets against a single property's concentrated risk
- [ ] Recognize that direct ownership can still make sense for non-portfolio reasons, like a future personal-use property

---

## Bottom Line

REITs and direct property ownership both give a foreign investor real exposure to US real estate, but they differ enormously in capital requirements, liquidity, ongoing effort, and tax complexity. For an investor whose goal is simply diversified real estate exposure within an existing portfolio, a REIT ETF accomplishes that with a fraction of the capital, effort, and compliance burden that direct ownership requires - direct ownership earns its place mainly when there's a specific, non-diversification reason for wanting an actual physical property.
