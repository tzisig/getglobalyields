A Canadian investor considering whether to buy a Florida condo outright, versus simply adding a US REIT ETF to the brokerage account she already has, runs into FIRPTA as the reason those two paths look nothing alike on the tax side. One triggers a specific US withholding regime built entirely around foreign ownership of American real estate. The other, in almost every realistic case, doesn't touch FIRPTA at all. Understanding why the two are treated so differently starts with what FIRPTA actually is.

---

## What FIRPTA Is Built to Do

**The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, enacted in 1980, exists to make sure foreign investors pay US tax on gains from US real estate the same way US persons do** - closing a gap where, before FIRPTA, a foreign seller of US property could potentially avoid US capital gains tax that a US seller couldn't. The mechanism it uses is withholding at the point of sale: rather than relying on a foreign seller to voluntarily file a US tax return and pay tax afterward, FIRPTA generally requires the **buyer** of a US real property interest from a foreign seller to withhold a percentage of the sale price and remit it to the IRS.

**"US real property interest" (USRPI) is defined broadly** - it covers direct ownership of US land and buildings, but also extends to stock in a "US real property holding corporation" (a US corporation where a majority of assets, by fair market value, consist of US real property interests). This broader stock-level reach is what makes FIRPTA relevant to discuss on a site about US equity investing, even though most readers here will never personally trigger it.

---

## How FIRPTA Withholding Works for a Direct Property Sale

**When a foreign person sells US real property directly, the buyer is generally required to withhold a percentage of the gross sale price** - not the gain, the total sale price - and remit it to the IRS using Form 8288, with the seller receiving a Form 8288-A documenting the amount withheld. This buyer-side withholding obligation is the mechanism's core design: it shifts the compliance burden onto the (often US-resident) buyer, who has much stronger incentive and ability to comply with US tax law than a foreign seller who may never interact with the US tax system again after the sale closes.

**Because the withholding is calculated on the gross sale price rather than the actual gain, it's frequently far larger than the seller's real eventual tax liability** - a property sold for a modest gain, or even at a loss, can still trigger a substantial withholding amount at closing, with the excess recoverable only by filing a US tax return after the fact and claiming a refund. A foreign seller with a specific, documentable expectation of a smaller actual tax liability can apply to the IRS in advance for a withholding certificate reducing the withheld amount closer to the true expected liability, rather than accepting the standard gross-price withholding and waiting for a refund.

**Certain smaller transactions carry reduced or no withholding** - notably sales below a specific price threshold where the buyer intends to use the property as a personal residence - but these carve-outs are narrow and condition-specific rather than a general small-transaction exemption, and don't apply to the REIT-stock scenario this site's readers are actually more likely to encounter.

---

## The Publicly Traded Stock Exception - Where Most Readers Actually Land

**Stock in a US real property holding corporation is a USRPI in principle - but Congress built in a specific exception for stock that's "regularly traded on an established securities market."** For a foreign investor holding a small stake in a publicly traded, regularly traded company - which describes essentially every retail investor buying REIT shares or a REIT ETF through a standard brokerage account - this exception is what actually determines whether FIRPTA applies at all.

**The threshold for this exception differs depending on what's being held.** For general publicly traded US corporation stock, the standard "regularly traded" FIRPTA exception applies to a holder owning 5% or less of the stock. **For REIT stock specifically, the 2015 PATH Act raised this threshold to 10%** as part of a broader set of changes intended to make US REITs more attractive to foreign capital - meaning a foreign investor can hold up to 10% of a publicly traded REIT's stock and still fall outside FIRPTA's disposition-gain rules on that holding, compared to the tighter 5% threshold that applies to non-REIT publicly traded stock.

**For nearly every individual investor covered by this site**, holding REIT shares or a REIT ETF through a standard brokerage account, actual ownership stakes are a small fraction of a percent of any REIT's total market capitalization - meaning the practical answer to "does FIRPTA apply to me buying and later selling REIT shares" is almost always no, without needing to calculate the exact percentage in any individual case.

## When FIRPTA Actually Becomes Relevant to a Real Investor

**Direct ownership of US real property** - buying an actual house, condo, or commercial building - is the clearest case where FIRPTA's withholding regime applies in full, and where a foreign buyer or seller should plan for it as a real, mechanical part of the transaction rather than an afterthought discovered at closing.

**A large, concentrated position in a non-publicly-traded REIT or real estate partnership** - a private REIT, a real estate limited partnership, or a similar vehicle that doesn't trade on a public exchange - falls outside the "regularly traded" exception entirely, regardless of the percentage held, since that exception specifically requires public trading on an established market. This is a meaningfully different situation from the diversified, exchange-traded REIT ETF holding most readers of this site are likely to actually own.

**A foreign investor whose holding in a single publicly traded REIT genuinely approaches or exceeds the 10% ownership threshold** - an unusual position size for an individual retail investor, but not impossible for a very large account concentrated in one REIT - would need to evaluate FIRPTA's disposition-gain rules directly rather than relying on the general exception.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does FIRPTA affect the dividends a REIT pays me, or only gains from selling shares?**
FIRPTA's core mechanism targets gain from disposing of a US real property interest, not ordinary dividend income - REIT dividend taxation, covered in our [US REITs guide](/real-estate/us-reits-international-investors-guide/), follows the standard non-resident withholding framework rather than FIRPTA's disposition rules. There is a separate, narrower FIRPTA-related withholding rule that can apply to certain REIT capital gain distributions attributable to the REIT's own sale of US real property, which is a distinct mechanism from the general dividend withholding most REIT income falls under.

**If I buy and sell a REIT ETF regularly, does each sale trigger FIRPTA withholding at my broker?**
No - the publicly traded stock exception means a standard REIT ETF, and the REITs held within it, generally fall entirely outside FIRPTA's disposition withholding regime for a retail-sized position, so routine buying and selling doesn't trigger the kind of sale-price withholding that applies to a direct property sale.

**Is FIRPTA the same thing as the standard 30% non-resident withholding on dividends?**
No - they're separate mechanisms addressing different things. The 30% (or treaty-reduced) rate applies to ordinary US-source income like dividends and interest; FIRPTA specifically targets gain from disposing of US real property interests, using a gross-sale-price withholding mechanism rather than an income-tax-rate withholding mechanism.

**Can a foreign investor avoid FIRPTA withholding entirely on a direct property sale?**
Not avoid it outright in a normal sale, but it can be reduced through an IRS withholding certificate application when the seller can document that actual tax liability will be lower than the standard gross-price withholding would produce - this requires proactive filing before closing, not an automatic adjustment.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Recognize that FIRPTA targets gain from disposing of US real property interests, not routine dividend income
- [ ] Confirm that a REIT ETF or diversified REIT stock holding falls under the publicly traded stock exception (10% threshold for REIT stock, 5% for other publicly traded US corporation stock)
- [ ] Treat direct US real property ownership as a genuinely different tax situation requiring specific FIRPTA planning, distinct from holding REIT shares
- [ ] For a large, concentrated REIT position approaching the ownership threshold, evaluate FIRPTA's disposition rules directly rather than assuming the general exception applies
- [ ] For any actual direct property sale, plan for buyer-side withholding on the gross sale price, and consider an IRS withholding certificate if actual liability will be materially lower

---

## Bottom Line

FIRPTA is a real, mechanically significant withholding regime for foreign investors selling US real property directly - but for the vast majority of international investors whose real estate exposure comes through publicly traded REITs or REIT ETFs, the publicly traded stock exception means FIRPTA's disposition-gain rules simply don't apply. Understanding where that line sits is what separates the two very different tax realities of owning US property directly versus owning it through the stock market.
