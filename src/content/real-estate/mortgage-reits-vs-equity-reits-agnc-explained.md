Every REIT covered elsewhere on this site - Realty Income, Welltower, American Tower, Invitation Homes - actually owns physical buildings and collects rent from tenants. Mortgage REITs (mREITs) like AGNC Investment Corp own no real estate at all. They own mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, financed with borrowed money, earning the spread between what they pay to borrow and what the mortgages yield. It's a fundamentally different business wearing the same "REIT" label, and the double-digit yields this category is known for come directly from how that business actually works.

---

## The Business Model: Borrow Short, Lend Long

**A mortgage REIT's core trade is straightforward to describe and genuinely risky to run: borrow money short-term, cheaply, and use it to buy longer-term mortgage-backed securities that yield more.** The financing typically comes through repurchase agreements (repos) - the mREIT pledges its MBS holdings as collateral, receives cash, and agrees to repurchase the collateral later at a price reflecting the short-term interest rate. The difference between what the mREIT earns on its MBS holdings and what it pays to finance them is the net interest spread - the entire source of the company's profit.

**Leverage is what turns a modest spread into a double-digit yield.** Standard mREIT leverage runs 5 to 9 times equity - meaning for every $1 of shareholder capital, the REIT borrows $5 to $9 more to buy additional mortgage securities. AGNC specifically ran leverage around 7.0 to 7.5 times tangible book value in 2026, with 7.4x at the end of the second quarter. Apply that much leverage to even a modest 2-percentage-point spread, and the return on the underlying equity capital gets multiplied dramatically - which is exactly where AGNC's roughly 13% dividend yield actually comes from, not from owning some especially lucrative pool of mortgages.

---

## The Real Numbers Behind AGNC's Yield

**AGNC's second-quarter 2026 figures show the spread mechanics concretely:** an average asset yield of 4.95% against a net interest spread of 2.06% - meaning the company's borrowing cost sat around 2.89%. That spread produced net spread income of $0.40 per share for the quarter, funding the $0.36 per share quarterly dividend with a real, if not enormous, coverage margin. The company has maintained its dividend for 75 consecutive months - a genuinely notable stretch of stability for a business model this sensitive to rate movements, though maintaining the same dividend and maintaining the same book value are two different things.

**Tangible book value, not the dividend, is where the real volatility shows up.** AGNC's tangible net book value per share fell 5.6% in a single quarter earlier in 2026, from $8.98 to $8.38, before recovering to $8.58 by the second quarter. As of early August 2026, AGNC's stock traded around 28% above that $8.58 book value - a real, current gap between what the market is paying and what the company's own accounting says its net assets are worth, requiring the market to be pricing in something beyond current book value to justify the premium.

---

## Why the Spread Can Compress Fast

**The mechanism that makes AGNC's yield so high is the same mechanism that can cut it sharply, and it's worth seeing the arithmetic directly.** If a mortgage REIT borrows at 3% and holds MBS yielding 5%, the spread is 2 percentage points. If short-term financing costs rise to 4% while the MBS yield stays fixed (because the underlying mortgages were issued years earlier at a fixed rate), the spread compresses to just 1 percentage point - a 50% reduction in profitability from a financing cost move that, by itself, doesn't sound dramatic. Because mREITs run leveraged 5-9x, that spread compression hits the return on equity multiplied by the same leverage factor that amplified the yield in good conditions.

**Agency versus non-agency MBS is a real distinction worth knowing, separate from the leverage question.** Agency mortgage-backed securities - like most of AGNC's holdings - carry quasi-government credit backing, meaning default risk on the underlying mortgages is largely not the investor's problem; the risk that matters is almost entirely about interest rate and spread movement, not borrower default. Non-agency mREITs, holding mortgages without that government-linked backing, add real credit risk on top of the same interest-rate and leverage risk agency-focused mREITs already carry.

---

## Why This Is a Completely Different Bet From an Equity REIT

**Every risk factor covered for the equity REITs elsewhere on this site - tenant concentration, occupancy, property-type demand - simply doesn't apply to a mortgage REIT, which owns no property and has no tenants at all.** In its place: financing-cost risk, interest rate spread risk, leverage risk, and (for non-agency holdings) credit risk - a genuinely different risk profile requiring genuinely different analysis, not a version of the same real estate questions covered for Realty Income or Welltower elsewhere in this category.

---

## Dividend Withholding, With One Added Wrinkle

**AGNC and other mortgage REITs distribute to non-US investors under the same NRA withholding framework** covered throughout this site's [taxes category](/taxes/) - treaty rate or 30% default. One detail specific to mREITs worth knowing: because their income is largely interest-based (from MBS holdings) rather than rental income, the character of REIT distributions can differ somewhat from an equity REIT's mix of ordinary income, capital gains, and return of capital - worth confirming with your broker's specific tax reporting for the distributions actually received rather than assuming identical treatment to an equity REIT.

---

## Bottom Line

AGNC's roughly 13% yield isn't a free lunch or a mispriced opportunity - it's the direct output of a leveraged borrow-short-lend-long spread business, running 7x-plus leverage against a spread that can compress by half from a financing-cost move that wouldn't even register as major news for an equity REIT. Mortgage REITs and the equity REITs covered throughout the rest of this category share a tax structure and not much else - understanding that the businesses are fundamentally different, not just differently risky versions of the same thing, is the actual prerequisite for deciding whether AGNC's yield is worth what it's compensating for.
