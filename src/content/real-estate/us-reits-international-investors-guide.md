A Brazilian investor with a US brokerage account holding QQQ and a handful of dividend ETFs looks at his allocation and notices something missing: every position he owns moves with the same broad equity market. Real estate is the asset class most commonly pitched as a genuine diversifier against exactly that - and the only real estate exposure available in the same account, without wiring money to a separate property manager or foreign title office, is a real estate investment trust. The mechanics of holding one are simple. The tax treatment is not quite the same as the dividend stocks already in the account, and that difference is worth understanding before buying the first share.

---

## What a REIT Actually Is

**A real estate investment trust is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate - office buildings, shopping centers, apartment complexes, data centers, warehouses, cell towers - and is legally required to distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders as dividends** in exchange for avoiding corporate-level income tax on the distributed portion. That 90% distribution requirement is the structural reason REITs tend to carry higher dividend yields than the broader stock market: the tax code effectively forces the payout, rather than leaving it to management's discretion the way an ordinary company's dividend policy works.

**Publicly traded REITs trade on US stock exchanges exactly like any other stock or ETF**, meaning a REIT itself, or a REIT-focused ETF holding a basket of them, can be bought and sold through the same brokerage account already used for US equities - no separate account, no property-specific paperwork, no foreign real estate registry to navigate.

---

## How REIT Dividends Are Taxed Differently From Ordinary Dividends

**This is the part that catches investors coming from a general US-equity background off guard.** Ordinary dividends from most US companies can qualify for reduced tax treaty withholding rates when they meet the IRS's "qualified dividend" criteria, covered in detail in our [qualified vs non-qualified dividends guide](/taxes/qualified-vs-non-qualified-dividends-foreign-investors/). **REIT dividends are structurally different: because the REIT itself paid no corporate tax on the distributed income, the dividend it pays out is generally treated as ordinary, non-qualified income** - taxed at the standard 30% US withholding rate for non-resident aliens, or the "dividends" rate specified in your country's tax treaty with the US if one applies, the same treaty mechanism covered in our [tax treaty tie-breaker guide](/taxes/tax-treaty-tie-breaker-rules-explained/).

**A REIT distribution can also include a "capital gain" component and, less commonly, a "return of capital" component**, each reported separately on the year-end tax statement (a 1099-DIV for US-taxable accounts, though most non-US investors instead receive a 1042-S reflecting the actual withholding applied). The capital gain portion generally follows its own treatment rather than the ordinary-income REIT dividend rate, and return-of-capital distributions - effectively a partial return of your own original investment rather than income - aren't taxed as a dividend at all, though they do reduce your cost basis. **The breakdown between these components varies REIT by REIT and year by year**, and isn't something an investor can predict or control in advance; it's disclosed after the fact on the tax statement itself.

## FIRPTA: The Real Estate-Specific Wrinkle

**The Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (FIRPTA) is a separate piece of the US tax code that applies specifically to foreign investors' gains connected to US real property**, and it's worth understanding even though it mostly doesn't apply to the way most international investors actually hold REITs. In its general form, FIRPTA can subject a foreign investor's gain on disposing of a US real property interest to US tax and withholding that wouldn't otherwise apply to an NRA's capital gains, covered in general terms in our [FIRPTA explainer](/real-estate/firpta-explained-foreign-investors/).

**The exception that matters for nearly every reader of this site: publicly traded REIT stock is specifically excepted from FIRPTA for an investor who holds 10% or less of the REIT's stock** (a threshold raised from 5% by the 2015 PATH Act), because the stock is "regularly traded on an established securities market." An individual investor buying REIT shares or a REIT ETF through a standard brokerage account is, in virtually every realistic case, nowhere near a 10% ownership stake in a REIT with a market capitalization in the billions - meaning FIRPTA's disposition-gain rules simply don't come into play for buying and later selling REIT shares the normal way. FIRPTA becomes practically relevant mainly for direct ownership of US property, or a large, concentrated private REIT position - not for a diversified REIT ETF holding in a retail brokerage account.

## REITs vs the Rest of a US Equity Portfolio

**The diversification argument for REITs rests on a real, if inconsistent, historical pattern: real estate returns don't move in perfect lockstep with the broader stock market**, driven partly by different underlying economics (rental income and occupancy rates versus corporate earnings growth) and partly by REITs' interest-rate sensitivity, which behaves somewhat differently from how growth stocks react to rate changes. This correlation isn't fixed or guaranteed - REITs and the broader market can and do move together during broad market stress, particularly sharp downturns where most asset classes sell off together - but over full market cycles, the correlation has historically been meaningfully below 1.0, which is the actual basis for including REITs in a diversified allocation rather than a promise of protection during any specific downturn.

**A leveraged-ETF-heavy portfolio - the kind covered throughout this site's [strategies category](/strategies/) - has a specific reason to consider REIT exposure that a plain index-fund portfolio doesn't share as acutely: TQQQ and similar leveraged funds concentrate risk entirely within technology-heavy US equities, amplified.** A REIT allocation sitting alongside a leveraged core position doesn't reduce the leveraged position's own volatility, but it does mean the portfolio as a whole isn't entirely dependent on one sector's fortunes moving in one direction.

## Practical Access: REIT ETFs vs Individual REITs

**A broad REIT ETF (holding dozens to hundreds of individual REITs across property sectors) diversifies away the risk of any single REIT's property-specific problems** - a struggling shopping mall REIT or an overleveraged office-space REIT - the same diversification logic that applies to choosing a broad index fund over individual stocks, covered generally in our [index funds category](/index-funds/). **An individual REIT position concentrates both the potential upside and the property-sector-specific risk into a single company**, which may be a deliberate choice for an investor with a specific view on a particular property sector (data centers, industrial warehouses, healthcare facilities), but isn't the default, lower-effort approach most international investors are looking for from a real estate allocation.

**Sector composition varies significantly between broad REIT ETFs** - some are weighted heavily toward traditional retail and office space, others lean toward the newer, faster-growing categories like data centers and cell towers - so checking a fund's actual sector breakdown before buying matters more for REIT ETFs than it typically does for a broad total-market equity fund, where sector weights are closer to whatever the overall economy looks like.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a US bank account or special brokerage permissions to buy REITs or REIT ETFs?**
No - REITs and REIT ETFs trade on the same US exchanges as any other US stock, so if your broker already gives you access to buy US-listed ETFs like VOO or QQQ, the same access covers REITs. No separate application or account type is required.

**Are REIT dividends eligible for the reduced tax treaty dividend rate my country has with the US?**
Generally yes, in the sense that most tax treaties' dividend articles apply to REIT distributions the same way they apply to ordinary dividends, though some treaties carve out specific, sometimes higher, rates for REIT dividends specifically - this varies by treaty and is worth confirming for your specific country rather than assuming the general treaty dividend rate automatically applies without exception.

**Is a REIT ETF automatically better diversification than owning individual company stocks in the same sectors those REITs cover?**
A REIT ETF diversifies across many individual REITs and property types, which reduces single-company risk - but it doesn't diversify away real-estate-sector risk itself. An investor already holding real-estate-adjacent positions (homebuilders, construction, or mortgage-related stocks) should account for that overlap rather than treating a REIT ETF as entirely independent exposure.

**Do REITs pay dividends more frequently than typical US stocks?**
Many REITs pay monthly or quarterly distributions rather than the standard quarterly cadence common among typical US dividend stocks, though this varies REIT by REIT - check the specific distribution schedule for any REIT or REIT ETF before assuming a particular frequency.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Understand that REIT dividends are generally taxed as ordinary, non-qualified income at your treaty (or 30% default) withholding rate, not the reduced qualified-dividend rate
- [ ] Confirm your specific country's tax treaty doesn't carve out a different rate for REIT dividends specifically
- [ ] Recognize that FIRPTA's disposition-gain rules generally don't apply to buying and selling publicly traded REIT stock held at 10% or less ownership
- [ ] Check a REIT ETF's actual sector composition (retail, office, industrial, data centers) before buying, rather than assuming even weighting
- [ ] Consider whether real estate exposure meaningfully complements an existing portfolio, particularly one concentrated in leveraged tech-sector ETFs
- [ ] Read the year-end tax statement's breakdown of ordinary dividend, capital gain, and return-of-capital components rather than assuming a REIT distribution is taxed uniformly

---

## Bottom Line

REITs give international investors a straightforward way to add US real estate exposure through the same brokerage account already used for stocks, with genuine historical diversification benefits against a broader equity portfolio - but the tax treatment of REIT dividends differs meaningfully from ordinary qualified dividends, generally landing in the higher, non-qualified withholding category. Understanding that distinction, and confirming how it interacts with your specific country's tax treaty, matters more for REITs than it does for most other US equity holdings.
