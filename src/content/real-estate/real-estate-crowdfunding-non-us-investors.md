An investor who's read about real estate crowdfunding platforms letting US residents buy a fractional stake in a specific apartment complex or development project for a few hundred dollars, and goes looking to sign up from outside the US, generally runs into the same wall: most of these platforms simply don't accept non-US investors. This isn't an oversight or a technical limitation - it's a direct consequence of US securities law, and understanding why explains a real, structural difference between crowdfunding platforms and the publicly traded REITs covered elsewhere in this category.

---

## What Real Estate Crowdfunding Platforms Actually Are

**Real estate crowdfunding platforms pool capital from many individual investors to fund a specific property purchase, development project, or a fund holding several such projects** - a fundamentally different structure from a publicly traded REIT, which is itself already a company whose shares trade freely on a stock exchange. A crowdfunding investment is typically a direct or near-direct stake in a specific deal or a private fund, not a share of stock in a publicly traded company.

**This structural difference is the entire reason access differs so sharply between the two.** A publicly traded REIT's shares are registered securities freely tradable on a public exchange, available to essentially any investor with brokerage access, foreign or domestic. A crowdfunding platform's offerings are frequently structured as private placements - securities exempt from full SEC registration under specific rules, most commonly Regulation D - which come with real restrictions on who can legally participate.

---

## Why Private Placement Rules Restrict Foreign Access

**Regulation D offerings avoid the cost and disclosure burden of a full public securities registration, but in exchange, they're generally restricted to a defined pool of eligible investors** - commonly "accredited investors" in the US context, a status defined by specific income or net worth thresholds under US securities law. **Extending these offerings to foreign investors adds compliance complexity many platforms simply choose not to take on**: verifying accredited-investor-equivalent status under a foreign jurisdiction's own rules, navigating that jurisdiction's own securities regulations governing the offer of foreign investments to its residents, and managing the tax withholding and reporting obligations that come with foreign ownership - all real costs a platform incurs for a slice of its user base that may represent a relatively small fraction of total capital raised.

**The practical result, common across many (though not universally all) US real estate crowdfunding platforms**, is a straightforward "US persons only" restriction at sign-up, verified through the same KYC (know your customer) process used for opening any financial account. This isn't a judgment about foreign investors' creditworthiness or sophistication - it's a cost-benefit decision platforms make about whether the additional compliance burden of serving foreign investors is worth it.

---

## Where the Access Picture Is More Mixed

**Not every crowdfunding-adjacent real estate vehicle applies the same blanket restriction.** Some platforms offer certain fund structures registered under different SEC rules (a Regulation A+ offering, for instance) with broader eligibility than a standard Regulation D private placement, and some platforms have built specific non-US investor onboarding paths for at least some jurisdictions. This varies platform by platform and can change over time as platforms adjust their compliance approach - checking a specific platform's current eligibility rules directly, rather than assuming a blanket restriction applies universally across the entire crowdfunding category, is the only reliable way to know your own situation.

**Even where a platform does accept non-US investors, additional friction is common** - a more involved verification process, restrictions on which specific offerings are open to foreign participants, and less favorable tax withholding treatment than a US resident investor would face on the same deal, since the underlying tax mechanics for foreign ownership of a private US real estate vehicle can be more complex than for a publicly traded REIT, covered generally in our [REITs vs direct ownership guide](/real-estate/reits-vs-direct-real-estate-ownership-non-us-investors/).

## Why This Makes the REIT Path More Practical for Most International Investors

**Publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs sidestep this entire access problem structurally**, since they're registered securities tradable through any standard brokerage account, foreign or domestic, without the accredited-investor or private-placement restrictions that govern crowdfunding platforms. For an international investor whose actual goal is diversified real estate exposure - rather than a specific interest in a particular development project or the higher (and less liquid, less diversified) return potential some individual crowdfunding deals target - a REIT ETF accomplishes the same underlying goal without running into an access wall in the first place.

**This isn't a claim that crowdfunding platforms offer nothing a REIT can't** - project-specific, potentially higher-return deals with different risk characteristics than a diversified REIT basket are a genuinely different product, appealing to investors specifically seeking that profile. But for the international investor whose primary access channel is a standard brokerage account, and whose goal is portfolio-level real estate diversification rather than a specific development bet, the practical access gap makes REITs the more realistically available option in the vast majority of cases.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are there any real estate crowdfunding platforms that reliably accept non-US investors?**
Some platforms do accept certain non-US investors, particularly for offerings structured under rules with broader eligibility than a standard Regulation D private placement, but this varies by platform and can change - confirm directly with any specific platform's current eligibility policy rather than assuming based on general market patterns.

**Is the "accredited investor" requirement the same as a general wealth or income threshold everywhere?**
Accredited investor status is specifically a US securities law concept with its own defined income and net worth thresholds; it doesn't automatically translate to or apply the same way for a foreign investor's own jurisdiction, which is part of why platforms find cross-border private placement compliance genuinely complex rather than a simple checkbox.

**Would investing through a US-based entity or relative solve the access problem?**
This raises its own separate set of legal, tax, and beneficial-ownership complications that are entirely outside the scope of this guide, and generally isn't a straightforward workaround - any such structure should be evaluated with a qualified legal and tax advisor rather than treated as a simple access solution.

**Does the same access restriction apply to publicly traded REIT-focused mutual funds, not just ETFs?**
Publicly traded, exchange-listed funds (ETFs and many closed-end funds) generally don't carry the same private-placement-style restrictions - the access gap discussed in this guide is specifically about privately placed crowdfunding deals, not registered, exchange-traded real estate funds broadly.

## Bottom Line

Real estate crowdfunding platforms are frequently restricted to US persons because of the private placement securities rules many of them operate under - a structural access gap that publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs simply don't have, since they're registered securities available through any standard brokerage account. For most international investors seeking real estate exposure, that access difference alone makes REITs the more realistically available path.
