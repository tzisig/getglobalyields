A Delaware Statutory Trust solves a specific, common problem for a real estate investor doing a [1031 exchange](/real-estate/1031-exchange-foreign-investors-explained/): you've sold an actively managed rental property, you're deferring the gain, but you no longer want to be the one fielding maintenance calls on the replacement property either. A DST lets you roll the proceeds into fractional, passive ownership of institutional-grade real estate instead - at a real cost, and with an accredited-investor bar most casual descriptions of the product skip over entirely.

---

## What a DST Actually Is

**A Delaware Statutory Trust is a legal entity that holds title to real estate - often a large, institutional-quality asset like a multifamily complex, industrial portfolio, or net-lease retail property - and sells fractional beneficial interests in that trust to individual investors.** Each investor owns a proportional interest in the trust, not a direct deed to the property, and a professional sponsor handles all property management, leasing, and operations. Because the IRS treats a DST interest as direct property ownership for tax purposes under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, it qualifies as valid replacement property in a 1031 exchange - the mechanism that makes DSTs relevant to an exchange investor in the first place, rather than just another passive real estate fund.

---

## The Real Cost of Entry

**Typical minimum investments run $25,000-$100,000** per DST offering, low enough that an investor can spread a single exchange's proceeds across several different DSTs for diversification, something that's rarely practical with a single directly-owned replacement property. **Most DST offerings are structured as Regulation D private placements, which generally means accredited-investor status is required** - net worth exceeding $1 million excluding your primary residence, or income exceeding $200,000 individually (or $300,000 jointly) in each of the two preceding years. Some sponsors structure offerings to also accept a limited number of non-accredited "sophisticated" investors under a separate SEC rule, but this is the exception, not the norm - most DST product is genuinely restricted to accredited investors.

**Fee loads are a real cost that's easy to underestimate from marketing materials alone.** Between selling commissions, organizational and offering costs, and acquisition fees charged upfront, plus ongoing asset-management fees, total fee load commonly runs in the range of 5-10% of the investment over the life of the deal - lower-load or "no-load" DST products exist from some sponsors as an alternative, but aren't the default structure, and are worth specifically asking about if fee drag is your primary concern.

---

## The Non-US Investor Eligibility Question

**This is the part where honesty matters more than a confident-sounding answer: whether a non-US investor can actually invest in a specific DST is genuinely not a single yes-or-no question, and no general source answers it cleanly.** Reg D offerings can be structured with different terms by different sponsors, and some may apply additional restrictions - residency requirements, cross-border securities compliance, or outright exclusion of non-US persons - that vary deal by deal rather than following one industry-wide rule.

**The practical implication: don't assume a DST is available to you as a foreign investor simply because it's a valid 1031 replacement property in principle.** Confirm directly with the specific sponsor of any offering you're considering, and ideally with securities counsel experienced in cross-border transactions, before counting on a DST as your exchange's fallback plan. This is a meaningfully different situation from buying a REIT ETF on the open market, which carries none of this deal-by-deal eligibility uncertainty - a distinction worth keeping in mind if you're weighing a DST against the more straightforwardly accessible REIT options covered in our [best REIT ETFs guide](/real-estate/best-us-reit-etfs-international-investors/).

## DST vs a Directly-Owned Replacement Property

**The core tradeoff is passivity versus control.** A directly-owned replacement property gives you full decision-making authority - refinancing, renovating, choosing tenants, selling on your own timeline - but requires active management or the cost of hiring it out. A DST removes all of that decision-making from your hands entirely: sponsors control major decisions, and investors typically can't add debt, make capital improvements beyond what's budgeted, or force a sale ahead of the trust's planned disposition timeline. **Liquidity is also meaningfully worse than direct ownership** - DST interests are illiquid private-placement securities with no established secondary market, generally held until the sponsor executes a planned disposition, often years out, rather than something you can sell on short notice if your circumstances change.

---

## DST vs Just Holding a REIT ETF

**A REIT ETF and a DST solve genuinely different problems, even though both offer passive real estate exposure.** A REIT ETF is liquid, tradeable daily, requires no accreditation, and involves no 1031-specific mechanics at all - but selling REIT ETF shares to realize a gain doesn't defer that gain the way rolling 1031 proceeds into a DST does. **A DST exists specifically to preserve 1031 tax deferral for an investor exiting a directly-owned property** - if you're not doing a 1031 exchange to begin with, a REIT ETF is very likely the simpler, more liquid, and more broadly accessible way to get passive real estate exposure, DST accreditation and eligibility questions aside entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I split a single 1031 exchange's proceeds across multiple DSTs?**
Yes - this is one of the more common reasons investors choose DSTs specifically, since a $25,000-$100,000 minimum per offering makes it practical to diversify a single exchange's proceeds across several different DST sponsors and property types, something rarely achievable with one directly-owned replacement property.

**What happens to my investment when the DST's sponsor eventually sells the underlying property?**
You receive your proportional share of the sale proceeds, generally including any gain - at which point you can complete another 1031 exchange into a new replacement property (including another DST) to continue deferring the tax, or accept the taxable event if you're ready to realize the gain at that point.

**Is a DST the same thing as a Tenants-in-Common (TIC) structure, another 1031-eligible passive option?**
No - TICs also qualify as 1031 replacement property but involve direct co-ownership with the practical complications of needing unanimous or majority consent among co-owners for major decisions, which DSTs avoid by centralizing decision-making with the sponsor. The tradeoff is that DST investors have even less individual control than TIC co-owners typically retain.

**If I'm not accredited, are there any legitimate ways to access DST-style real estate exposure?**
A limited number of DST sponsors extend a portion of an offering to non-accredited "sophisticated" investors under specific SEC provisions, but this isn't the norm. For most non-accredited investors, a liquid REIT ETF remains the more realistically accessible path to passive real estate exposure, even without the 1031 deferral mechanic.

## Practical Checklist

- [ ] Confirm you meet accredited-investor status before evaluating specific DST offerings, since most require it
- [ ] Ask each sponsor directly about total fee load - selling commissions, offering costs, acquisition fees, and ongoing management fees - rather than relying on marketing summaries
- [ ] If you're a non-US investor, confirm eligibility directly with the specific DST sponsor and consider securities counsel before relying on a DST as your exchange's replacement property plan
- [ ] Understand that DST interests are illiquid, sponsor-controlled, and typically held until a planned disposition years out - not a short-notice-liquidity vehicle
- [ ] If you're not doing a 1031 exchange in the first place, compare against a liquid REIT ETF before assuming a DST is the right passive real estate vehicle

---

## Bottom Line

A DST genuinely solves the "I want out of active property management but still need valid 1031 replacement property" problem, at real minimums most investors can meet and with real diversification benefits a single directly-owned property can't offer. But it comes with a meaningful fee load, an accredited-investor bar, illiquidity most buyers underestimate going in, and - for a non-US investor specifically - a genuine, deal-by-deal eligibility question that no general guide, this one included, can answer for you in advance. Confirm directly with the sponsor before treating a DST as your exchange's fallback plan.
