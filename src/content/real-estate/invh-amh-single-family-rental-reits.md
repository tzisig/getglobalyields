Invitation Homes (INVH) and American Homes 4 Rent (AMH) own tens of thousands of individual houses between them, renting single-family homes the way an apartment REIT rents units - except each unit is a standalone house in a different neighborhood, with its own maintenance needs and its own local market dynamics. Both companies just ran into a genuinely new development in 2026: federal legislation that changes how large institutional landlords are allowed to grow, and it's pushing the whole sector in a specific direction.

---

## The Business Model: Scattered-Site Rental at Scale

**Both companies buy or build single-family houses, then rent them out and manage them professionally** - centralized leasing, maintenance, and property management applied to a portfolio spread across many individual addresses rather than one apartment complex. Invitation Homes yields around 4.61% as of mid-2026, with a quarterly dividend of $0.30 per share and eight consecutive years of dividend increases. American Homes 4 Rent yields somewhat lower, around 3.97%, with a quarterly dividend of $0.33 and six consecutive years of increases - both real, if shorter, track records than the multi-decade streaks covered in our [net lease REIT guide](/real-estate/net-lease-reits-realty-income-explained/), reflecting how much younger this REIT subsector is relative to older commercial real estate categories.

**Occupancy is the key operating metric to watch, and it's softened somewhat recently.** Invitation Homes' same-store average occupancy moderated from 97.2% to 96.3% year over year, with July 2026 trending around 96.6% - still a high absolute occupancy rate, but a real, measurable step down from the near-full-capacity levels the sector ran at previously.

---

## Scale and Geography: More Concentrated Than "National Landlord" Implies

**Invitation Homes owns roughly 84,000 to 86,000 homes outright, generating around $2.4 billion in annualized net operating income, with a broader managed platform covering closer to 110,000 homes once third-party-managed properties are included.** Despite operating across 16-17 core markets, the portfolio is geographically concentrated rather than evenly spread: roughly 38% of homes sit in Western US markets, about 32% in Florida, and another 19% across other Southeastern markets, with named core markets including Atlanta, Phoenix, and Dallas - areas chosen specifically for strong job growth and inbound migration supporting a deep renter pool. That concentration means Invitation Homes' results lean heavily on Sun Belt and Southeast housing and rental market conditions specifically, not a diversified national average.

**American Homes 4 Rent runs a similar scattered-site model at a comparable scale, though with less granular portfolio detail publicly emphasized** - the company also holds partial interests in thousands of additional houses through joint ventures alongside its wholly-owned portfolio, a structure that adds some complexity to comparing the two companies' total scale on an apples-to-apples basis.

---

## The Institutional Ownership Story Is Smaller Than the Headlines Suggest

**This is worth stating clearly, because the popular narrative and the actual data diverge in a real way.** Nationally, institutional investors own only about 0.35% of the total US housing stock, and roughly 3.0% of single-family *rental* homes specifically - a small fraction of the market, not the sweeping "Wall Street buying up all the starter homes" picture often assumed. Large investors (350+ homes) specifically own around 5% of single-family rentals, per 2026 research from John Burns Research & Consulting.

**The regional picture looks very different from the national one, though, and both are true at once.** Institutional investors hold an estimated 25% of single-family rental homes in the Atlanta metro area specifically - a genuinely large local concentration even while the national figure stays small. And a separate, flow-based statistic tells a different part of the story: Redfin found investors purchased 19% of single-family homes *sold* in the first quarter of 2026 - a transaction-share figure, not a stock-ownership figure, but a real signal of how active institutional buying has been in the current market even if their cumulative ownership share remains modest.

---

## A Genuinely New Law Is Redirecting Both Companies

**The most important, current development for this sector: the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act now bars investors who own more than 350 single-family homes from purchasing additional properties out of the existing housing stock.** Both Invitation Homes and American Homes 4 Rent own well over that threshold, meaning this law directly restricts how they've historically grown - by acquiring existing houses.

**There's a specific, deliberate carve-out that changes where growth capital goes instead: purpose-built build-to-rent (BTR) communities are explicitly exempt from the restriction.** A large investor can't keep buying up existing single-family homes past the 350-home threshold, but building new, purpose-designed rental communities from the ground up remains unrestricted. This is accelerating a shift already underway - build-to-rent has drawn more than $50 billion in institutional capital since 2020 and is increasingly treated as its own institutional asset class, not a niche experiment - and the new law gives large operators a direct regulatory incentive to lean further into building new supply rather than competing with individual homebuyers for existing houses.

**For INVH and AMH specifically, this matters for how to read their forward growth story.** A portfolio that grew historically through acquiring existing homes now has a real, legally-driven reason to shift toward development pipelines and purpose-built rental communities instead - a different capital allocation pattern, with different construction and lease-up risk, than simply buying an already-occupied house in an established neighborhood.

---

## What This Means for the Investment Case

**The occupancy softening and the acquisition restriction point in a similar direction: the easy-growth phase of buying existing homes at scale is behind both companies, replaced by a harder, more capital-intensive build-to-rent growth path.** That doesn't make either company a bad investment - build-to-rent genuinely adds new housing supply rather than just changing who owns existing supply, which is a real, policy-favored distinction - but it does mean projecting INVH and AMH's growth by extrapolating their historical acquisition-driven expansion is no longer a reasonable baseline going forward.

## Bottom Line

Invitation Homes and American Homes 4 Rent both rent out single-family houses at real institutional scale, with Invitation Homes offering a higher current yield and a longer dividend-increase streak, and both facing the same real, new constraint: a 2026 federal law that blocks large investors from buying more existing homes while explicitly exempting build-to-rent development. Institutional single-family ownership remains a small share of the national housing stock despite the popular narrative - but that's now less relevant than where the new legal incentive is pushing large operators like these two, which is toward building new rental communities rather than continuing to acquire existing houses.
