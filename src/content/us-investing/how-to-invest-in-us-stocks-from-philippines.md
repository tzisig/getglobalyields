The single most consequential fact in this guide is not the US treaty rate - it is whether you are a **Philippine resident** or an **OFW (Overseas Filipino Worker)**, legally classified as a non-resident citizen. That status determines whether the Philippines taxes your US investment income at all, and the answer for OFWs is more favorable than most guides make clear.

This guide covers both tracks, the 25% treaty rate that applies either way, and which brokers work.

Everything here is verified from official sources for 2026.

## Meet Ana

Ana is 32, has worked as a nurse in the UAE for six years, and is legally classified as an OFW - a non-resident citizen of the Philippines for tax purposes. She wants to start investing her savings in US stocks and is not sure whether the Philippines will tax those gains on top of whatever the US withholds. We will follow her case alongside a second, shorter scenario for a Philippine-resident investor, since the two outcomes are genuinely different.

---

## Step 1: What You Can Actually Buy

The Philippines is not in the EU or EEA, so the PRIIPs restriction that blocks direct US-domiciled ETF access for European residents does not apply here. **Ana can buy VOO, VTI, and QQQ directly** through a broker offering US market access.

---

## Step 2: The Treaty - 25% on Dividends, Same for Everyone

The US-Philippines tax treaty dates to 1976 and remains in force - despite some outdated guides claiming otherwise. It caps US withholding on dividends paid to individual Philippine investors at **25%**, one of the higher individual rates among treaty countries, claimed with Form [W-8BEN](/taxes/w8ben-form-non-us-investors/).

| Income Type | Default US Rate | Treaty Rate (Individual) |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 30% | **25%** |
| Capital gains (securities) | Generally not US-taxable for non-residents | Residence country only |

This rate is identical whether Ana is classified as an OFW or a Philippine resident - the treaty does not distinguish. What differs enormously is what happens after the US withholding, on the Philippine side.

## Step 3: OFW vs. Resident - Two Very Different Philippine Outcomes

**If you're an OFW** - legally a non-resident citizen - the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) generally taxes you only on Philippine-source income. Gains from selling shares of a non-Philippine company are considered foreign-source income and are generally not subject to Philippine income tax for a non-resident citizen. By the same logic, US dividend income typically falls outside Philippine tax scope too. **In Ana's ordinary case: 25% US withholding on dividends, 0% US tax on capital gains, and nothing further from the Philippines.**

**If you're a Philippine resident**, the BIR taxes worldwide income - including US dividends and capital gains - at progressive rates up to **35%** under the TRAIN Act. The 25% US withholding is creditable against this liability, but the Philippine side is real and can be substantial for higher earners. Domestic Philippine dividends get a flat 10% final withholding tax for residents - a materially lower, different rate that does not carry over to US dividend income.

**The line that actually matters:** Ana's favorable outcome depends on correctly maintaining non-resident-citizen (OFW) status under BIR criteria - not simply on holding an OFW-designated visa or working abroad informally. If her status is ambiguous, or she splits significant time between the Philippines and her country of work, she should confirm her actual classification with a Philippine tax professional before assuming this treatment applies.

**A worked comparison of the two outcomes on the same $2,000 dividend:** for Ana as an OFW, the entire tax cost is the $500 US withholding (25%) - nothing further. For a Philippine-resident investor with the identical $2,000 dividend, the same $500 US withholding applies first, and then the gross $2,000 also enters their Philippine progressive-rate calculation, with the $500 already paid creditable against whatever Philippine liability results - potentially leaving a meaningful additional Philippine tax bill on top, depending on the resident's overall income bracket for the year. This is precisely why residency classification, not the treaty rate itself, is the single most consequential fact in this guide.

## Step 4: The W-8BEN Form

Whether Ana is an OFW or a resident, Form W-8BEN goes to her broker and brings US withholding down from the 30% default to the treaty's 25%. It does not by itself determine her Philippine tax outcome - that depends entirely on her residency classification, described above.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Assuming OFW status is automatic.** It depends on specific BIR criteria, not just working abroad. Confirm your classification if there is any ambiguity.

**A Philippine resident assuming the 10% domestic dividend rate applies to US dividends.** It does not - US dividend income is taxed at the progressive schedule, with a credit for US withholding.

**Believing the old "no US-Philippines treaty" claim.** It is wrong. The 1976 treaty is in force and sets the 25% individual rate.

**Skipping W-8BEN because "OFWs don't owe Philippine tax anyway."** The form still matters - it is what brings your US withholding down from 30% to 25% regardless of Philippine tax status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**As an OFW, do I owe Philippine tax on my US stock investments?**
Generally no, provided your non-resident-citizen status is correctly maintained under BIR criteria. Gains from non-Philippine shares are treated as foreign-source income and are generally outside Philippine income tax scope for a non-resident citizen, and by the same logic, US dividend income typically falls outside it too.

**What's the US withholding rate on my dividends?**
25%, under the US-Philippines tax treaty (1976), once Form W-8BEN is filed with your broker - the same rate for both residents and OFWs. Without the form, the default 30% applies.

**Is there really a US-Philippines tax treaty?**
Yes. It has been in force since 1976. Claims that no treaty exists are outdated or incorrect.

**I'm a Philippine resident, not an OFW - how does my tax situation differ?**
You are taxed on worldwide income, including US dividends and capital gains, at progressive rates up to 35% under the TRAIN Act. The 25% US withholding is creditable against this, but Philippine tax still applies on top.

**Which brokers accept Filipino investors and OFWs?**
Interactive Brokers and eToro are both confirmed to accept Philippine residents and citizens for account opening, based on standard residency/citizenship documentation.
